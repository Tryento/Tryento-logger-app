# Black soldier fly (Hermetia illucens) larval rearing operations: nursery, trays, substrates, moisture, harvest, fasting and benchmarks

Scope note: these notes cover the larval stage only, from neonate/nursery to fasting and separation. Tryento context: a tropical lowland farm in Venezuela, rooms not necessarily climate-controlled. Each tray is seeded with weighed eggs (g) plus a weighed starter ("iniciador": bagazo, afrecho, yogurt or other), then fed repeatedly (kg per feeding), fasted (default 24 h), weighed before and after the fast, and separated into "clean larvae" (g). Unless a line says otherwise, "wet" means as-fed or fresh weight and "DM" means dry matter.

Recurring abbreviations: 5-DOL = five-day-old larvae; BCR = bioconversion rate; FCR = feed conversion ratio; WRI = waste reduction index; ECI = efficiency of conversion of ingested feed; NFC/NCC = non-fibre (non-cellulose) carbohydrates; BSG = brewer's spent grain.

## 1. Nursery stage: neonate diet, moisture, duration, density, and direct egg seeding versus a separate nursery

### Takeaway
The best-documented SOP, the Eawag manual, hatches eggs over a moist chicken-feed mash (30% dry feed and 70% water, covered with 0.5–1 cm of coco peat). Larvae stay there for about 5 days, are sieved at 1 mm, counted by weighing a subsample, and dosed into grow-out containers by count. A 2026 study found chicken-feed-nursed neonates heavier early and better at surviving than neonates nursed on food waste. I found no peer-reviewed trial that directly compares hatching eggs onto the grow-out tray with using a separate nursery.

### Cited Findings
- **Eawag/Dortmans et al. (2017), hatchling (nursery) container.**
  - Harvested egg clutches ("eggies") are placed on a grate called a "hatchling shower" over a 60 × 40 × 12 cm hatchling container. Neonates drop into the container.
  - The container is changed on a schedule and colour-coded by day, so that each container holds one daily cohort.
  - Feed: 3 kg per container of "30% dry chicken (broiler) feed and 70% water", covered with "dry and sieved coco peat (0.5–1.0 cm thickness) to avoid loss of moisture". That is 900 g of feed on a dry-feed basis per 60 × 40 cm container.
  - The larvae "remain and feed for five days on nutritious feed (chicken feed) to become 5-DOL".
  - Source: [Dortmans et al. 2017, Eawag "Black Soldier Fly Biowaste Processing"](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)
- **Eawag, separating and counting 5-DOL.**
  - The six-day-old hatchling containers are sieved with a 1 mm manual sieve.
  - Two random 2 g samples of the purified 5-DOL mix are hand-counted with tweezers and a click counter.
  - The total 5-DOL mass is weighed, and the mass needed per larvero is calculated as M_larvero = L_larvero × M_total / L_total.
  - Planning rule: "600–800 larvae per kg of wet waste fed during the whole treatment period".
  - Source: [Eawag 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)
- **Eawag, colony share.** 2–5% of 5-DOL are kept back to regenerate the colony. They go to 55 × 35 × 16 cm nursery containers on 2 kg of 30% chicken feed and 70% water, and are fed for about 2.5 weeks until they become prepupae. — [Eawag 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)
- **Cerckel et al. (2026), Frontiers in Microbiology: nursery diet effects.**
  - Design: nursery from 0 to 7 days after egg harvest (DAH) on chicken feed (CF) or artificial supermarket food waste (SFW) at 27 °C and 60% RH. Substrate was given at DAH 0 and DAH 4. 170 larvae were transferred to fattening at DAH 7, and fattening ran DAH 8–15.
  - Final weight and survival by route (nursery → fattening):

    | Route | Final weight (g) | Survival |
    |---|---|---|
    | CF → CF | 0.205 | 92.9% |
    | SFW → CF | 0.231 | 83.8% |
    | CF → SFW | 0.149 | 86.8% |
    | SFW → SFW | 0.156 | 75.1% |

  - CF-nursed larvae were heavier at DAH 8–10.
  - Larvae adapted faster when the fattening diet matched the nursery diet.
  - The CF nursery substrate had a higher water-holding capacity, while the SFW substrate had free water.
  - Source: [Cerckel et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13534125/)
- **Loiotine et al. (2024), handling.** Handling 6-day-old larvae (sampling, washing, drying and weighing about 30 larvae, either every 4 days or once) did not significantly change:
  - larval duration (8.2 d)
  - final weight (150–160 mg)
  - survival (94.5–96.2%)
  - bioconversion efficiency (12.9–14.3%)
  - WRI (7.3%/day)

  Source: [Loiotine et al. 2024, Frontiers in Veterinary Science](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/veterinary-science/articles/10.3389/fvets.2024.1330342/full)
- **Welfare Part II study (2025).** Larvae were started in grow-out trials at 6 days old (27 ± 1 °C, 65 ± 5% RH) and grown for 7–12 days. — [Insects 2025, 16(1):5 (Part II)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11765738/)
- **Commercial egg-to-larva yield claims (unverified).** A seller claims "1 gram of BSF eggs will yield 20,000–35,000 BSF larvae". Another figure in the same search results was 39,000–42,000 larvae per g of eggs. Both are commercial or grey-literature numbers. — [Kimmy Farm (commercial)](https://kimmyfarm.com/en/how-many-grams-of-bsf-larvea-after-growing)

### Inferences
- **Nursery versus direct seeding.** A separate nursery (about 4–7 days on a moist, nutrient-dense mash) has three advantages:
  1. Grow-out trays can be dosed by larval count rather than by egg mass. This removes the contested 37,000–77,000 eggs/g uncertainty and variable hatch rates from the tray KPI.
  2. Neonates are protected from wet, acidic or fermenting grow-out substrates (yogurt, fresh bagazo).
  3. Survival and weight are more uniform (Cerckel 2026).

  Direct seeding of eggs plus a small starter on the tray (Tryento's current method) is simpler. However, the "g larvae per g eggs" KPI then combines hatch rate, neonate survival and grow-out performance.
- **Tryento's "iniciador" is effectively an in-tray nursery.**
  - Afrecho (wheat bran) moistened to about 70% water behaves most like the Eawag chicken-feed mash: dry, nutrient-dense and able to hold water.
  - Yogurt alone as a starter is liquid and acidic, so neonates risk drowning. This is an inference; no direct study was found.
  - Bagazo (BSG) is fibrous. Chia et al. 2018 (Section 4) found unsupplemented spent grain gave lighter larvae than yeast-supplemented grain.
- **Suggested SOP if a nursery is adopted.** Moist starter at about 70% water, a thin coco-peat or dry-bran cover against drying, 5 (4–7) days at about 27–30 °C, a 1 mm sieve, and a 2 g subsample count before dosing trays. This is adapted directly from Eawag 2017.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed head-to-head comparison of eggs hatched directly onto the grow-out substrate versus a separate nursery was found.
- No peer-reviewed nursery stocking density (neonates per cm² or grams of eggs per container) was found. Eawag gives the container size and feed mass but not a number of eggs per hatchling container.
- No peer-reviewed hatch-rate figure (%) was gathered in this session. Other researchers' notes (01/03) may cover it.

## 2. Tray rearing: tray size and depth, larval density, and effects of density on growth, mortality and heat

### Takeaway
The main practical benchmark (Eawag) is about 4 larvae/cm²: 10,000 5-DOL per 40 × 60 × 17 cm crate, 40,000 per m², with the substrate layer kept at 5 cm or less. Controlled studies put the optimum at about 5 larvae/cm² (Diener, Parra Paz), or 5–7.6 larvae/cm² depending on diet.

Larval metabolism heats the substrate core. Li et al. 2023 measured at least 10 °C above air temperature at mass-rearing densities (up to 10,000 larvae per pan). In warm air (30 °C), smaller populations grew better, which matters for Tryento's non-climate-controlled tropical rooms.

### Cited Findings
- **Eawag rule of thumb.**
  - "10,000 5-DOL in a larvero (40x60x17cm) feeding on 15kg of wet waste (75% water) for 12 days", and "40,000 5-DOL per 1m2 treatment area, which are fed 60kg of biowaste over a period of 12 days".
  - "If the depth of the waste in the larvero is more than 5cm, larvae will have difficulties to process it entirely and the waste on the bottom will remain unprocessed."
  - Stacked larveros need open ventilation frames between levels for air exchange and oxygen.

  Source: [Eawag 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)
- **Too much waste per tray.** "If too much waste is used, an unprocessed waste layer can build up heat through bacterial activity… The untouched feed will also attract other filth flies. Providing not enough waste will starve the larvae." — [Eawag 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)
- **Welfare Part II (2025).**
  - Tested 5, 10 and 15 larvae/cm² at 27 °C. Optimum was 5 larvae/cm² on an "omnivorous" diet and 7.57 larvae/cm² on a control diet.
  - Final weight was 108 mg at 5 larvae/cm² and 98 mg at 15 larvae/cm².
  - Survival was 96.5–98.5% with no density effect.
  - The paper cites Diener et al. and Parra Paz et al. as recommending 5 larvae/cm², and Abduh et al. as reporting lower productivity at 1–1.4 larvae/cm².

  Source: [Insects 2025 Part II](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11765738/)
- **Barragán-Fonseca et al. (2018, Colombian first author).**
  - Tested 0.31–2.47 larvae/cm² in 15.5 × 10.5 cm boxes at 27 °C, with a fixed 0.6 g DM per larva.
  - Development to first prepupa took 13–27 days depending on density and diet. It was fastest (13 d) on the most concentrated diet at the lowest density.
  - Larval crude fat was "strongly affected by nutrient concentration and by larval density", while protein stayed within narrow limits.

  Source: [Barragán-Fonseca et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6221057/)
- **Li et al. (2023), Insect Science, heat.**
  - Tested 0 to 10,000 larvae per pan at 20 and 30 °C air temperature.
  - "Larval activity increased substrate temperatures significantly (i.e., at least 10 °C above air temperatures). Low air temperature favored growth with the higher population sizes while high temperature favored growth with low population sizes."
  - Best individual weights were 0.126 and 0.124 g, with FCR 1.92–2.08 g/g, for 10,000 larvae at 20 °C or 100 larvae at 30 °C. The FCR basis is not stated in the abstract.
  - Moving larvae from 30 to 20 °C at about 10 days old increased production weight by 16%.

  Source: [Li et al. 2023](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/1744-7917.13198)
- **Klammsteiner et al. (2025), iScience.**
  - Tested 0, 1.25, 2.5 and 5 larvae/cm² on canteen food waste at 27.5 °C ambient in 9.5 cm boxes.
  - Each doubling of density raised peak substrate temperature by 0.6–2.4 °C. Peaks were about 31 °C at 2.5 larvae/cm² and about 32.3 °C at 5 larvae/cm², around days 16–19 after hatching. These small boxes do not reproduce the large-mass heating seen by Li et al.
  - Substrate pH rose with density: 5.7–6.6 at 1.25 larvae/cm² versus 7.8–7.9 at 5 larvae/cm². Higher density also kept the substrate wetter.
  - The study quotes an upper thermal limit of about 44 °C and says "constant exposure to environmental temperatures of 36°C–37°C already result in negative effects". The authors urge logging substrate temperature.

  Source: [Klammsteiner et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12205609/)
- **Parra Paz et al. (2015, Colombian authors; affiliation not verified in this session).** Density and feeding-rate trial on vegetable waste. The Part II review cites it as giving an optimum of 95–163 mg feed per larva per day and 5 larvae/cm². — [Parra Paz et al. 2015, Waste and Biomass Valorization](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12649-015-9418-8), as summarised in [Insects 2025 Part II](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11765738/)

### Inferences
- **Tray sizing for Tryento.** A 40 × 60 cm tray (2,400 cm²) at 4–5 larvae/cm² holds about 10,000–12,000 larvae. With eggs at 37,000–77,000/g, an assumed 70–80% hatch and about 90% neonate survival, that needs roughly 0.2–0.45 g of eggs per tray. The hatch and survival figures are assumptions, not sourced values; calibrate them against Tryento's own counts.
- **Density in a warm room.** At a room temperature of 28–32 °C, the Li 2023 result (warm air favours smaller populations) and the 36–37 °C chronic-harm threshold point the same way. Stay at or below about 4–5 larvae/cm², keep layers shallow (at most 5 cm), leave air gaps between stacked trays, and measure core temperature daily.
- **Tray overheating indicators.** A core temperature above about 40 °C is an alarm. Larvae massing at the surface or climbing walls are behavioural signs. The 40 °C threshold is inferred from the 44 °C limit and the 36–37 °C chronic threshold.

### Gaps
- There is no peer-reviewed conversion of "kg of eggs per m²" for grow-out trays. It depends on eggs/g (contested) and hatch rate.
- No tropical-ambient (≥30 °C, uncontrolled) density trial with substrate-temperature logging at farm scale was found.

## 3. Feeding: rate per larva per day, batch versus incremental feeding, frequency, and the weight versus waste-reduction trade-off

### Takeaway
The anchor study is Diener et al. 2009: 100 mg per larva per day of chicken feed at 60% moisture, which is about 40 mg DM per larva per day. It gave the best trade-off between waste reduction (41.8%) and prepupal weight (48 mg DM). Later work places optima at about 90–175 mg wet feed per larva per day depending on diet.

Eawag's waste-processing regime is much leaner: about 31 mg DM per larva per day, in 3 equal feedings on days 1, 5 and 8. It favours waste reduction over larval size. Batch (all-at-once) feeding shortens development, while daily feeding gives heavier larvae but takes longer.

### Cited Findings
- **Diener et al. (2009).**
  - "A daily feeding rate of 100 mg chicken feed (60% moisture content) per larva resulted in an optimum trade-off between material reduction efficiency (41.8%, SE 0.61) and biomass production (prepupal dry weight: 48.0 mg, SE 2.0)."
  - This equals a feeding capacity of 3–5 kg/m²/day of market waste and 6.5 kg/m²/day of human faeces, and could yield 145 g DM of prepupae per m² per day.
  - Chitin-corrected crude protein of prepupae was 28.2–42.5% depending on feed amount.

  Source: [Diener et al. 2009, Waste Management & Research](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0734242X09103838)
- **Welfare Part II (2025).**
  - Modelled optimum: about 90 mg feed per larva per day on the omnivorous diet and about 175 mg per larva per day on the control diet. Achieved rates were 60–134 mg per larva per day.
  - Final weight was 59.8 mg at 50 mg per larva per day versus 160.1 mg at 200 mg per larva per day.
  - FCR was 7.50 at 50 mg (the most efficient), 5.25 at the omnivorous optimum and 9.59 at the control-diet optimum. The basis is not stated; it looks wet or as-fed.
  - Substrate reduction was 79.6–85.5%.
  - Literature range cited: 25–500 mg substrate per larva per day. Nyakeri et al. found an optimum of 200 mg per larva per day and Abduh et al. 100 mg per larva per day.

  Source: [Insects 2025 Part II](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11765738/)
- **Eawag regime.**
  - 10,000 larvae and 15 kg wet waste (75% water) over 12 days, in three equal feedings on day 1, day 5 and day 8.
  - In practice, 5 kg per 40 × 60 cm larvero per feeding.
  - Harvest after 12 days of feeding, on day 13.

  Source: [Eawag 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)
- **Meneguz, Gasco & Tomberlin (2018), batch versus daily feeding.**
  - The daily feeding system gave "the heaviest final larval weight (0.149 g), but required a longer time (11.3 d) than [batch feeding] to reach the prepupa stadium".
  - Feeding system affected final larval weight, ingested feed, larval mortality and time to pupa.

  Source: [Meneguz et al. 2018, PLoS ONE](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0202591)
- **Barragán-Fonseca et al. (2018).** Feeding "3× weekly by visual estimation" left larvae food-limited. A fixed 0.6 g DM per larva given up front avoided this. — [Barragán-Fonseca et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6221057/)
- **Reactor comparison (search-result summary; paper not read in full).** A food-waste reactor run in single-batch mode for 10 days reduced wet weight by 38.9% and gave a larval yield of 80.9 g per kg of waste. Single-batch and multi-batch operation both treated waste effectively. — [Frontiers of Environmental Science & Engineering 2025, continuous self-separation reactor](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11783-025-1963-1)

### Inferences
- **Eawag's feeding rate in dry matter.** 15 kg × 25% DM = 3.75 kg DM. Divided over 10,000 larvae and 12 days, that is about 31 mg DM (125 mg wet) per larva per day.
- **Diener's rate in dry matter.** 100 mg wet at 60% moisture is 40 mg DM per larva per day.
- **Practical target band for Tryento.** About 30–50 mg DM per larva per day. Convert to kg of each feed using its DM%:

  | Feed (DM, approx.) | kg as-fed per 10,000 larvae per day |
  |---|---|
  | Afrecho (~88–90% DM) | ~0.35–0.55 |
  | Bagazo BSG (~16–25% DM) | ~1.2–3 |
  | Yogurt (~12–15% DM, assumed) | ~2–4 |

  The DM figures for afrecho and yogurt are typical values that were not sourced in this session. BSG at 15.8% DM is from Bava 2019. Measure DM on site with an oven at 105 °C for 24 h, the Eawag method.
- **Choosing a rate.** Low rates (about 25–50 mg wet per larva per day) maximise waste reduction and FCR efficiency but give small larvae (about 60 mg). High rates (about 150–200 mg wet per larva per day) give heavy larvae (about 150–200 mg), leave more unconsumed residue and worsen FCR.
- **Choosing a feeding pattern.** For a larvae-as-product farm, incremental feeding (e.g., days 1, 5, 8, or every 2–3 days) keeps the layer shallow and limits heating and anaerobic pockets. Batch feeding shortens the cycle.

### Gaps
- No study compares specific feeding frequencies (daily versus every 2–3 days versus 3 feedings) under tropical ambient conditions.
- Feeding-rate optima for BSG, wheat bran or yogurt specifically (mg DM per larva per day) were not found.

## 4. Substrates: bagazo, afrecho, yogurt/dairy, chicken feed, fruit and vegetables, manures, mixes; nutrient targets, fibre, pH, fermentation; Latin American studies

### Takeaway
**Nutrient targets.** Performance tracks protein and digestible, non-fibre carbohydrate. Formulate towards:
- about 14–19% crude protein and 13–15% or more NFC on a DM basis, with P:NFC around 1:1 to 1:2 (Gold et al. 2020);
- a summed protein plus carbohydrate content of about 40–47% of DM at P:C 1:2 (Barragán-Fonseca et al. 2018).

**Substrate by substrate.**
- **Brewer's spent grain.** Supports high-protein larvae but is fibrous (NDF about 54% DM). Alone, it slows development (22 days versus 15 on a hen diet in one study). It improves with brewer's yeast or a richer co-substrate.
- **Sugarcane bagasse.** Lignocellulosic and very poor alone. It needs pretreatment or co-substrates.
- **Dairy/whey.** Mexican and Algerian studies found cheese whey supported or even increased growth when mixed with a co-substrate. No yogurt-specific study was found.
- **pH.** Acidic starting pH (as low as 4) only slowed early growth, because larvae drive substrate pH to about 9.
- **Lactic-acid fermentation.** Tends to help modestly and cut ammonia.

### Cited Findings
**Terminology: "bagazo" and "afrecho"**
- In Latin American animal-feed literature, brewer's spent grain is called "bagazo de cerveza", "bagazo de cebada" or "bagazo (húmedo) de cervecería". It is delivered at 75–80% moisture and spoils quickly: use within 2–5 days in warm climates. — [produccion-animal.com.ar, Bagazo de cebada](https://www.produccion-animal.com.ar/tablas_composicion_alimentos/161-Bagazo_cebada.pdf); [Nutrinews, Bagazo de cerveza](https://nutrinews.com/materias-primas-bagazo-de-cerveza/); [SciELO México, bagazo húmedo de cervecería in sheep](https://www.scielo.org.mx/scielo.php?script=sci_arttext&pid=S2448-61322017000300021); [Contexto Ganadero (Colombia)](https://www.contextoganadero.com/ganaderia-sostenible/bagazo-de-cebada-una-alternativa-para-alimentar-al-ganado)
- "Afrecho" is also used regionally for brewer's grains ("afrecho de cebada", in a Latin American dairy forum), not only for wheat bran. — [Engormix forum](https://www.engormix.com/lecheria/miscellaneous/afrecho-cebada-como-conservarlo_f11459/)
- The BSG search results also included a thread on large-scale conservation of barley bagazo in Venezuela, so BSG is locally available as a feed. — [search results including Lpernia/Engormix](https://www.lpernia.com/2023/03/21/el-bagazo-o-cebadilla-de-cerveza-en-rumiantes/)

**Nutrient targets**
- **Gold et al. (2020), Waste Management.** Biowaste formulations had "a protein-to-NFC ratio of 1:1, a protein content of 14–19%, and a NFC content of 13–15% (dry mass)". Against poultry feed as the benchmark:
  - vegetable canteen waste performed best and cow manure worst;
  - formulations improved performance and reduced variability compared with single wastes;
  - remaining variability correlated with fibre and lipid content.

  Source: [Gold et al. 2020](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0956053X19306658)
- **Barragán-Fonseca, Pineda-Mejia, Dicke & van Loon (2018), J. Econ. Entomol.** Vegetable by-product diets based on distiller's grains were formulated at P:C 1:2. The best was cabbage plus old bread at "47% P+C". P:C 1:2 and 1:3 performed similarly, and high protein raised larval fat. — [Barragán-Fonseca et al. 2018, JEE](https://doi.org/10.1093/jee/toy270)
- **Barragán-Fonseca et al. (2018), density and nutrient study.** At 27 °C, the most concentrated diet (14% protein, 46% NCC) gave the shortest development (13 d). The most dilute (3.5% protein, 12% NCC) was slowest. — [Barragán-Fonseca et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6221057/)
- **P:C optimum (search snippet; paper not read).** "Highest overall performance… P:C ratios between 1:2–1:3, corresponding to P:E of 11.2–14.4 g/MJ." — [JIFF 2023, "Optimal dietary protein to carbohydrate ratio"](https://dx.doi.org/10.3920/JIFF2022.0102)
- **Lalander et al. (2019), J. Cleaner Production.**
  - 8 urban waste fractions plus 2 controls were tested.
  - Waste-to-biomass conversion ratio and development time depended mainly on "content of total volatile solids and protein content"; final prepupal weight depended on volatile solids only.
  - Abattoir waste, food waste, human faeces and abattoir waste mixed with fruit and vegetables were "highly suitable". Elsewhere they are summarised as less suitable: fruit and vegetables alone, and sewage sludges.

  Source: [Lalander et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclepro.2018.10.017)
- **Gold et al. (2018) review.** Recommends formulating biowastes to a similar protein content and amino-acid balance, and "pre- and co-treatment of biowaste with beneficial microbes", to raise performance. — [Gold et al. 2018, Waste Management](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.wasman.2018.10.022)

**Brewer's spent grain (bagazo de cerveza)**
- **Bava et al. (2019), Italy.**
  - Setup: 25 °C, 1,000 two-day-old larvae per 21 × 27 cm box (about 1.8 larvae/cm²), fed ad libitum.
  - BSG composition: 15.8% DM, 15.8% crude protein, 53.6% NDF (DM basis).
  - Results, BSG versus hen diet:

    | Measure | BSG | Hen diet |
    |---|---|---|
    | Final weight | 0.98 g/10 larvae (98 mg/larva) | 229 mg/larva |
    | Development | 22 d | 15 d |
    | Survival | 95.9% | 97.5% |
    | WRI | 3.01%/d | 4.46%/d |
    | ECI | 0.25 | 0.27 |

  - BSG larvae composition: 36.5% DM; 54.1% crude protein and 23.2% ether extract on a DM basis.
  - Maize distiller's grains gave 197 mg per larva but only 73% survival. Okara gave the best ECI (0.36).

  Source: [Bava et al. 2019, Animals](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6617253/)
- **Meneguz et al. (2018), J. Sci. Food Agric.** Brewery by-products versus winery by-products: brewery larvae reached prepupa in 8.0 trial-days versus 20.2. Brewery larvae had 395.7 g/kg DM protein, and WRI was 5.3 g/d for brewery versus 2.4 for winery. The authors call brewery by-products "very promising", noting BRE availability is limited. — [Meneguz et al. 2018, JSFA](https://doi.org/10.1002/jsfa.9127)
- **Chia et al. (2018), Kenya.** Spent grain supplemented with brewer's yeast (D1) versus spent grain alone (D2):
  - final larval wet weight was higher on D1;
  - the larval thermal constant was 250 ± 25 degree-days on D1 versus 333 ± 51 on D2, i.e., about 33% more heat-time needed on plain grain.

  Source: [Chia et al. 2018, PLoS ONE](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0206097)
- **Brewery by-products and yeast (2024, Animal; search snippet).** "Increasing brewer's spent yeast inclusion improves larval bioconversion." — [Animal 2024, brewery by-products](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1751731124002192)
- **Why BSG is slow (search snippet).** The longer development on BSG is attributed to "high content of cellulose, lignocellulose and lignin". — [Brewery waste streams paper (academia.edu)](https://www.academia.edu/70900307/Brewery_s_waste_streams_as_a_valuable_substrate_for_Black_Soldier_Fly_Hermetia_illucens_Diptera_Stratiomyidae_)

**Sugarcane bagasse (bagazo de caña)**
- **Bothma et al. (2024), JIFF, South Africa.**
  - Sugarcane bagasse "requires pretreatment to improve its digestibility"; "a combination of thermal and biological pre-treatments was essential to liberate sugar monomers".
  - Microbes without enzymes were "not feasible".
  - Steam pretreatment followed by enzymatic hydrolysis and microbial biotreatment gave BCR 13.4%.

  Source: [Bothma et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1163/23524588-00001186)
- **Zandi-Sohani & Tomberlin (2024), Iran.**
  - Bagasse plus vinasse was the best sugarcane by-product mix but far below canteen leftovers: 0.64 g versus 3.15 g sample weight at 14 days. The weight unit appears to be per sample group, not per larva.
  - Bagasse plus molasses was very poor (0.06 g), because "BSFL did not grow well in substrates containing molasses", probably since sticky sugars hamper movement.
  - Pure bagasse was not tested.

  Source: [Zandi-Sohani & Tomberlin 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11508635/)
- **Microbial fermentation of sugarcane waste.** Fermenting with Bacillus subtilis, Enterococcus faecalis and Saccharomyces cerevisiae increased larval weight, survival and conversion compared with unfermented waste (abstract-level). — [PubMed 40460905](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40460905/)

**Dairy, whey and yogurt**
- **Caltzontzin-Rabell et al. (2024), Mexico (Querétaro-based authors).** "Cheese whey did not affect the growth of the larvae, on the contrary, it increased it (final weight of 0.252 ± 0.003 g)." Larvae were 42.5% lipid and 35.0% protein (DM). — [Caltzontzin-Rabell et al. 2024, Engineering Reports](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/eng2.12853)
- **Algeria, whey plus spent coffee grounds (search snippet).** Adding whey to a spent-coffee-grounds diet raised waste reduction, WRI, digestibility and bioconversion. Larval protein rose from 34.18% to 40.11% DM and fat fell from 37.87% to 36.06%. — [J. Agric. Appl. Biol. 2024](https://jaabjournal.org/index.php/jaab/article/view/371)
- **Peru (UNSCH, Ayacucho).** Thesis on producing BSF larval meal from vegetable residues plus lactosuero (whey); title and record only, no data read. — [UNSCH repository](https://repositorio.unsch.edu.pe/items/f7687d98-cf41-40ac-8637-d220bf62c96a)
- **Acidic substrates.** Initial pH of 4.0, 6.1, 7.5 or 9.5 "impacted larval weight on the first, third, and fifth day, but not at the end of the trial". Larvae raised pH to 8.9–9.4 in all treatments from day 4 onward. — [Meneguz et al. 2018, PLoS ONE](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0202591)
- **Lactic-acid bacteria.**
  - Adding fruit fermentation broth or a commercial LAB broth to kitchen waste increased growth rate by 9.96% and 7.96% after 6 days.
  - Fruit broth lowered pH, enriched Lactobacillus, and suppressed ammonifiers, reducing ammonia emission.

  Source: [Li et al. 2023, Insect Science](https://doi.org/10.1111/1744-7917.13185)
- The BSFL nursery microbiome was dominated by Lactobacillaceae (Lactobacillus). — [Cerckel et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13534125/)

**Chicken feed and other benchmarks**
- Prepupal protein was 399–431 g/kg DM across chicken feed, vegetable waste, digestate and restaurant waste. Ether extract and ash varied widely:

  | Substrate | EE (g/kg DM) | Ash (g/kg DM) |
  |---|---|---|
  | Chicken feed | 336 | 100 |
  | Vegetable waste | 371 | 96 |
  | Restaurant waste | 386 | 27 |
  | Digestate | 218 | 197 |

  Source: [Spranghers et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1002/jsfa.8081)
- Across four by-product diets, BSF converted feed more efficiently than mealworms and crickets. Diet did not affect BSF survival, and BSF composition "remained similar". — [Oonincx et al. 2015, PLoS ONE](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0144601)
- Gainesville diet (a common lab control): 30% alfalfa meal, 20% corn meal and 50% wheat bran at 70% moisture. — [Yang, Tomberlin & Jordan 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/microbiology/articles/10.3389/fmicb.2021.601253/full)
- **Manure.** On pig manure, only 12.5% of manure DM, 13% of C and 25% of N were stored in larvae. Larvae increased CO₂ (247 vs 148 g/kg DM) and NH₃-N (7 vs 4.5 g/kg DM) emissions. — [Parodi et al. 2021, Waste Management](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.wasman.2021.04.001)

**Other Latin American studies**
- **Colombia.** Parra Paz et al. 2015 (density and feeding rate on vegetable waste, above) and Barragán-Fonseca et al. 2018 (first author Colombian). — [Parra Paz 2015](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12649-015-9418-8); [Barragán-Fonseca 2018](https://doi.org/10.1093/jee/toy270)

  - Honduras: Zamorano bioconversion of different substrates. — [bdigital.zamorano.edu](https://bdigital.zamorano.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/6e8fbd98-b4e1-4a38-91c4-6ee1391855be/content)
  - Brazil: UFSC thesis on bioconversion and larval quality. — [repositorio.ufsc.br](https://repositorio.ufsc.br/bitstream/handle/123456789/245484/EDUARDO%20BUSS_2022.1.pdf?sequence=1&isAllowed=y)

### Inferences
- **Which "bagazo" Tryento means.** In Venezuela (a sugarcane-producing country), plain "bagazo" most commonly means sugarcane bagasse. In feed and brewery contexts it is qualified: "bagazo de cerveza/cebada/cervecería". Tryento should record which one it uses, because the two behave completely differently:
  - Brewer's spent grain: wet (about 75–80% water), about 16–25% crude protein on a DM basis, fibrous. A workable substrate, better when enriched.
  - Sugarcane bagasse: dry, lignocellulosic, very low protein. Essentially a bulking agent, not a feed, unless pretreated.

  Consider logging "bagazo_cerveza" and "bagazo_caña" as separate feed types, and do not assume "afrecho" is always wheat bran when buying from suppliers.
- **BSG as a single feed.** Expect slower cycles (up to about 20+ days from young larvae at 25 °C), lighter larvae (about 100 mg), and high-protein, lower-fat larvae. Mixing in afrecho (wheat bran), yeast or a sugar/starch source should shorten the cycle; this follows from the P+C targets and Chia 2018.
- **Yogurt/dairy.** Supplies protein, lactose (non-fibre carbohydrate) and lactic-acid bacteria, but it is liquid and acidic. Evidence suggests it is useful as a minority wet component mixed into a dry, absorbent carrier (afrecho or BSG). It is risky as the main or starter substrate, because free liquid can drown neonates and cause anaerobic, sour trays. Early acidity is tolerated because larvae raise the pH. No yogurt-specific trial was found, so this is an inference from whey, pH and LAB studies.
- **Formulation check with lab data.** Target roughly 15–20% crude protein and 25–45% starch/sugar on a DM basis, with low lignin, e.g., BSG plus wheat bran plus some dairy.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found on yogurt (or expired yogurt or dairy-plant waste) as a BSF substrate. Only cheese whey studies exist.
- No peer-reviewed wheat bran-only performance data were gathered. Wheat bran appears mainly as 50% of the Gainesville diet.
- No explicit C:N ratio or lignin ceiling for BSF substrates was sourced in this session.
- No Venezuelan BSF substrate study was found.
- I could not verify whether the Venezuelan colloquial term "nepe" refers to brewer's grains.

## 5. Moisture and physical conditions: substrate moisture, excess water, temperature monitoring, ammonia, CO₂ and oxygen, odour and leachate

### Takeaway
Keep the mixed substrate at about 70–75% water.
- **Too wet (80% or more):** a slurry that cannot be sieved and is anaerobic.
- **Too dry (below 45–60%):** slower growth and more microbial carbon loss.

Fix wet feeds by gravity dewatering or by blending with dry absorbent material (wheat bran, rice husk, sawdust, coco peat). In the final days, ventilate with fans so the residue dries to a crumbly state for sieving.

Monitor substrate core temperature. The larval upper limit is about 44 °C, and chronic exposure to 36–37 °C is harmful. Larvae add at least 10 °C over air temperature, so a 30 °C room can reach 40 °C or more in the core.

### Cited Findings
- **Eawag moisture limits.**
  - Feed should be 60–90% water to be treatable.
  - Above 80% (a "slurry like texture") it should be dewatered or blended to below 80%. The rearing chapter uses 85% as the dewatering trigger.
  - Below 70% (or 60% in the task sheet; test: "squeezing a handful… if less than a few drops of water emerge… too dry") add clean water.
  - A dry bulk material "(e.g. rice husk/wheat bran) could be added to reach the critical limit".
  - Passive dewatering: cloth bag in a bucket, 24 h, then another 24 h.
  - Moisture is measured by oven drying a sample of about 500 g at 105 °C for 24 h.

  Source: [Eawag 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)
- **Eawag ventilation.** "Ventilate the stacks with fans during the last few days… to increase evaporation. A crumbly waste residue will be the result, which can be easily sieved." How intense depends on air humidity and starting moisture. — [Eawag 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)
- **Cheng et al. (2017), food waste at 70/75/80% moisture.**
  - At 70% and 75%, residue separated effectively with a 2.36 mm sieve; at 80%, "sieving of the residue was not feasible".
  - Lower moisture slowed growth, a trade-off with sieving.
  - Survival was at least 95% at all three levels.

  Source: [Cheng et al. 2017, Waste Management](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.wasman.2017.05.046)
- **Bekker et al. (2021), 45–85% moisture.**
  - Larvae became prepupae only at 45–75% moisture.
  - At 45%: fastest specific growth (0.73/day), shortest growth period (13 d), lowest maximal dry weight (88 mg), and 22% of substrate carbon lost as microbial CO₂.
  - At 75%: 3% of substrate carbon lost as microbial CO₂.
  - Net growth efficiency was 0.62 at 45% and 0.52 at 75%.

  Source: [Bekker et al. 2021, Waste Management](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.wasman.2021.04.028)
- **Other moisture studies (search snippets).**
  - Best growth at 70% moisture, with survival above 95% at 60, 70 and 80%.
  - Highest individual fresh weight (158.6 mg) at 70% moisture with 250 larvae; highest biomass at 75% with 300 larvae.

  Source: [IJRIAS moisture study](https://rsisinternational.org/journals/ijrias/articles/effect-of-substrate-moisture-on-the-growth-performance-and-survivability-of-the-black-soldier-fly-hermetia-illucens-l-larva/)
- **Yakti et al. (2023), substrate structure.**
  - Fibres with higher water-holding capacity (e.g., cellulose powder at 9.45 mL/g versus straw at 3.6 mL/g) "could improve the structure and reduce mortality". Lower bulk density was associated with better growth.
  - Particles larger than 3 mm gave 42 g fresh and 15 g dry yield, versus 26 g and 8 g for particles of 1 mm or less.

  Source: [Yakti et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10054678/)
- **Eawag particle size.** Shred feed to "smaller than 1–2 cm". — [Eawag 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)
- **Temperature thresholds.**
  - Tomberlin et al. (2009): survival of 4- to 6-day-old larvae to adults was "74–97% at 27 and 30 °C but was only 0.1% at 36 °C". Development took about 4 d (11%) longer at 27 °C than at 30 °C. — [Tomberlin et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1603/022.038.0347)
  - Chia et al. (2018): thermal maxima for larvae, prepupae and pupae were 37.2–44.0 °C. The lower larval development threshold was about 10.4–12.3 °C. The most favourable population growth was at 30 °C. — [Chia et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0206097)
  - Substrate heating: see Li et al. 2023 and Klammsteiner et al. 2025 in Section 2.
- **Gas emissions.**
  - Parodi et al. (2020), on an industrial substrate: 1% of dietary N and 24% of C were lost as gases. Direct CH₄ plus N₂O emissions were 16.8 g CO₂-eq per kg dry larvae. "Ammonia emissions… could have been avoided if larvae would have been harvested before the CO₂ peak was reached." — [Parodi et al. 2020, J. Cleaner Production](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclepro.2020.122488)
  - Parodi et al. (2021): larvae raise CO₂ and NH₃ emissions on manure. — [Parodi et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.wasman.2021.04.001)
  - Bava et al. (2019): BSF larvae produced no detectable CH₄. — [Bava et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6617253/)
- **Leachate.** Eawag advises draining excess liquid, discharging it for treatment or biogas, and wet-harvesting slurries. — [Eawag 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)
- **Odour.** Lactic fermentation broth reduced ammonia emission from kitchen waste. — [Li et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/1744-7917.13185)

### Inferences
- **Venezuelan climate.** In humid lowland air, evaporation from trays is slow. Wet feeds (yogurt, fresh BSG at about 75–80% water) will push trays past 80% moisture unless blended with afrecho or dewatered.
- **Moisture targets for Tryento.**
  - Mix at about 70–75% water; squeeze test: a few drops only.
  - Hold back further wet additions when the surface is shiny or liquid pools.
  - Run fans over the trays for the last 2–3 days.
- **Temperature SOP.**
  - Take a daily core temperature per tray with a probe thermometer at mid-depth.
  - Log it alongside room temperature.
  - If the core exceeds about 38–40 °C: split the tray, remove excess feed, or add dry bulking material; or delay the next feeding.
- **Early warnings.** Ammonia smell and a pH rise toward 9 are normal late in the cycle. A strong ammonia peak means harvest is due or overdue (Parodi 2020). Sour or rotten smells with grey, wet pockets indicate anaerobic zones (see Section 9).

### Gaps
- No quantitative guideline for CO₂ or NH₃ concentrations (ppm) in rearing-room air was found.
- No quantitative ventilation rate (air changes per hour) for BSF larval rooms was found.
- No quantitative oxygen threshold in the substrate was found.

## 6. Benchmarks: bioconversion, waste reduction, FCR (wet versus dry), survival, final weight, days to harvest, kg per m², and g larvae per g eggs

### Takeaway
On good substrates at 27–30 °C, published benchmarks are:
- survival 90–98%;
- final larval weight about 100–250 mg wet (about 35–39% DM);
- grow-out of about 8–14 days after a 5–7 day nursery (13–22+ days total larval period, depending on diet);
- bioconversion about 10–25% of substrate DM;
- waste reduction about 40–80% (wet) or 55–85% (substrate).

Wet (as-fed) FCRs of 5–10 are typical of wet wastes, versus about 2–4 on a DM basis. To compare Tryento's wet FCR with published dry FCR, convert with the dry-matter fractions of feed and larvae (formula below).

### Cited Findings
- **Standard metric definitions.** Bosch et al. (2020) propose standardised reporting. Larval biomass gain should be corrected for initial biomass, in g DM:

  BCR = (larval DM out − larval DM in) / substrate DM in.

  Colony origin, rearing procedures and reference substrates should also be reported. — [Bosch et al. 2020, JIFF](https://doi.org/10.3920/JIFF2019.0004)
- **Eawag harvest weighing.** Each larvero is weighed at harvest, then the larvae separately, "to monitor the treatment performance (larval yield and waste reduction)". Residue = net larvero content − larvae. DM of 50 larvae and of residue is measured at 105 °C for 24 h. "Waste reduction of up to 80% on wet weight basis has been demonstrated." — [Eawag 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)
- **Published benchmark values:**
  - Diener 2009 (chicken feed at 100 mg per larva per day): material reduction 41.8%; prepupal dry weight 48 mg; 145 g DM prepupae per m² per day. — [Diener et al. 2009](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0734242X09103838)
  - Klammsteiner 2025 (food waste, medium density): BCR 32.4%. — [Klammsteiner et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12205609/)
  - Loiotine 2024: bioconversion efficiency 12.9–14.3%; final weight 150–160 mg; survival 94.5–96.2%; about 8.2 d of grow-out from 6-day-old larvae. — [Loiotine et al. 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/veterinary-science/articles/10.3389/fvets.2024.1330342/full)
  - Welfare Part II 2025: FCR 5.25–9.59 (basis unstated, apparently as-fed); substrate reduction 79.6–85.5%; survival 95.9–98.5%. — [Insects 2025 Part II](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11765738/)
  - Bava 2019 (25 °C): ECI 0.25 (BSG) to 0.36 (okara); final weight 98 mg (BSG) to 229 mg (hen diet); larval DM 36.5–38.9%. — [Bava et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6617253/)
  - Li 2023: FCR 1.92–2.08 g/g at the best settings; best individual weight 124–126 mg. — [Li et al. 2023](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/1744-7917.13198)
  - Lalander 2015 (continuous reactor): 55.1% material degradation; 11.8% waste-to-biomass conversion on a total-solids basis. — [Lalander et al. 2015 via Paperity](https://paperity.org/p/36621720/high-waste-to-biomass-conversion-and-efficient-salmonella-spp-reduction-using-black)
  - Bothma 2024 (pretreated sugarcane bagasse): BCR 13.4%. — [Bothma et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1163/23524588-00001186)
  - Parodi 2021 (pig manure): 12.5% of DM stored in larvae. — [Parodi et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.wasman.2021.04.001)
  - Food-waste reactor, single batch over 10 d: 38.9% wet-weight reduction; 80.9 g larvae per kg waste (a wet FCR of about 12.4). — [Springer 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11783-025-1963-1)
- **Development time and temperature.**
  - Development took 11% longer at 27 °C than at 30 °C. — [Tomberlin et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1603/022.038.0347)
  - Time to first prepupa was 13–27 d at 27 °C depending on diet and density. — [Barragán-Fonseca et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6221057/)
  - Eawag: 5 d nursery plus 12 d grow-out, harvested on day 13 of grow-out, i.e., about 17–18 d after hatching. The data come from a facility in Indonesia (tropical); whether it was climate-controlled was not checked. — [Eawag 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)
- **Egg-to-larvae (commercial, unverified).** "1 gram of BSF eggs will yield 20,000–35,000 BSF larvae… you will have 5.2 kg – 9.1 kg mature BSF larvae" after about 2 weeks. — [Kimmy Farm](https://kimmyfarm.com/en/how-many-grams-of-bsf-larvea-after-growing)

### Inferences
**Converting wet FCR to dry FCR.** Define the dry-matter fractions:
- DM_feed = the weighted DM fraction of all feed given, including the starter;
- DM_larvae = the DM fraction of clean larvae (about 0.35–0.39 from Bava 2019; measure your own).

Then:
- **FCR_dry = FCR_wet × (DM_feed / DM_larvae)**
- Conversion % (wet) = 100 / FCR_wet.
- BCR (DM) ≈ 100 / FCR_dry, when initial larval mass is negligible, which holds for egg-seeded trays.

Worked examples, using larvae at 0.36 DM:
- **Bagazo de cerveza (DM about 0.20–0.25).** A wet FCR of 8 gives a dry FCR of 8 × 0.22/0.36 ≈ 4.9, so BCR ≈ 20%.
- **Afrecho logged dry (DM about 0.88).** A wet FCR of 3 gives a dry FCR of 3 × 0.88/0.36 ≈ 7.3, so BCR ≈ 14%.

The consequence: a feed logged dry makes the "wet" FCR look excellent while the true dry conversion may be mediocre. Tryento's FCR is only comparable across trays if every feed type has a stored DM% and the app computes FCR on a DM basis. Also log water added as zero-DM input, or exclude it consistently.

**Worked per-tray example (Eawag-type tray, 40 × 60 cm, 10,000 larvae, BSG-type wet feed).** The figures below are inferences built from cited parameters.

| Step | Assumption or calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Eggs in | 37,000–77,000 eggs/g × assumed 70–80% hatch × ~90% survival to transfer | ≈ 0.18–0.43 g eggs |
| Feed | 15 kg wet at 75% water (Eawag), 5 kg on days 1, 5 and 8 | 3.75 kg DM total (~31 mg DM/larva/d) |
| Larvae out | ~9,000–9,500 survivors × 120–180 mg | ≈ 1.1–1.7 kg wet clean larvae (≈ 0.4–0.6 kg DM at 36% DM) |
| Wet FCR | 15 / 1.1–1.7 | ≈ 9–14 |
| Conversion (wet) | kg larvae / kg feed | ≈ 7–11% |
| Dry FCR | FCR_wet × 0.25/0.36 | ≈ 6.2–9.4 |
| BCR (DM) | 100 / FCR_dry | ≈ 11–16% |
| g larvae per g eggs | 1.1–1.7 kg / 0.18–0.43 g | ≈ 2,600–9,400 g/g (≈ 2.6–9.4 kg per g eggs) |

- To reach 150–200 mg larvae, feed closer to 40–60 mg DM per larva per day (roughly double Eawag's rate). Expect wet FCR on 20–25% DM feeds to fall to about 5–8.
- **Areal yield.** 40,000 larvae/m² × 0.9 survival × 0.15–0.20 g ≈ 5.4–7.2 kg wet larvae per m² per cycle (about 12–13 days of grow-out). This agrees with Diener's 145 g DM/m²/day × 13 d ≈ 1.9 kg DM ≈ 5 kg wet per m².
- **Days, from egg seeding at about 28–30 °C.** Eggs hatch in about 4 days (commercial source) if seeded as eggs rather than neonates. The larval period to harvest (before prepupa) is about 13–18 days on good feed and 20+ days on BSG alone. Seeding-to-fasting is therefore roughly 17–24 days. Fasting-to-separation is 1 day with a 24 h fast.
- **"g larvae per g starter".** The starter is a small first feed. This KPI mainly reflects starter dose, not efficiency. Include starter DM in total feed for FCR rather than tracking it as a separate efficiency metric.

### Gaps
- There is no peer-reviewed benchmark for "g larvae per g eggs". It depends on eggs/g (contested), hatch % and survival.
- Several FCR values in the literature (Li 2023; Part II) do not state wet or dry basis in the abstracts.
- No published labour-hours per tray were found (see Section 7).

## 7. Harvest timing and separation: late larva versus prepupa, sieves and mesh, drying before sieving, self-harvesting, labour, and frass

### Takeaway
Harvest at the end of the feeding stage (late 5th or early 6th instar), when larvae are at peak weight and before they darken into prepupae. Eawag does this 12 days after 5-DOL are stocked. Crude fat peaks and protein is still high at this point; prepupae burn fat and gain minerals and chitin.

Dry the residue to a crumbly state (fans in the final days) and sieve:
- about 3 mm for manual sieves;
- about 5 mm for vibrating sieves;
- about 2.36 mm worked in one food-waste study;
- 1 mm for 5-DOL.

Self-harvesting ramps collect only a share of prepupae and leave escapees.

### Cited Findings
- **Eawag harvest timing and sieves.**
  - "After 12 days of waste treatment… the larvae have reached their maximum weight, but have not yet transformed into prepupae. Their nutritional value is, therefore, at its maximum."
  - "A sieve mesh size of around 3 mm for manual sieving and 5 mm for automated sieving", on an angled sieve; "with a higher shaking frequency, the mesh size of the sieve can be bigger".

  Source: [Eawag 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)
- **Eawag wet (slurry) harvest.**
  - Spread material on a non-shaking 5 mm flat screen in sun or strong light. Photophobic larvae crawl down through the mesh into a container.
  - Strain the larvae out, rinse twice, drain on cloth, and place in a drying container of coco peat or sawdust for "around one day". This "helps to clean their skin and gives them time to empty their gut".
  - Then shake-sieve the coco peat out; it can be reused.

  Source: [Eawag 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)
- **Self-harvesting.** Continuously fed systems with prepupal self-harvest ramps "a great share of the prepupae always remain in the material, leading to unwanted fly populations and loss of harvest". Eawag recommends batch containers instead. — [Eawag 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)
- **Moisture and sieving.** A 2.36 mm sieve separated residue at 70–75% feed moisture but not at 80%. — [Cheng et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.wasman.2017.05.046)
- **Composition over the life cycle (Liu et al. 2017).**
  - Crude fat rose rapidly from 4 to 14 days, peaking at 28.4% DM.
  - Crude protein fell to a minimum of 38% at the 12-day larva.
  - Fat dropped from 24.2% (early prepupa) to 8.2% (late pupa), while protein peaked at 46.2% in the early pupa.

  Source: [Liu et al. 2017, PLoS ONE](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0182601)
- **Prepupal composition (search snippet).** About 32% protein, 37% lipids, 19% minerals and 9% chitin on a DM basis. — [Food Research International 2018 (ScienceDirect)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0963996917308669)
- **Emissions.** Harvest before the CO₂ peak to avoid ammonia emissions. — [Parodi et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclepro.2020.122488)
- **Post-harvest processing (Eawag).**
  - Blanch in boiling water for about 1–2 min, which "kills them instantly… sanitises the product" and "triggers the larvae to empty their guts".
  - Dry to below 10% moisture for storage; larvae contain about 30% oil, which goes rancid.
  - Sun drying alone does not make larvae storable.

  Source: [Eawag 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)
- **Frass/residue (Eawag).** The residue is immature: its microbial activity is "still very high, and compete[s] for oxygen and nitrogen with the soil… might stunt, damage or even kill the surrounding plants". Options: compost about 2 months, vermicompost, or send wet residue to biogas. — [Eawag 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)
- **Labour (Eawag, whole facility).** Capacity of 21 t/week of waste (about 14 million 5-DOL/week) with 3 workers and about 40 m² for daily full-capacity operation, or 9 t/week with 1 worker on a Monday/Wednesday/Friday schedule. — [Eawag 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Harvest trigger for Tryento.** The first few percent of larvae turn dark (prepupal colour) and/or feeding activity stops, typically about 12–14 days after transfer on good feed. Harvest at this point rather than waiting for most larvae to become prepupae. Past this point, weight and fat fall and escape and pupation losses rise.
- **Sieving.** Dry the residue (fans, or a dry-bran top dressing) before sieving on a 3 mm manual screen. If a tray is sloppy, use the light-avoidance wet method.
- **Frass.** Keep frass as a separate logged output. Record wet mass and DM so that waste reduction can be calculated. Compost it before sale or use.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed labour time per tray (minutes per tray for sieving) was found.
- No study quantifying separation efficiency by mesh size (percentage of larvae lost to frass) was found.

## 8. Fasting (gut purging) before harvest: purpose, duration, weight loss, microbial and quality effects; is 24 h right?

### Takeaway
Fasting empties the gut of partly digested substrate. This reduces the microbial load and contaminant residues carried in the product (pesticide residues in BSFL were mostly gone within 24 h), and yields cleaner larvae.

The evidence base is thin. One study reported gut clearance after 6 h in water or 48 h without food. Eawag uses about 1 day in dry coco peat.

The 24 h default is consistent with the available evidence. No peer-reviewed weight-loss percentage for a 24 h fast was found, so Tryento's own before and after weights are themselves a useful dataset.

### Cited Findings
- **Food-safety review (2024).**
  - Gut emptying through pre-killing starvation is "understudied but promising".
  - A single cited study found "6 hours in water or 48 hours without food" cleared the gut, with "improved organoleptic and nutritional qualities and reduced microbial load".
  - A mealworm study found no starvation effect on microbial numbers.
  - Trade-offs: more time between harvest and processing, and possible welfare concerns.
  - Rinsing was ineffective against persistent parasite stages. Blanching has "superior antimicrobial effects" but does not eliminate spore-formers.

  Source: [Mitigation strategies review, PMC11171339](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11171339/)
- **Berggreen et al. (2026), JIFF.** Larvae fasted for 0, 12, 24, 48 and 72 h. "In BSFL, most residues were eliminated within 24 hours, while mealworms required up to 72 hours." Fasting is described as "an effective post-harvest strategy to reduce pesticide residues". — [Berggreen et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1163/23524588-bja10386)
- **Yang, Tomberlin & Jordan (2021).** 11-day-old larvae on Gainesville diet at 27 °C were starved 24 and 48 h. Gut microbial diversity "decreased significantly", with shifts in Actinomyces, Microbacterium, Enterococcus, Sphingobacterium and Leucobacter. The study did not report weight loss. — [Yang et al. 2021, Frontiers in Microbiology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/microbiology/articles/10.3389/fmicb.2021.601253/full)
- **Common practice (search summary).** "Larvae are typically starved for 24 hours to empty their gut." This comes from a search-result summary, not a primary study. — [Frontiers 2021 (as surfaced in search)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/microbiology/articles/10.3389/fmicb.2021.601253/full)
- **Eawag.** Rinsed larvae sit for about 1 day on dry coco peat or sawdust, which "gives them time to empty their gut which adds to the quality". Blanching also triggers gut emptying. — [Eawag 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)
- **Ohhara et al. (2026).** Critical weight of 83 mg, "above which larvae irreversibly committed to metamorphosis". Larvae below this weight survived starvation for months. — [Ohhara et al. 2026, Arthropod Structure & Development](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.asd.2026.101535)

### Inferences
- **Is 24 h right?** Keep 24 h as the default. It matches the pesticide-clearance data (most residues gone by 24 h) and the Eawag practice. 48 h adds little documented benefit, costs more weight and fat, and pushes larvae above the 83 mg critical weight toward prepupation. The last point is an inference, not directly tested.
- **Fasting conditions.** Fast on a clean, dry, absorbent medium such as coco peat, sawdust or dry bran that is not food, or in clean trays. Fasting larvae left in their own frass can re-ingest residue.
- **Log the fast.** Record fasting duration (h) and room temperature. Weight loss = (pre − post) / pre. Build an internal benchmark per substrate; expect a higher loss for wet, bulky feeds.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed percentage weight loss for BSFL after 12, 24 or 48 h of fasting was found in this session.
- Microbial log-reduction data specific to fasting in BSFL (as opposed to blanching) are scarce. Only a single study is cited in the 2024 review.

## 9. Problems: pests, fungal growth, escaping larvae, anaerobic/sour trays, and heat die-offs, with prevention

### Takeaway
The main biological threats to larval trays are:
- filth flies (house flies, fruit flies) breeding in unconsumed feed;
- ants and predators;
- mites and phorid flies;
- parasitoid wasps that attack pupae (mainly a colony/pupation-area risk).

Most tray failures are physical:
- over-wet or over-deep feed leading to anaerobic, sour trays;
- excess feed leading to heat and flies;
- core overheating above about 40 °C.

Prevention comes down to four controls: correct moisture and depth, feeding to consumption, ventilation, and physical exclusion (screens, water moats) with cleaning between batches.

### Cited Findings
- **Excess feed (Eawag).** Excess feed forms an unprocessed layer that "can build up heat through bacterial activity" and "will also attract other filth flies". — [Eawag 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)
- **Ant control (Eawag).** Table legs stand in water-filled ant-trap containers: "Four ant traps per love cage table. The containers should always contain water." — [Eawag 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)
- **Continuous systems (Eawag).** Failure "due to diseases, mycotoxins or environmental influences… is fatal to the whole treatment unit". Batch containers (larveros) divide the risk. — [Eawag 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)
- **Parasitoids.**
  - Dirhinus giffardii in a Ghana BSF facility killed "up to 71% of pupae in a batch, with an average of 52%". — [Devic & Maquart 2015 (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/286346095_Dirhinus_giffardii_Hymenoptera_Chalcididae_parasitoid_affecting_Black_Soldier_Fly_production_systems_in_West_Africa)
  - In large-scale production in India, three pupal parasitoids were found: Dirhinus sp., Trichopria sp. and Calyoza sp. Dirhinus parasitism reached 71.95–76.30%. — [Indian Journal of Entomology](https://indianentomology.org/index.php/ije/article/view/2985)
  - A pupal parasitoid (Eniacomorpha hermetiae) has been described as a potential threat to mass production. — [Zootaxa](https://www.mapress.com/zt/article/view/zootaxa.4638.2.4)
  - Covering trays with nets is reported as "the only effective option" against parasitoids in high-volume systems. — [WUR welfare review (as surfaced in search; not read in full)](https://edepot.wur.nl/583853)
- **Mites, nematodes and phorids.** Control relies on quarantine and cleaning of new stocks and substrates, and on cleaning bins, hands and tools between larval populations. — [WUR welfare review (as surfaced in search)](https://edepot.wur.nl/583853)
- **Heat.** Survival fell to 0.1% at a constant 36 °C, and the upper limit is about 44 °C. Larvae raise the substrate at least 10 °C above air temperature. — [Tomberlin et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1603/022.038.0347); [Chia et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0206097); [Li et al. 2023](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/1744-7917.13198)
- **Wet and anaerobic trays.**
  - Feed above 80% water becomes a slurry that cannot be sieved and forms "a sticky mass, which would make harvesting almost impossible" at intermediate states. — [Eawag 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf); [Cheng et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.wasman.2017.05.046)
  - Molasses-rich (sticky) substrates restricted larval movement and growth. — [Zandi-Sohani & Tomberlin 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11508635/)
- **Microbial hazards in waste-fed larvae.** Spore-formers (B. cereus, C. perfringens) were found at 1.3–6.6 log CFU/g, and yeasts and moulds at 1.3–4.4 log CFU/g, depending on the waste. — [Waste Management 2025, microbial safety of BSFL on food waste](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0956053X25000194)
- **Pesticides.** Fluopyram and etofenprox in substrate reduced total BSFL mass produced. — [Berggreen et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1163/23524588-bja10386)

### Inferences
- **Troubleshooting guide for Tryento trays:**

  | Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
  |---|---|---|
  | Sour or rotten smell, grey wet layer, larvae crowding the surface or climbing out | Too wet and/or too deep; anaerobic | Stop wet feeds; top-dress with afrecho or other dry bulking material; split the tray; ventilate |
  | Very hot core (>40 °C), larvae fleeing to edges, sudden die-off | Overfeeding, high density, warm room | Reduce feed per feeding; spread to more trays; improve airflow; feed in cooler hours |
  | House fly or fruit fly maggots, adult flies around trays | Unconsumed feed, especially fruit, yogurt or sugary material | Feed to consumption; bury wet feed under a dry top layer; screen rooms |
  | Mould mats on the surface | Surplus dry feed not being worked; low larval density | Avoid large surplus feedings; match feed to larval number; stir or break up mats |
  | Escapes | Excess moisture, heat, ammonia, or larvae reaching prepupa | Smooth-walled trays with a lip or overhang, moisture and heat control, harvest before prepupa |

  These symptom-to-cause links are inferred from the cited mechanisms.
- Yogurt and fruit are high-risk attractants for Drosophila and Musca. Mixing them into drier material immediately and not overfeeding are the key controls.

### Gaps
- No quantitative data on rodent or bird losses were found.
- No data on mite outbreaks in larval trays (as opposed to colonies) were found.
- No data on the prevalence of specific fungi, or on fungicide-free control protocols, were found.

## 10. Biosecurity and hygiene for the larval rearing room

### Takeaway
The best-documented practical protocol (Eawag) relies on:
- one-cohort, all-in/all-out batch containers;
- a central "dirty" feeding station;
- pressure-washing and drying containers, screens and buckets after every batch;
- gloves and lab coats for staff;
- quality control of incoming waste to exclude chemical contaminants;
- physical barriers (water-moat ant traps; nets or screens against flies and parasitoids).

Post-harvest heat treatment (blanching) is needed for microbial safety, because larval treatment alone does not sanitise the product.

### Cited Findings
- **Eawag facility practices.**
  - A centralised feeding station concentrates "a potentially filthy area in one spot".
  - Larveros, screens and buckets are cleaned with a high-pressure washer and left to dry after each use.
  - Hatchling containers are cleaned "with the pressure washer, a scrubbing brush and detergent".
  - Workers use "lab coat and latex gloves for handling 5-DOL and residue", and "gloves and protective clothing during feeding activities".

  Source: [Eawag 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)
- **Waste intake control (Eawag).** Reject waste with "acids, solvents, pesticides, detergents and heavy metals… especially… in a liquid or dissolved form, as this can easily contaminate the whole batch". Remove plastics. Use only safe water to moisten dry feed. — [Eawag 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)
- **Pathogen reduction (Eawag).** Risk reduction from BSF treatment comes "mainly through material reduction (~80%) rather than through pathogen inactivation". — [Eawag 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)
- **Post-harvest treatment.** "Following harvest, larvae require heat or other treatments for microbial inactivation and safety." — [Gold et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.wasman.2018.10.022)
- **Blanching.** Superior antimicrobial effect with minimal quality loss, but it does not eliminate spores. — [PMC11171339](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11171339/)
- **Salmonella.** Salmonella spp. and viruses were reduced in a continuous BSF reactor. Retention time was a key factor. — [Lalander et al. 2015](https://paperity.org/p/36621720/high-waste-to-biomass-conversion-and-efficient-salmonella-spp-reduction-using-black)
- **Quarantine and cleaning.** Quarantine new stock and substrates, and clean bins, hands and tools between populations (mites, nematodes, diseases). Use nets against parasitoids. — [WUR welfare review (as surfaced in search)](https://edepot.wur.nl/583853)

### Inferences
- **Hygiene SOP for Tryento:**
  1. Clean and dry each tray between cycles.
  2. Keep feed storage (fresh BSG and yogurt spoil within days in the heat; use BSG within about 2–5 days) separate from the larval room.
  3. Screen windows and doors against flies and wasps.
  4. Put water-moat or oil-moat feet on racks against ants.
  5. Keep the colony/pupation room separate from larval grow-out, to limit spread of parasitoids and mites.
  6. Record feed batches per tray, so that a failed tray can be traced to a feed lot.
- **Tie SOP compliance to the data model:** feed lot ID, feed DM%, tray core temperature and fasting duration per tray.

### Gaps
- No published BSF-specific cleaning-agent or disinfectant protocol, and no environmental microbial targets for larval rooms, were found.
- No Latin American regulatory hygiene requirements for insect-for-feed facilities were researched in this session (outside scope).
