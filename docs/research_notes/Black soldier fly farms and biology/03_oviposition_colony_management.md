# Black soldier fly (Hermetia illucens) oviposition, egg harvesting, incubation and breeding-colony management

Scope note: quantitative notes for running and benchmarking Tryento's adult colonies ("insectarios" ICA, ICB, ICC, JN3A). Cages run at 30 °C ±1 and 72% RH ±3. Adult mating and light conditions belong to another researcher and appear here only where they change oviposition. Unless stated otherwise, "eggs/g" means fresh egg mass as scraped from traps.

## 1. Oviposition behaviour: onset after emergence, site choice, clutch size, eggs per female

### Takeaway
At 27-30 °C, females mate from about day 2 after emergence and start laying at about day 3-4. Most of a same-age cohort's laying happens in the following week. Each female lays roughly one clutch of about 400-1,000 eggs (about 10-30 mg) into dry, narrow crevices (1-3 mm) placed close to, but not on, a moist, smelly substrate. Realized output is about 300-500 eggs per female. Temperature, adult age structure and a microbially active attractant are the main levers.

### Cited Findings
- Mating began 2 d after emergence and oviposition at 4 d of age (Sheppard colony, greenhouse, Georgia, USA). Adults mated and laid at 24 °C to 40 °C or more and at 30-90% RH. Booth & Sheppard (1984) found 99.6% of field oviposition at 27.5-37.5 °C. — [Sheppard et al. 2002, J. Med. Entomol.](https://forensicentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2016/11/sheppard-colony-methods-2002.pdf)
- The pre-oviposition period depends on temperature: 4.3 d at 25 °C, 3.1 d at 30 °C and 2.46 d at 35 °C. Modelled fecundity peaks at 28.6 °C, is 23% lower at 35 °C and 54% lower at 20 °C. — [Kobelski et al. 2024, Front. Bioeng. Biotechnol.](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/bioengineering-and-biotechnology/articles/10.3389/fbioe.2024.1404776/full)
- The pre-oviposition period was about 4 d at all adult densities (26-27 °C, 60% RH). The oviposition period ran 11-15 d, and the peak fell on day 8.8-10.3 under long light regimes. With 12-18 h light the peak came around day 3 and laying lasted about 13 d; with 2-6 h light the peak came around day 5 and laying lasted about 16 d. — [Hoc et al. 2019, PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0216160)
- Pre-oviposition was 4.0-4.4 d whatever the adult diet (28 °C, 71% RH). Eggs per clutch were 412-478 and did not differ by diet. The oviposition period was 6.4 d for unfed adults and 17.2 d for adults fed sugar + milk powder + peptone. Adults on that diet produced about 3× more eggs (60,694 vs 20,808 per 250 females). — [Bertinetti et al. 2019, J. Insect Sci.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6369865/)
- Clutch size: a mean of 998 eggs per egg mass and 29.1 mg per mass; individual eggs about 0.028 mg. — [Booth & Sheppard 1984, Environ. Entomol.](https://academic.oup.com/ee/article-abstract/13/2/421/2393178). The Sheppard colony averaged 603-689 eggs per cardboard flute (Tomberlin 2001, cited in [Sheppard et al. 2002](https://forensicentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2016/11/sheppard-colony-methods-2002.pdf)).
- Manual rule of thumb: a female lays 400-800 eggs. The Indonesian reference unit budgets about 350 eggs per female. — [Dortmans et al. 2017, Eawag manual](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)
- Age matters. In the same-age treatment, 2-d-old males paired with 2-d-old females mated 2.2× more than when females were 6 d old, and 2.7× more than when both sexes were 6 d old. 97.8% of matings were seen on days 2-6. — [Dickerson et al. 2024, J. Econ. Entomol.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38775295)
- Cage cohort age: same-age cohorts (females 1-3 d old at release) laid 6.58× more trapped eggs than mixed 1-16-d cohorts, and those eggs were 1.17× more fertile. Mixed cohorts laid 2.09× more often in cage material outside the traps. The authors recommend releasing flies into cages within the first 1-2 d of adulthood. Preprint, not peer reviewed, and the age effect is confounded by holding flies before release. — [Lemke et al. 2024, bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.08.13.607807)
- Site choice is mainly driven by smell. Traps above boxes with no attractant caught almost no eggs. Anecdotal observation suggests local humidity also steers laying, and eggs spread along the cage netting nearest a wet wall. — [Lemke et al. 2024](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.08.13.607807)
- Larger larvae and females lay more. Egg production correlated positively with female body weight. — [Zhang & Puniamoorthy 2025, Insects](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11857012/). The largest larvae produced adults with the best clutch parameters. — [Georgescu et al. 2020, Eur. J. Entomol.](http://www.eje.cz/artkey/eje-202001-0023_body_weight_loss_of_black_soldier_fly_hermetia_illucens_diptera_stratiomyidae_during_development_in_non-feed.php)

### Inferences
- At Tryento's 30 °C, expect the first eggs about 3 d after the first adults emerge. Mating and laying peak for each emergence cohort about 3-7 d after that cohort emerges. The 30 °C setpoint sits slightly above the modelled fecundity optimum (28.6 °C) but well inside the productive range.
- Continuous or top-up loading of pupae into an open cage creates mixed-age populations and more eggs laid on the netting. Each insectario should be one pupal cohort, loaded within a few days.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed estimate of how many clutches a typical female lays in mass cages (one vs several). Bertinetti's clutch counts and Nakamura et al. 2016's "additional unviable clutches" point to some multiple laying, but no figures were retrieved.
- I found no data from Venezuelan or other Latin American caged colonies on onset and peak timing.

## 2. Oviposition substrates (egg traps) and attractants: types, evidence, distance, timing and swap frequency

### Takeaway
Narrow, dry crevices of 1-3 mm work best: corrugated cardboard, stacked wood or plastic strips with 1-2 mm spacers, or floral foam holes. Place them directly above or beside a moist, fermenting attractant. Attractants that combine feed, frass or larval residue, dead adults and water, or fermented fruit and vegetables, beat plain feed. Live larvae or microbes in the attractant increase its pull. For a same-age cohort, put the attractant in from about day 2-3 after emergence (about 1 d after peak mating). Traps are normally changed every 1-3 days. They must come out before eggs hatch, which takes about 3.5 d at 30 °C.

### Cited Findings
- Slot width: over 90% of clutches went into 1-3 mm slots (1 mm 58.6%, 2 mm 16.4%, 3 mm 19.3%, 4-5 mm under 8%). Clutches were heavier in wider slots: 17.5 mg at 1 mm, 21.2 mg at 2 mm and 26.2 mg at 3 mm, and 23.1 vs 28.6 mg for 1 vs 2 mm in a second trial. The authors recommend 2-3 mm slots. Adjustable wooden boards were more reproducible than corrugated cardboard. Conditions: 27 °C, 60% RH, Gainesville diet. — [Georgescu et al. 2022, Insects](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9319695/)
- Height above the substrate: clutches were spread from 4 mm (22.1% of clutches) up to 28 mm (17.9%) above the substrate, so traps work within about 3 cm. The attractant's dry matter:water ratio matters: at 1:1, 43.2% of females laid (183 clutches); at 1:0.5, only 6.8%; at 1:1.5 and 1:2, 24-25%. — [Georgescu et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9319695/)
- Sheppard's traps were three layers of double-faced corrugated cardboard, about 3 flutes per cm, cut into 2.5 × 5 cm blocks. Smaller openings were preferred. The attractant was Gainesville diet mixed 1:1 by volume with water and made wetter, close to saturation, so females would lay in the dry cardboard rather than in the medium. Wet substrates are less attractive for laying directly on them. More than 100 egg-filled flutes were collected in a day. — [Sheppard et al. 2002](https://forensicentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2016/11/sheppard-colony-methods-2002.pdf)
- The Eawag "eggie" is a bundle of 5 thin wooden sheets (25 × 5 × 0.3 cm) with pushpins giving 1-2 mm gaps, 10 eggies per cage. They sit directly over the attractant container under a shading basket. Plastic eggies are preferred when weighing eggs, because wood and cardboard take up moisture and distort the net egg weight. — [Dortmans et al. 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)
- Eawag attractant recipe: 100 g dead flies from an old cage + 200 g nursery residue + 200 g old attractant residue + 1 L fermenting-fruit water (plain water if none), two containers per cage. The manual notes that eggs already laid attract more females, so it advises against harvesting every day. — [Dortmans et al. 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)
- Carbognin et al. 2025 compared six attractants: (a) frass + water; (b) dead adults + water; (c) MIX of chicken feed + frass + dead adults + water; (d) chicken feed + water; (e) fruit, legumes and vegetables; (f) no attractant. MIX gave the highest egg weight. With MIX, 1 collector per cage caught about the same egg weight as 5, so they recommend one. — [Carbognin et al. 2025, Entomol. Exp. Appl.](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/eea.70022)
- Plant-based substrates (fruit and vegetables) were most attractive to mated females, and pineapple was best. Perching preference tracked the weight of eggs laid. — [PeerJ 2023, behavioural responses to attractants](https://peerj.com/articles/15701/)
- Fermented fruit waste, using Effective Microorganisms, outperformed non-fermented waste as an oviposition attractant. — [Scholarhub UI (Makara J. Sci.)](https://scholarhub.ui.ac.id/science/vol29/iss4/2/)
- The most preferred oviposition substrates (for example millet porridge mash) were not the best for larval development. — [ResearchGate: substrates most preferred are not most suitable](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362889347_Substrates_most_preferred_for_black_soldier_fly_Hermetia_illucens_Linnaeus_1758_Diptera_Stratiomyidae_oviposition_are_not_the_most_suitable_for_their_larval_development)
- Bacteria mediate oviposition (Zheng et al. 2013, Sci. Rep.), and larval activity in inoculated substrate changes its volatile profile (Michishita et al. 2023). Lemke et al. therefore inoculated each attractant box (500 g Gainesville diet at 70% moisture) with about 1,000 larvae aged 7 d. Egg traps (three cardboard strips 10 × 3.5 × 1.3 cm) sat across a screened vent in the lid. — [Lemke et al. 2024](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.08.13.607807)
- Newer attractant leads, titles only, with no numbers retrieved: a bacterium isolated from the BSF ovary attracts oviposition ([PMC12467855](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12467855/)), and a chemically defined attractant and repellent has been described ([bioRxiv 2024](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.06.04.597456.full.pdf)).
- Hoc et al. used three pine planks (12 × 4.5 × 4 cm) spaced with screws, over 250 g of 7-day fermented carrot under mosquito netting. Traps were emptied every day. — [Hoc et al. 2019](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0216160)
- Park et al. used floral foam with 5 mm × 7 mm holes above 50% food waste + 50% calf feed, replaced daily. — [Park et al. 2016, Int. J. Indust. Entomol.](https://koreascience.kr/article/JAKO201606776010895.pdf)
- Timing (preprint): in same-age cohorts, putting the attractant in 1 d after peak mating raised trapped egg weight to 0.89 vs 0.78 g/cage/day (about 1.22-1.25×). Hatch rose to 60% vs 50%, and laying in cage material fell (2.31 vs 2.71 events/cage/day). In mixed-age cohorts, delay gave no benefit, and an attractant from day 1 is better at pulling old females into the traps. The authors say that for logistics, putting the attractant in from the start is acceptable because same-age cohorts did not do significantly worse. — [Lemke et al. 2024](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.08.13.607807)
- Traps were collected and replaced daily at 12:00-13:00, and eggs were brushed off with a size-2 filbert paintbrush. — [Lemke et al. 2024](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.08.13.607807)

### Inferences
- For Tryento, set the projected "ovipositores/atractante" date at about day 2-3 after the first adults emerge. If the colony-start date is the day pupae are loaded, that is about 10-12 d after start at 30 °C (see Q6). Placing traps on day 0 costs little in a same-age cage and helps catch early layers. Recording the planned-vs-actual gap enables a local test of whether delaying helps.
- Change traps every 1-2 d, never more than 2.5 d. Embryos show red eye spots at 2.5 d and hatch at about 3.5 d at 30 °C (Q4), so longer intervals lose larvae inside the cage. Leaving a few egg-laden traps in place, as Eawag suggests, may help attraction. That trades against hatch-in-cage losses and has not been tested quantitatively.
- A MIX-type attractant (feed + frass + dead adults + water, kept at about 1:1 dry matter:water and topped up with residue from active larval trays) matches the best-supported recipes and costs nothing on a larval farm.

### Gaps
- I found no study that measures the optimal horizontal distance between attractant and trap. The evidence is only that traps within about 0-3 cm above the attractant work.
- No peer-reviewed head-to-head of cardboard vs wood vs plastic flutes for egg yield per cage was found. Only slot-width data exist, and the Eawag advice on material is practical, not experimental.
- I could not access the Carbognin et al. 2025 full text for its egg weights, cage densities or attractant ratios.

## 3. Egg harvest: frequency, removal method, clutch weight, eggs per gram, egg mass vs larvae, damage and desiccation

### Takeaway
Most direct measurements put a fresh BSF egg at 25-29 µg, which is about **34,000-40,000 eggs per gram**. The ~37,000/g vendor figure is consistent with the literature. The ~77,000/g figure appears to come from one strain-specific dataset (Cai et al. 2022, Wuhan strain) and should not be used without local calibration. Harvest traps every 1-2 days. Either brush eggs off gently and weigh them, or weigh each whole trap against a standard tare weight and hatch the eggs on the trap. Keep eggs loose (no thick clumps), warm (never below about 6 °C) and moderately humid.

### Cited Findings
- Egg-weight data behind eggs per gram:
  - Booth & Sheppard 1984: 0.028 mg per egg; 998 eggs per 29.1 mg mass (about 0.029 mg) → about 34,000-36,000 eggs/g. — [Environ. Entomol.](https://academic.oup.com/ee/article-abstract/13/2/421/2393178)
  - Bertinetti et al. 2019: 0.02557 ± 0.00001 mg (28 °C) → about 39,100 eggs/g. — [J. Insect Sci.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6369865/)
  - Hoc et al. 2019: 0.025 ± 0.003 mg → about 40,000 eggs/g (35,700-45,500 across the SD). — [PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0216160)
  - Eawag manual: "one egg weighs about 25 µg" → 40,000 eggs/g. — [Dortmans et al. 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)
  - SUSINCHAIN (EU project) experiments: "0.000027 g ⇒ 1 egg" → about 37,000 eggs/g. — [SUSINCHAIN best-practice sheet 2023](https://susinchain.eu/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/Best-practice-storage-and-transport-of-BSF-eggs-and-neonates.pdf)
  - Vendor: 0.027 mg per egg, about 37,000/g. — [Kimmy Farm](https://kimmyfarm.com/en/product/black-soldier-fly-eggs-10g) (commercial; consistent with the above)
  - Outlier: Cai et al. 2022 report 411 mg for 31,647 eggs (Wuhan strain) and 637 mg for 49,049 eggs (inbred strain), and 4.3-6.4 mg of eggs per female for 331-492 eggs. That implies about 0.013 mg per egg, or about 77,000 eggs/g. — [Cai et al. 2022, Life](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9227254/)
- Clutch mass: 17.5-28.6 mg per clutch depending on slot width. — [Georgescu et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9319695/). Park et al.'s totals (368.7 g in 9,016 clutches) work out to about 41 mg per "clutch" (a hole may hold more than one female's eggs). — [Park et al. 2016](https://koreascience.kr/article/JAKO201606776010895.pdf). Hoc et al. measured 16.6-22.5 mg of eggs per female. — [Hoc et al. 2019](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0216160)
- Eawag measures harvest as the weight of a full eggie minus its empty weight, using standardized eggie types and weights. Egg number is then estimated from egg mass. The manual discourages touching eggs because every touch can damage them. — [Dortmans et al. 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)
- In the Eawag "hatchling shower", harvested eggies are not scraped. They hang on a grate over a hatchling container for 7 days, colour-coded by weekday, and are then cleaned without detergent and reused. — [Dortmans et al. 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)
- Egg handling risks: eggs are very sensitive to temperature swings and dehydration, which cause yield losses in storage. At most 3 g of 24 h-old eggs should go in a 500 mL box on a mesh, so they do not clump and die. Keep them above 25 °C at 60% RH, since eggs are sensitive to low temperatures; temperatures below 6 °C may kill them. — [SUSINCHAIN 2023](https://susinchain.eu/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/Best-practice-storage-and-transport-of-BSF-eggs-and-neonates.pdf)
- A patent on egg refrigeration notes that eggs dry up and die when RH is too low, and do not hatch when it is too high. Non-peer-reviewed. — [CN105724365A](https://patents.google.com/patent/CN105724365A/en)
- The weight-based "hatch rate" proxy (egg-mass loss after 7 d) overstates hatch because of desiccation weight loss and chorion residue, and understates it because some larvae never leave the cup. The authors say it should be treated only as an estimate. — [Lemke et al. 2024](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.08.13.607807)
- Higher egg output does not always mean more viable larvae. Hatchability varied with larval diet. — [Zhang & Puniamoorthy 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11857012/)

### Inferences
- Default working value: **1 g of fresh eggs ≈ 38,000 ± 3,000 eggs**. At 70-95% hatch that gives about **26,000-36,000 neonates per gram**. Tryento should calibrate this once or twice a year: count 3 × 10 mg aliquots under a stereo microscope and hatch 3 × 0.1 g aliquots on moist feed, counting larvae at day 5. Cai's ~77,000/g (13 µg per egg) is so far from every other measurement that a methods difference is the likeliest explanation (egg counting from photos, or eggs weighed after drying). If a supplier's strain really had eggs that small, "g of eggs" would mean twice as many larvae.
- For the KPIs, weigh eggs the same way every time: net of a standard trap tare, or scraped. Also log the trap interval (1, 2 or 3 d), because egg mass drops with desiccation and older eggs start hatching.

### Gaps
- No study was found that directly regresses "g of eggs seeded" against "5-day-old larvae recovered" at farm scale. The conversion above is built from egg weight × hatch × early survival.
- There is no quantitative data on how much egg mass is lost to desiccation per day on dry cardboard at 30 °C / 72% RH.

## 4. Incubation and hatching: temperature, humidity, hatch time, hatch rates, nursery substrate, fungi and mites, cold storage

### Takeaway
At 28-30 °C and about 60-80% RH, eggs hatch in about 3.5-4 d and 70-95% hatch. Incubate eggs above, not on, a moist starter feed so that neonates drop into it. Eggs placed directly on wet media, or kept in humid closed cups, get fungus. Eggs tolerate short cold storage poorly. The best published result is 1-day-old eggs held 10 d at 17 °C with 50% hatch. Chilling below about 6-10 °C is risky, and older prepupae or pupae store far better than eggs.

### Cited Findings
- Hatch time: 102-105 h at 24 °C. — [Booth & Sheppard 1984](https://academic.oup.com/ee/article-abstract/13/2/421/2393178). At 30 °C eggs hatched in 3.5 d; red eye spots appeared at 2.5 d, were well defined at 3 d, and those eggs hatched within 24 h. — [Sheppard et al. 2002](https://forensicentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2016/11/sheppard-colony-methods-2002.pdf). Eggs hatch in about four days on average. — [Dortmans et al. 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)
- The lower threshold for egg hatch lies between 12 and 16 °C, where hatching takes about 15 d. The whole life cycle completes between 15 and 37 °C. — [Chia et al. 2018, PLOS ONE](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6211680/) (summary via search results; the full text was not read).
- Egg eclosion success increased, and development time fell, as RH rose across 25, 40, 50, 60 and 70%. — [Holmes, Vanlaerhoven & Tomberlin 2012, Environ. Entomol.](https://academic.oup.com/ee/article/41/4/971/448259) (abstract level; the exact hatch percentages were not retrieved).
- Hatch benchmarks:
  - 93.5-96.5% (26-27 °C, 60% RH). — [Hoc et al. 2019](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0216160)
  - 71.6-81.8% (eggs incubated at 28 °C, 80% RH). — [Bertinetti et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6369865/)
  - About 70% for untreated fresh eggs (non-peer-reviewed). — [CN105724365A](https://patents.google.com/patent/CN105724365A/en)
  - 50-60% by the weight-loss proxy, which is biased low (26 °C). — [Lemke et al. 2024](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.08.13.607807)
  - The Eawag Indonesian flow budgets about 70% survival at the egg-to-early-larva step. — [Dortmans et al. 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)
- Nursery method: eggies from several days hang together over an open hatchling container, so neonates fall into the feed. The feed is 30% dry starter chicken feed + 70% water (3 kg per 60 × 40 × 12 cm container), covered with a thin layer of dry coco peat. The container is replaced every 1-3 days, and larvae stay 5 d to become "5-DOL" (five-day-old larvae), which are counted by weighing 2 × 2 g samples. — [Dortmans et al. 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)
- Fungus: placing eggs directly on media usually gave poor hatch and neonate survival, suspected to be fungal. A paper towel held snugly over the cup raised humidity and often led to fungus. Fungus was rarely seen when neonates were placed on fresh medium. Eggs were held in a 460 mL cup with a lid at 30 °C and ambient humidity until hatch. — [Sheppard et al. 2002](https://forensicentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2016/11/sheppard-colony-methods-2002.pdf)
- Mites: the poultry red mite (*Dermanyssus gallinae*) invaded a BSF colony, preying on larvae and prepupae and quickly degrading colony production and health. It is described as the first ectoparasite case in a BSF colony. — [Mahmoud et al. 2023, JIFF](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/361745265_Poultry_red_mite_Dermanyssus_gallinae_poses_a_risk_in_the_rearing_of_black_soldier_fly_Hermetia_illucens). Batch cage systems are argued to reduce the spread of such pests compared with continuous systems. — [Lemke et al. 2024](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.08.13.607807)
- Cold storage (peer-reviewed, 2026): 1-day-old eggs stored at 17 °C for 10 d hatched at 50.0%. By contrast, larvae kept 72.8% survival after 30 d at 15 °C, and prepupae kept 75.3% eclosion after 60 d at 14 °C. Dietary cryoprotectants (4% proline + 0.5% trehalose) improved cold tolerance. — [Wei et al. 2026, Insect Science](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41549582/) ([Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1744-7917.70234))
- Chilling eggs 24 h at 10 °C and 80% RH paused development for about a day. Embryos were least sensitive at about 38-58 h after laying, with more than 80% hatch relative to controls. — [Low-temperature embryonic development study, ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/354745668_The_phases_and_effects_of_low_temperature_exposure_on_embryonic_development_in_Hermetia_illucens_L_Diptera_Stratiomyidae) (summary only; full text not read)
- Egg refrigeration patent (non-peer-reviewed): storage at 0-10 °C and 70-80% RH extended egg life to 25 d with more than 30% hatch, vs about 70% for fresh eggs. Refrigerated eggs hatched 4-7 d after removal. — [CN105724365A](https://patents.google.com/patent/CN105724365A/en)
- Transport: eggs at 24 h old, at most 3 g per 500 mL box, above 25 °C and 60% RH. Neonates (2 d old) survive storage better than eggs but are density-sensitive: 1 g per box with 20 g feed + 18 g water, at 25 °C and 60% RH. Transfers should not exceed 4-5 d. — [SUSINCHAIN 2023](https://susinchain.eu/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/Best-practice-storage-and-transport-of-BSF-eggs-and-neonates.pdf)

### Inferences
- Tryento can incubate eggs in the cage room at 30 °C and about 70% RH. Expect hatch at about day 3-4. Plan to seed trays with neonates or 5-DOL, or to hang eggs over the tray, rather than burying eggs in wet substrate.
- To smooth weekly egg surpluses, holding **prepupae or pupae at about 14-15 °C** is far more robust than cold-storing eggs. If eggs must be held, keep it to a few days at about 17-20 °C, and start chilling after roughly the first 1.5 days of development. Log the storage days so hatch losses can be attributed.

### Gaps
- The exact hatch-vs-RH curve from Holmes et al. 2012 and the egg-stage temperature table from Chia et al. 2018 were not retrieved in full.
- I found no quantitative reports on mite species attacking BSF eggs specifically, or on fungal genera on egg traps.

## 5. Colony yield benchmarks: g eggs per kg pupae, eggs per female, productive lifespan, peak and closing, emergence and mortality curves

### Takeaway
Published trap yields range from about **8 to 37 g of eggs per kg of pupae** per cage cycle (Park et al. 2016, 12-14 d collection). Biology sets a ceiling of roughly **25-45 g per kg**. For a same-age cohort, daily output peaks about 3-10 d after adults appear. Individual adults live about 7-14 d. A cage fed by staggered emergence produces for about 2-3 weeks. Close it when daily egg mass falls to a small fraction of peak, roughly 14-21 d after first emergence.

### Cited Findings
- Density trial (greenhouse, Korea, May-Sept 2016, pupae held at 26 °C, eggs collected 12-14 d after emergence, floral-foam traps replaced daily):
  - 4 kg pupae (about 26,000 pupae) gave 33.1-42.9 g of eggs.
  - 8 kg (about 52,000) gave 101.1-127.6 g.
  - 10 kg (about 65,000) gave 281.9-368.7 g.
  - Cage size (1 × 1 up to 2.5 × 2.5 × 2.5 m) had no significant effect; higher density increased egg output. The recommendation was 1.5 × 1.5 × 2.5 m or 2 × 2 × 2.5 m cages with about 65,000 pupae, loaded at regular intervals for steady emergence.
  - [Park et al. 2016](https://koreascience.kr/article/JAKO201606776010895.pdf)
- Hoc et al. tested five adult densities (500-8,500 per m³) and recommended at least 6,500 per m³ with a female-dominant population. Egg output was 7.6 g/cage (female-dominant) vs 5.2 g/cage (male-dominant) at 6,500/m³, and 16.6-22.5 mg per female. Longer light increased eggs per cage (4.1 g at 2 h vs 6.8 g at 6 h). — [Hoc et al. 2019](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0216160)
- Kobelski et al. modelled a 14-day cycle giving about 315 mg of eggs per cage of 50 females at 25 °C and 16 h light, and about 345 mg (+9.4%) under optimized control. Optimization shortened the cycle by 1 day and cut light input by 41%. Time to 50% adult mortality was about 12.4 d at 22 °C and about 9.3 d at 37 °C. — [Kobelski et al. 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/bioengineering-and-biotechnology/articles/10.3389/fbioe.2024.1404776/full)
- Eggs per female:
  - About 350 in the Eawag reference unit. — [Dortmans et al. 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)
  - 331 ± 54 (Wuhan strain) vs 492 ± 92 (selected inbred line), at 27-30 °C and 60-70% RH, with an oviposition duration of 8-17 d. — [Cai et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9227254/)
  - About 83-243 per female stocked, depending on adult diet (unfed vs sugar+milk+peptone), at 28 °C. — [Bertinetti et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6369865/)
- Adult longevity was 8.7-12.1 d for males and 9.5-14.6 d for females, depending on adult diet (28 °C). — [Bertinetti et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6369865/). In a pilot, more than 50% of females were dead by about day 7, so trials were stopped then. — [Lemke et al. 2024](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.08.13.607807)
- Eawag removes each love cage (the mating cage) after 6 days: "No more eggs will be laid after one week because most females die within one week." Each love cage is filled with flies of similar age from 3-4 dark cages (the unlit emergence cages). — [Dortmans et al. 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)
- Larval diet sets the length of the laying period: 16+ days of egg production for chicken-feed and food-waste larvae vs 15 d (okara) and 8 d (brewer's grain) at 30-32 °C and 70% RH. Reported egg mass at day 5 / day 10 was 7.6 / 10.1 g (chicken feed) vs 3.5 / 3.6 g (brewer's grain) per replicate container started with 1,500 larvae. Whether these are cumulative or daily values was not clear from the retrieved text. — [Zhang & Puniamoorthy 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11857012/)
- Seasonal and colony data: in a greenhouse colony, egg-filled flutes per day rose from 5.6 (Nov, 26 °C) and 18.8 (Jan, 22 °C) to 66.7-71.9 (May-June), with adult emergence of 124-494 per day. — [Sheppard et al. 2002](https://forensicentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2016/11/sheppard-colony-methods-2002.pdf)
- Vendor claims (commercial, unverified, likely favourable): 700 → 811 g of eggs per m² of breeding space per month from stacked breeding modules. — [Soldier Fly Technologies](https://www.soldierfly.com/breeding). Up to 2 million pupae per chamber, 10 million per unit. — [Fly Farm Systems](https://flyfarmsystems.com/bsf-breeding-systems/)

### Inferences
- Derived benchmarks for Tryento's KPI "g eggs / kg colony biomass" (all from Park et al. 2016):
  - 4 kg pupae: 8.3-10.7 g/kg
  - 8 kg: 12.6-16.0 g/kg
  - 10 kg: 28.2-36.9 g/kg
  - Park's yield per kg rose with density, so small, sparse cages will look worse per kg.
- Derived benchmarks for "g eggs per day of operation": about 22-31 g/day for 10 kg of pupae over 12-14 d, which is about 2.2-3.1 g/day per kg of pupae. Denominators must match: define "days of operation" as first emergence to closing, not pupae loading to closing.
- Biological ceiling per kg of pupae: about 6,500 pupae × 85-95% emergence × 50% female × 350-600 eggs × 26 µg ≈ 25-48 g of eggs. Values above about 45 g/kg of pupae should be treated as a measurement error, such as wet eggs, trap-tare errors or biomass under-weighed.
- Closing rule: when 3 consecutive days of egg mass fall below about 10-15% of the cage's peak day, close it. With staggered emergence (Q6), this typically lands 14-21 d after the first adults appear, or about 25-30 d after pupae loading. Log the actual closing date against this rule to tune it locally.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed full daily egg-output curve for a large (≥5 kg pupae) cage run to exhaustion, and no adult mortality or survival curve for such a cage.
- No Latin American (tropical ambient, about 30 °C) breeding-cage yield data per kg of pupae were found.

## 6. Pupae management: pupation substrate, emergence containers, sex ratio, staggered emergence, holding temperature, pupae per kg

### Takeaway
Pupae weigh about 100-200 mg, which gives **about 5,000-7,000 pupae per kg (Park et al.: 6,500/kg)**. Adults weigh about 50-80 mg, about 12,000-20,000 per kg. **Tryento's 50,000 individuals/kg therefore overestimates the population about 2.5× if the biomass is adults, and about 7-8× if it is pupae.** Emergence starts about 10 d after pupae are set out and continues bell-shaped to about day 25. Early-emerging and early-crawling individuals are male-biased. Undisturbed pupation in moist wood chips or compost gives about 80-93% eclosion. Daily sieving of prepupae cuts eclosion by about a third and delays it about 3 d.

### Cited Findings
- 4 kg of pupae ≈ 26,000 pupae and 10 kg ≈ 65,000 pupae, which is about 6,500 pupae/kg or about 154 mg per pupa (pupae held at 26 °C). — [Park et al. 2016](https://koreascience.kr/article/JAKO201606776010895.pdf)
- Adult wet mass on wood chips was 71.0 ± 1.0 mg for males and 81.0 ± 1.4 mg for females (25.8 °C, 71% RH). — [Durosaro et al. 2025, bioRxiv preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.09.15.676317)
- Weight is lost through the non-feeding stages: 12.75%, 18.88% and 20.56% during the prepupal stage in three weight classes, with further significant losses in the pupal and adult stages. — [Georgescu et al. 2020](http://www.eje.cz/artkey/eje-202001-0023_body_weight_loss_of_black_soldier_fly_hermetia_illucens_diptera_stratiomyidae_during_development_in_non-feed.php)
- Pupation substrate and handling (25.8 °C): eclosion by day 30 was 82.2% on corn-cob grits, 81.5% on wood chips and 72.1% on frass. Daily 20-second sieving of prepupae cut eclosion from 92.5% to 61.9% (−30.6%) and delayed eclosion by 3.3-3.5 d (about 17 d undisturbed vs about 20.5 d handled, from prepupa). Wood chips gave the largest adults. — [Durosaro et al. 2025](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.09.15.676317)
- Eawag practice: prepupae are sieved (3 mm) from coco peat, 2 × 200 are weighed to get the mean weight, and batches of 500 (or 400 or 800, depending on schedule) go into each of 16 containers (60 × 40 × 12 cm, 3 kg moist compost + water each) per dark cage. The containers are filled daily for 5 d, about 40,000 prepupae per dark cage on the 500 schedule. Emergence starts about 10 d after placement, follows a bell curve, and ends with latecomers at about 25 d. Dark cages are dismantled after about 2.5-3 weeks. Flies in the dark stay inactive and do not mate until lured by light into the love cage. — [Dortmans et al. 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)
- Adults emerged 10-14 d after pupation. Feeding was stopped once more than 50% were prepupae, and the medium was left to dry for pupation in the rearing pan. — [Sheppard et al. 2002](https://forensicentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2016/11/sheppard-colony-methods-2002.pdf)
- Sex ratio shifts with the order of self-harvest. The male ratio (MR) falls as cumulative self-harvested prepupal mass (SPM, g) grows: MR = 0.7169 − 0.0003 × SPM. The first crawl-offs are about 72% male, and the ratio is balanced at about 700 g out of 1,562 g (10,000 larvae). — [Hoc et al. 2019](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0216160)
- Sex ratio at eclosion was F:M about 0.95 in both Wuhan and inbred strains. Eclosion was 85.2% (Wuhan) vs 96.8% (inbred line). — [Cai et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9227254/)
- Pupal weight is the best predictor of adult sex, and a 40:60 male:female ratio was best for egg production (Indonesian study, abstract level). — [Biodjati 2020](https://journal.uinsgd.ac.id/index.php/biodjati/article/view/9472)
- Holding: prepupae kept 75.3% eclosion after 60 d at 14 °C, and larvae kept 72.8% survival after 30 d at 15 °C. — [Wei et al. 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41549582/)
- Batches of flies should be released into cages within the first 1-2 d of adulthood. Holding adults, even in the dark, is associated with large productivity losses. — [Lemke et al. 2024](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.08.13.607807)

### Inferences
- **Population cross-check for Tryento:**
  - If the recorded colony biomass is **pupae** (the usual case): individuals ≈ kg × 5,000-7,000. Expected adults ≈ kg × 5,000-7,000 × 0.8-0.95 emergence ≈ **4,000-6,650 adults per kg of pupae**, of which about 50% are female.
  - If it is live **adults**: ≈ kg × 12,000-20,000.
  - The current factor of 50,000/kg implies 20 mg per individual, lighter than any published pupa or adult weight. It inflates population by 2.5-10× and makes any "eggs per female" KPI look 2.5-10× too low.
  - Recommended practice: weigh 3 × 100 pupae at loading and record the mean mass. That turns kg into a count for each batch, and it also tracks the size trend across generations.
- To synchronize: sort prepupae into daily cohorts and hold surplus cohorts at about 14-15 °C. Load an insectario with 3-5 consecutive days of pupae. Avoid daily sieving or handling of prepupae in the pupation boxes.
- Because early crawl-offs are male-biased and late ones female-biased, loading only the first prepupae of a batch into a cage biases the sex ratio toward males. Load across the whole crawl-off window.

### Gaps
- There is no published pupae-per-kg figure for Latin American strains or for Tryento's diet. It must be measured locally.
- I found no controlled study of pupal holding temperatures between 20 and 27 °C as a way to shift emergence by a few days without loss.

## 7. Breeding programme and genetics: selecting breeders, fraction diverted, generation tracking, inbreeding, wild stock, decline signs

### Takeaway
Only a small share of production is needed to sustain the colony: Eawag keeps 2-5% of five-day-old larvae (5-DOL). The main genetic risk is not census size but a small effective population (Ne, the number of individuals that actually pass on genes). That risk comes from drawing breeders from a few cages, days or clutches. Captive BSF lose diversity fast. One colony founded from wild flies collapsed by F5. A long-running colony had Ne of only about 22-59 despite mass rearing. Commercial lines show the highest inbreeding. The species is native to the Americas, and South America (including north-western South America, where Venezuela lies) holds the most genetic diversity. Local wild flies are therefore a valuable source for controlled augmentation, but they should be introduced in large numbers and trialled on a sub-line first.

### Cited Findings
- A small fraction of 5-DOL (2-5%) is kept in the rearing unit to reproduce the colony. High survival and many eggs per female mean less needs to be retained. — [Dortmans et al. 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)
- Early domestication: a colony established from wild founders had small Ne, lost genetic diversity, diverged rapidly between generations, and collapsed by the fifth generation, most likely through inbreeding depression. The authors conclude that domestication is achievable with careful population augmentation and selection in the early generations. — [Rhode et al. 2020, Animal Genetics](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32524667/)
- A mass-reared colony founded in 2012 from wild flies was sampled at F28, F48, F52 and F62. It showed reduced diversity, inbreeding and successive generational differentiation from the wild source. Estimated Ne was 59.0 (F28), 56.7 (F48), 22.6 (F52) and 24.4 (F62). The authors warn that Ne below 100 carries a risk of inbreeding depression. Mated pairs were highly related (0.455-1.000 vs −0.034 in the source), indicating positive assortative mating. Multiple paternity was found in 2 of 5 families, the first evidence of polyandry in BSF. They recommend testing wild augmentation on a small subset first. — [Hoffmann et al. 2021, Insects](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8224309/)
- Global structure: 2,862 individuals from 150 wild and captive populations in 57 countries. Origin is in the Americas, most likely South America. Central-east South America holds the highest allelic diversity and most private alleles, and north-western South America was a secondary source that colonized Central America. Captive populations have significantly lower allelic richness than wild ones, and the dominant commercial strain (founded 1998 from about 20,000 wild pupae in Georgia, USA, about 200 generations) shows a founder effect and strong selection. The authors note that regional wild populations may hold advantageous alleles and warn of introgression from traded strains into wild populations. — [Kaya et al. 2021, BMC Biology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8101212/)
- Genomes show two cryptic lineages that diverged millions of years ago. Most commercial and academic stocks derive from one North-American-derived lineage (the Sheppard stock), whose heterozygosity is about 39% lower than wild populations (0.031 vs 0.051). A South American captive sample retained the highest heterozygosity (reported as 0.087). The authors recommend diverse South American sources, controlled introgression, genetic monitoring, and checking for hybrid vigour vs hybrid breakdown when crossing divergent lineages. — [Generalovic et al. 2026, Genome Biol. Evol.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13171150/) (values taken from a tool summary of the full text; verify before quoting)
- Inbreeding coefficients (F_IS) were 0.121-0.234 across 11 populations. Wild populations were lowest (0.121, 0.144) and the two commercial populations highest (0.234, 0.227). Selected lines lost Ne sharply after the selection phase (for example about 257,700 → 49,500). Recommendations: a broad founder base, periodic augmentation at set intervals, internal renewal from several preserved lines, and routine monitoring of heterozygosity, F_IS and ancestry. — [Silvaraju et al. 2025, BMC Biology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12797794)
- Counter-example: deliberately inbreeding from the single clutch with the most hatched larvae each generation, for 10 generations followed by 20+ generations of relaxed selection, **raised** eggs per female 1.55× (331 → 492) and eclosion from 85% to 97% (Wuhan strain, China). This is a single study. — [Cai et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9227254/)
- Size selection: the largest larvae yield adults with the best clutch parameters. — [Georgescu et al. 2020](http://www.eje.cz/artkey/eje-202001-0023_body_weight_loss_of_black_soldier_fly_hermetia_illucens_diptera_stratiomyidae_during_development_in_non-feed.php). Egg output correlates with female body weight. — [Zhang & Puniamoorthy 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11857012/). Long-term industrial selection for larval body weight has been reported (Facchini et al. 2022, Front. Genet., cited in [Lemke et al. 2024](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.08.13.607807)); its numbers were not retrieved.
- Mixing overlapping generations or cohorts over long periods may promote admixture that contributes to inbreeding depression. Not all mass-reared insect populations suffer it when managed properly. — [Lemke et al. 2024](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.08.13.607807)
- One generation takes about 38-45 d at 27-30 °C (larvae 20-24 d on wheat bran). — [Cai et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9227254/)

### Inferences
- **Fraction to divert:** with realistic survival (70-90% hatch, 70-80% larva→pupa, about 6,500 pupae/kg), each kg of breeder pupae needs about 9,000-13,000 larvae, about 0.25-0.35 g of eggs. Because 1 kg of pupae yields about 10-30 g of eggs (Q5), the breeding line consumes only about **1-3% of egg output**, in line with Eawag's 2-5% of 5-DOL.
- **Selecting breeders:** do not take only the largest or the first-crawling larvae. That biases the sex ratio (Q6) and shrinks Ne. Two good-practice options: (a) take a random, volumetric sample of eggs pooled from **all active cages across several harvest days**; or (b) apply mild size selection (for example discard the smallest 20-30%) on a large pooled cohort. Rearing breeder trays on a consistent, good diet matters as much as selection, because female size drives fecundity.
- **Generation tracking:** at 30 °C, expect about 6-7 weeks per generation, about 8 per year. The F-labels (F6, F7…) should increment when a new insectario's pupae descend from eggs of the previous generation's cages. When eggs from cages of different generations are pooled, record the lowest generation plus "mixed", or keep lines separate.
- **Genetic safeguards:** keep at least 2-3 parallel sub-lines as separate breeding streams, swapping about 5-10% of breeders between them every few generations. Aim for Ne well above 100, meaning thousands of breeders contributing each generation from many cages, not a few clutches. Because BSF are native to Venezuela, a wild-caught cohort (egg traps baited with fermenting waste outdoors) could be brought up in quarantine for 1-2 generations, checked for mites and pathogens, and crossed into one sub-line first, as Hoffmann et al. and Silvaraju et al. recommend. Given Rhode et al.'s collapse by F5, never start a production line from a small wild sample alone.
- **Warning signs of decline to chart per generation:**
  - falling mean pupal mass
  - falling emergence %
  - falling g of eggs per kg of pupae
  - falling hatch %
  - sex ratio drifting away from about 50:50
  - longer larval development
  - more eggs laid outside the traps
  - A sustained drop of 20% or more across 2-3 consecutive generations, with conditions held constant, should trigger augmentation or a line cross.

### Gaps
- No published BSF estimate of the per-generation loss of fecundity or hatch as a function of Ne was found, so the thresholds above are management judgement, not literature values.
- There are no genetic data specifically from Venezuelan wild BSF populations. Kaya et al.'s north-western South America cluster is the closest.
- The Facchini et al. 2022 selection-response figures and the breeder fraction at large industrial producers were not retrieved.

## 8. Scheduling: continuous-production calendar and colonies to start per week

### Takeaway
Continuous supply comes from staggering cohorts rather than from one big cage. In the Eawag reference unit, a new mating cage is filled every 2 days from 3-4 dark cages. Mating cages are closed after 6 days and dark cages after about 3 weeks, while egg traps rotate daily over a 7-day hatching shower. Plan backwards from trays per week, using grams of eggs per tray, grams of eggs per kg of pupae, and a 20-30% safety margin.

### Cited Findings
- Eawag cycle:
  - Dark cages are loaded with 500 prepupae per container per day for 5 days (16 containers).
  - A love cage connects to 3-4 dark cages for 30 min each to collect same-age flies. Three love cages in the figure were filled at 2-day intervals. Each love cage holds 6,000-10,000 flies, 10 eggies and 2 attractant boxes, and is removed after 6 d.
  - Dark cages are removed after about 3 weeks.
  - Eggies are replaced per an egg-harvest schedule and hang on the hatchling shower for 7 d. Three new hatchling containers are made each day at full capacity.
  - 5-DOL are counted and dispatched daily. Nursery containers (15,000 5-DOL on 2 kg feed) run about 2.5 weeks to produce prepupae for the next dark cages.
  - The manual's appendices give full weekly work calendars for "every day" and "half capacity" operation.
  - [Dortmans et al. 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)
- Park et al. recommend introducing pupae into housing cages at regular intervals, because adult number is critical for continuous daily egg production. — [Park et al. 2016](https://koreascience.kr/article/JAKO201606776010895.pdf)
- Industry is split between continuous release (one large cage continually supplied with pupae) and batch cages (isolated cohorts, cleaned between cycles). Batch systems reduce pest spread (for example red mite) and allow each cage to be tuned; continuous systems reduce labour. — [Lemke et al. 2024](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.08.13.607807)
- Model-based control of temperature and light shortened a 14-d egg-production cycle by 1 d and raised egg output 9.4%. — [Kobelski et al. 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/bioengineering-and-biotechnology/articles/10.3389/fbioe.2024.1404776/full)

### Inferences
- **Worked example (planning arithmetic built from the cited benchmarks, not a published plan):**
  1. Targets: T = 200 larval trays per week, seeded at G = 0.5 g of eggs per tray (about 19,000 eggs, about 13,000-18,000 neonates). Egg demand E = T × G = **100 g/week**, plus a 25% buffer = **125 g/week**.
  2. Planning yield: Y = 20 g of eggs per kg of pupae per cage cycle (mid-range of Park et al.; use Tryento's own trailing average once available).
  3. Pupae to load: P = 125 / 20 ≈ **6.3 kg of pupae per week** (about 40,000 pupae at 6,500/kg).
  4. Cage cadence: 2 new insectarios per week (for example Monday and Thursday), each with about 3.1 kg of pupae collected over the previous 3-4 days. With about 10 d from loading to first emergence, then about 14-18 d of laying (Q5-6), each cage is productive for about 2.5 weeks. About 5-6 cages are therefore active at once, 2-3 of them at peak.
  5. Breeding line: 6.3 kg of pupae/week ÷ (0.75 larva→pupa × 0.8 hatch) ≈ 68,000 eggs ≈ **1.8 g of eggs/week** (about 1.5% of output) seeded into dedicated breeder trays. Those trays are harvested 5-6 weeks later to feed that week's cages. Pool the 1.8 g from at least 3-4 different cages and days (Q7).
  6. Egg flow: pull traps daily or every other day. Hang them over nursery trays or scrape and weigh them. Any egg surplus is best absorbed by holding prepupae at 14-15 °C rather than storing eggs (Q4).
- The same arithmetic can be written directly into the app: E = T × G; P = E × (1 + buffer) / Y_trailing; breeder eggs = P × pupae_per_kg / (hatch × survival × eggs_per_g). Each insectario record should keep kg of pupae, mean pupal mass, the date of first emergence, and planned vs actual attractant and closing dates. With those fields, g eggs/kg and g eggs/day can be compared on the same basis across cages.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed or manual source was found that gives a complete industrial breeding-unit calendar in trays per week or kg of eggs per day. Vendor figures (700-811 g of eggs/m²/month; 2-10 million pupae per unit) are marketing claims that cannot be checked.
- The FAO/ICIPE manuals and the Eawag 2021 second edition were not reviewed. Their schedules may differ from the 2017 edition used here.
