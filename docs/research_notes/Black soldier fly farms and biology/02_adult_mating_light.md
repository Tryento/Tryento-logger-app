# Black soldier fly (Hermetia illucens) adult mating behaviour and the environmental drivers of mating: light, temperature, humidity and cage design

Scope: adult reproduction only (emergence to oviposition). Built for a small indoor tropical-lowland farm with three Raspberry-Pi-controlled mating cages, each with two separately switched LED channels ("visible/warm" and "blue"), misting, a heater and fans. Climate target 30 °C ±1, 72% RH ±3. The current experiment: cage 1 has visible light 06:00–17:30 (11.5 h) with blue nested at 08:30–14:30 (6 h); cages 2 and 3 are controls. Researched September 2026.

Naming corrections for the report writer (verified from the sources below):
- The 2022 J. Econ. Entomol. study of adult density × photoperiod is by **Liu, Najar-Rodriguez, Morel & Minor (2022)**, not Hoc. **Hoc et al. (2019, PLOS ONE)** is the density, sex-ratio and photoperiod optimisation study.
- **Macavei et al. (2020, PLOS ONE)** is the light-source × adult-nutrition study, not the "four artificial light sources" paper, which is **Liu et al. (2020, J. Photochem. Photobiol. B)**.
- **Klüber et al. (2020, J. Econ. Entomol.)** is the LED colour-temperature study.

## 1. Adult mating behaviour: lekking, coupling, duration, time of day, remating, timing after emergence, lifespan

### Takeaway
BSF adults have a lek-like mating system. Males quest in the air, often in swarms near light, and defend perches. A male grabs a female in flight, the pair drops to a surface and couples end-to-end for about 20–40 min. Mating starts about 2 days after emergence and peaks on the first 1–3 days in the cage. Oviposition follows about 2 days later (day 4 onward). Recent 2021–2025 work overturns the old "females mate once" view: both sexes can mate several times. Captive, domesticated strains can mate without a lek, even as single pairs in a 3 L box under LED. Adults need no food but live longer with water (about 11 d) and longer again with sugar (about 27 d).

### Cited Findings
**Lekking and territoriality**
- Wild males use lekking sites where they wait for females and defend them against other males. A resting male seizes an intruding male, and after a brief descent the intruder retreats. First described near kudzu (*Pueraria montana*) plants — [Tomberlin & Sheppard 2001, Florida Entomologist 84:729](https://journals.flvc.org/flaent/article/view/75030); [Lemke et al. 2024, Insects 15:770](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11508282/)
- In a sunlit, tropical semi-outdoor setting (Bandung, Indonesia), males formed groups of 4–12. They displayed by "bending the tips of their abdomens" and "opening the hooks on their aedeagi while wing-fanning", and aggregation was more pronounced in larger cages — [Julita et al. 2020, J. Entomol. 17:117](https://scialert.net/fulltext/?doi=je.2020.117.127)
- In captivity (0.93 m³ pop-up cages, 90♂+90♀, 50 W LED, 12:12 L:D), males concentrated near the light in aerial swarms during the early hours. Females mostly occupied perches and joined swarms intermittently. Females used perches 1.42× more than males overall and 2.56× more in the morning. The authors take this as partial preservation of the lek system in cages — [Lemke, Rollison & Tomberlin 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11508282/)
- A 2025–2026 review describes adults forming "lek-like swarms in which both sexes can mate multiple times in a polygynandrous mating system", with protandry (males emerge several days before females) — [Lemke & Puniamoorthy 2026, Evolutionary Applications, doi 10.1111/eva.70249](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/eva.70249)
- Wild neotropical flies at three sites in San Ramón, Costa Rica, were found only within 7.5 m of oviposition sites, and those sites were highly female-skewed. No mating was seen within 30 m of oviposition sites because males were absent, which implies mating happens away from oviposition sites. This is a preprint and not peer reviewed — [Lemke, Smith et al. 2025, Preprints.org 202512.0942](https://www.preprints.org/frontend/manuscript/4f71bd49d228b7d74330e7dbf4973397/download_pub)
- It had been "a general belief that a single male is unable to attain successful courtship" (a lek was thought necessary). However, a single randomly chosen male and virgin female mated readily in a 3.0 L container (19.5×19.5×11.3 cm) at 28 °C and 70% RH, under two 50 W full-spectrum LED bars (Philips VerticalGrow120) placed 8 cm above the lid. The authors suggest domestication may have removed the lek requirement. This is a preprint (bioRxiv, March 2025) — [Jensen et al. 2025 preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.09.09.611978.full.pdf)

**Aerial encounter and coupling**
- A male grasps a flying female by crossing his hind legs around her abdomen, then the pair lands. Successful copulation happens only after landing. In the ethogram, grasping led to mounting in 65% of cases and mounting led to copulation in 72%. Only males wing-fan during courtship. Male–male encounters look similar, with both extruding the aedeagus. Conditions: 28 ± 3 °C, 60 ± 10% RH, a commercial "BSF breeding lamp" (SPR AG Tech BSF-4-100), and 45.5×24.5×35 cm Plexiglas cages — [Rebora et al. 2026, J. Comp. Physiol. A](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13396126/)
- Mating is 90% "reverse coupling" (end-to-end) and 10% mounting position — [Julita et al. 2020](https://scialert.net/fulltext/?doi=je.2020.117.127)
- Males chase, grab and press the female to the ground and mount her dorsally. Females arrive at leks about 2 days after males start holding territories — Tomberlin & Sheppard 2001 as summarised by [Rebora et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13396126/)
- Females can express mate preference based on male courtship behaviour — [Giunti et al. 2018, Entomologia Generalis 38:29–46](https://www.schweizerbart.de/papers/entomologia/detail/38/89814/Male_courtship_behaviour_and_potential_for_female_mate_choice_in_the_black_soldier_fly_Hermetia_illucens_L_Diptera_Stratiomyidae)
- In a sunlit greenhouse with 7×9×5 m of flight space, "aerial questing by males … precedes mating" — [Sheppard et al. 2002, J. Med. Entomol. 39:695](https://forensicentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2016/11/sheppard-colony-methods-2002.pdf)

**Mating duration**
- Mean 36 min (range 28–48 min) under tropical sunlight — [Julita et al. 2020](https://scialert.net/fulltext/?doi=je.2020.117.127)
- About 30 min — Giunti et al. 2018 and Julita et al. 2020 via [Rebora et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13396126/); about 30 min in 0.93 m³ greenhouse cages — [Lemke et al. 2024 bioRxiv / JIFF 2025 "Heterogeny in cages"](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.08.13.607807v1.full); about 35 min, with sperm transfer near the end — Manas et al. 2025 via [Lemke et al. 2026 preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.08.26.672371v3.full.pdf)
- For males mating consecutively, the median duration rose from about 20 min (1st mating) to about 40 min (3rd) and about 30 min (4th) — [Jensen et al. 2025 preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.09.09.611978.full.pdf)

**Time of day**
- Under sunlight (sunrise about 05:30; artificial-light treatments on 08:00–17:00, 28 °C, 60% RH, 1.8×1.2×1.5 m cages, about 1,000 flies), 85% of mating happened in the morning, peaking at 23 pairs at 10:00. Light was about 110 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ during the morning mating peak and about 200 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ at 13:00 — [Zhang et al. 2010, J. Insect Sci. 10:202](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3029228/)
- Time of day and light intensity together explained mating (r² = 0.49, P < 0.0001). Time of day, temperature and humidity explained oviposition (r² = 0.58) — [Tomberlin & Sheppard 2002, J. Entomol. Sci. 37:345](https://meridian.allenpress.com/jes/article/37/4/345/77716/Factors-Influencing-Mating-and-Oviposition-of)
- In the Indonesian tropics, mating occurred 06:00–18:00 and peaked 11:00–12:00. Oviposition peaked 13:00–14:00. Hourly light averaged 6,159 lx (range 437–13,618 lx), with the temperature and light peak at 13:00–14:00 — [Julita et al. 2020](https://scialert.net/fulltext/?doi=je.2020.117.127)
- Under summer sunlight with individually marked flies, matings were positively correlated with light intensity and humidity and negatively correlated with temperature — [Chiabotto et al. 2024, JIFF 10:2017](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Observation-of-mating-behavior-using-marked-flies-Chiabotto-Grosso/b0506b5830b6884f8e47a9554bf24786236d009d) (abstract-level only; coefficients not retrieved)

**Remating and number of matings**
- Older view: Tomberlin & Sheppard (2002) speculated that females mate and oviposit only once — [Heussler et al. 2018 summary](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0197896). Bertinetti et al. (2019) state BSF "typically mate only once" — [search summary of Bertinetti et al. 2019](https://academic.oup.com/jinsectscience/article/19/1/19/5315633)
- Newer view: with individually tagged adults of a breeding strain, females can be polygynandrous. Multi-mated females lay eggs several times, which increases egg production — [Muraro et al. 2024, JIFF 10:1423](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Mating-and-oviposition-of-a-breeding-strain-of-fly-Muraro-Lalanne/f069cf05550a772b0f847d69c9249ce18720a8ac)
- "Both male and female H. illucens can mate more than once" (Chiabotto 2024; Dickerson 2024; Hoffmann 2021; Jones & Tomberlin 2021; Muraro 2024) — [Jensen et al. 2025 preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.09.09.611978.full.pdf)
- Male polygyny results: one male with four virgin females for 4 h (28 °C, LED) gave 100% of males mating at least once, 96% twice, 88% three times and 52% four times. The median gap between the end of mating 1 and the start of mating 2 was 2 min 43 s. With a single female, only 80% of males mated (P = 0.018), and median latency was 54 min versus 7 min 17 s with four females. Of 63 clutches from polygynous males, 92% produced larvae, and there was no sign of sperm depletion. Female remating was "very limited" in this setup — [Jensen et al. 2025 preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.09.09.611978.full.pdf)
- Mating increases female longevity but decreases male longevity. Females normally live shorter than males. The authors infer nuptial gifts (seminal nutrients) — [Harjoko et al. 2023, Animal Behaviour 200:25–36](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S000334722300060X) (abstract-level)
- Females have morphologically complex sperm-storage organs. Sperm is stored in the spermathecae until oviposition, and stored sperm takes about 2 days to reach use — [Lemke & Puniamoorthy 2026](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/eva.70249); Manas et al. 2024 via [Lemke et al. 2026 preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.08.26.672371v3.full.pdf)

**Time from emergence to mating and oviposition**
- 69% of mating occurred 2 days after eclosion and 70% of oviposition 4 days after eclosion — [Tomberlin & Sheppard 2002](https://meridian.allenpress.com/jes/article/37/4/345/77716/Factors-Influencing-Mating-and-Oviposition-of); the same pattern (mating from day 2, oviposition from day 4) appears in [Sheppard et al. 2002](https://forensicentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2016/11/sheppard-colony-methods-2002.pdf)
- No mating was seen before day 2 and no oviposition before day 4. Daily egg output peaked on day 5, and 99.3% of eggs were laid between days 4 and 10 in the unfed control. Conditions: 28.1 °C, 70.8% RH, natural light 10:14 L:D, 120×87×75 cm net tent, 50♀+50♂ — [Bertinetti et al. 2019, J. Insect Sci.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6369865/)
- In the tropics, first mating came at 2 days post-emergence, mating continued over days 2–8, oviposition started at day 4 (2 days after mating) and ran 4–10 days — [Julita et al. 2020](https://scialert.net/fulltext/?doi=je.2020.117.127)
- Pre-oviposition period by study: 2–4 days under LED, fluorescent or halogen light at 27 °C, 16:8 — [Heussler et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0197896); about 4 days — [Hoc et al. 2019](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0216160); 2–6.6 days depending on treatment — [Macavei et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7182258/)
- In batch cages of 1–4-day-old flies (0.93 m³, 100♂+100♀, semi-outdoor greenhouse, Texas), mating peaked on day 1 after introduction with a small second hump on days 3–4. Trap oviposition peaked about 2 days later. Mating correlated best with trap oviposition lagged 2 days (97.5% "Pearson percent correlation") and with egg weight and hatch rate lagged 3 days. This is a preprint — [Lemke, Li & De Smet 2026 preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.08.26.672371v3.full.pdf)
- Adult age matters. Pairs in which both sexes were 2 days old had 2.2× more matings than 2-day males with 6-day females, and 2.7× more than 6-day pairs — [Dickerson et al. 2024, J. Econ. Entomol. 117:1225](https://academic.oup.com/jee/article/117/4/1225/7679433)
- Unmated females rarely lay. Of 247 isolated virgin females, 4% laid a clutch in their lifetime — [Jensen et al. 2025 preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.09.09.611978.full.pdf). Other studies report that females do lay unfertilised clutches (Dickerson 2024; Harjoko 2023; Tomberlin & Sheppard 2002, cited in the same preprint).

**Adult lifespan with and without water or sugar**
- At 28.1 °C and 70.8% RH, females lived 9.49 d unfed and 11.43 d with water only (agar 13.09 d, milk 14.58 d). Males lived 8.70 d unfed and 11.49 d with water. Oviposition lasted 6.4 d unfed, 7.0 d with water and 17.2 d with milk. Hatchability was 72–82% with no significant diet effect — [Bertinetti et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6369865/)
- At 27 °C, 70% RH and 16:8 with an LED mix, flies given sugar and water lived 27.1 d (females) and 27.1 d (males). With nothing, they lived 8.4 d (females) and 7.2 d (males). Per-female egg output was 27.1 mg with sugar and water, 17.2 mg with water only, and 0.10 mg under neon light with nothing — [Macavei et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7182258/)
- Adults need no food for successful reproduction: a colony was maintained 3 years on water only (misting) — [Sheppard et al. 2002](https://forensicentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2016/11/sheppard-colony-methods-2002.pdf). Adults follow a "hybrid capital-income breeding strategy (adults do not need to feed but can benefit from supplemental nutrition)" — [Lemke & Puniamoorthy 2026](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/eva.70249)
- Adult lifespan under tropical sunlight is 12–14 days, with males outliving females — [Julita et al. 2020](https://scialert.net/fulltext/?doi=je.2020.117.127)

### Inferences
- In a batch-loaded cage, the useful mating window is roughly days 1–4 after emergence and the egg window roughly days 4–10. A light treatment therefore has most of its effect in the first 3–4 days of each cohort. That is when to record mating counts. Egg-trap data should be read with a 2–3 day lag.
- Because domesticated strains mate readily in small containers, cage volume is less critical than it was for wild strains. Tryento's strain history is unknown. A strain recently derived from local Venezuelan wild flies may behave more like the wild neotropical flies and depend more on sunlight-like light. This is untested.
- Water-only adults live about 11 d, which is longer than the effective egg window. Water is the key input. Sugar extends life to about 27 d but mainly extends the tail of the oviposition period.

### Gaps
- Muraro et al. 2024 and Chiabotto et al. 2024: full-text numbers (share of females that remate, mean matings per individual) were not retrieved; only abstract-level claims are available.
- Harjoko et al. 2023: the actual longevity values for mated and virgin flies of each sex were not retrieved.
- Mating duration and time-of-day data under constant artificial light (no sun) at 30 °C are sparse. Most time-of-day data come from sunlight studies.

## 2. Light: why BSF need light to mate; source comparisons; visual sensitivity; intensity thresholds

### Takeaway
BSF mating is visually mediated: males find and chase females in flight in bright light. The compound eye has UV-, blue- and green-sensitive photoreceptors (sensitive over about 332–535 nm). Blue (about 440–450 nm) plus green (about 505–540 nm) light is what matters. Blue or UV alone is not enough (a 350–450 nm lamp produced zero mating). Every peer-reviewed comparison since 2016 finds LEDs at least as good as other lamps, with lower heat and energy. Tuned UV–blue–green LEDs do best on insemination and larvae produced. Intensity requirements conflict across studies. Early sunlight work put the minimum at about 63 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ with most mating above 200. Later LED work got fertile eggs at 40–59 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ and even about 700 lx. More light speeds up and increases mating (23% → 70% cumulative mating from 0.92 → 431 W m⁻²).

### Cited Findings
**Why light is required and visual sensitivity**
- "BSF mating is visually mediated, artificial illumination is critical for successful indoor breeding" — [Liu et al. 2020, J. Photochem. Photobiol. B 205:111815](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jphotobiol.2020.111815) (abstract)
- Electroretinograms show UV-, blue- and green-sensitive photoreceptors (trichromatic), tuned to 332–535 nm. The ventral eye is maximally sensitive to blue. The dorsal eye is sensitive to blue-green (504 nm). UV adds secondary peaks of 40% (ventral) and 20% (dorsal) — [Oonincx et al. 2016, J. Insect Physiol. 95:133](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27751886/) (via [search summary](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Photoreceptor-spectral-sensitivity-of-the-compound-Oonincx-Volk/b7800a660a13f57c2253ab257ab92212ee2a2226))
- Oonincx et al. designed a UV + blue + green LED system (spectrum 365–515 nm) and compared it with fluorescent tubes (300–650 nm). Both gave equal numbers of egg clutches, but LED gave significantly more larvae. Only 10♂+10♀ per cage were used (0.0007 flies cm⁻³). The oviposition peak came at day 16 under LED versus day 10 under fluorescent — [Oonincx et al. 2016 abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27751886/); cage details via [Heussler et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0197896)
- The recommended LED ratio UV:B:G = 1:1:3 "would optimize the visual pigment photo-equilibrium" (Oonincx et al.) — [Macavei et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7182258/)
- Males are drawn to UV-A/B and blue-green light (Zhang 2010; Oonincx 2016), and females temporarily join these aerial swarms to mate — [Lemke et al. 2026 preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.08.26.672371v3.full.pdf)

**Sunlight vs quartz-iodine vs rare-earth (Zhang et al. 2010)**
- Sunlight gave about 70 mating pairs per day. A 500 W quartz-iodine lamp (350–2,500 nm, 135 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) gave about 40 pairs per day, which is 61% of sunlight. A 450 W rare-earth lamp (350–450 nm, 160 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) gave **no mating**. Egg production under quartz-iodine was 61.9% of sunlight, but total egg masses were not significantly different. Development of the offspring was the same. Conditions: 28 °C, 60% RH, lamps on 08:00–17:00, 1.8×1.2×1.5 m cages, about 1,000 flies — [Zhang et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3029228/)
- The authors concluded that 450–700 nm is the most crucial range. They note that many Diptera have receptors peaking in blue to green (λmax 480–530 nm) — [Zhang et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3029228/)
- Earlier failures: a 40 W Sylvania Gro-Lux and a 430 W "Pro Ultralight" system produced no mating (Tomberlin & Sheppard 2002) — [Zhang et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3029228/)

**LED vs fluorescent vs halogen (Heussler et al. 2018)**
- Three lamps were set to the same 59 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹:
  - LED: 400–750 nm, peak 438 nm.
  - Fluorescent: 311–711 nm, peaks 430/541/612 nm.
  - Halogen: continuous from 300 nm.
- Conditions: 27 °C (halogen heated the cage to 29 °C), 60% RH, 16:8 L:D, 39×28×28 cm cages, 30–40 pairs, 4 replicates per lamp.
- All three produced fertile eggs, with no difference in pre-oviposition period (2–4 d), oviposition period (8–13 d), clutch mass (1.9–2.3 mg) or egg mass per female (61.2–79.9 mg). Half-life was longest under LED (males about 15+ d, females about 13 d) and shortest under halogen (about 7 d and about 4 d), which the authors attribute to heat.
- Only 10–22% of eggs were laid in the cardboard; the rest went on sponges, walls and lids.
- LEDs were recommended for their energy efficiency.
- Source: [Heussler et al. 2018, PLOS ONE 13:e0197896](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0197896)

**Four light sources and a tuned "BSFLED" (Liu et al. 2020)**
- Four sources were tested: halogen, white LED plus UV fluorescent, metal halide, and a purpose-built BSFLED matched to BSF spectral sensitivity. BSFLED was the most energy-efficient and gave the "highest mating success in terms of the percentage of inseminated females and fertile clutches". "Colony effects and light flickering effects on mating success were detected." The best mating came under BSFLED at 12L:12D — [Liu et al. 2020 abstract via Europe PMC](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jphotobiol.2020.111815); [search summary](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Mating-success-of-the-black-soldier-fly,-Hermetia-Liu-Najar-Rodriguez/d760bec9d41e7d089a1db744e8d5d01599118db1)

**LED mix vs white LED vs neon, crossed with adult diet (Macavei et al. 2020)**
- Three sources were tested at 16:8 L:D, 27 ± 0.5 °C, 70 ± 10% RH, in 32.5 cm BugDorm cages with 15♀+15♂:
  - Mixed LEDs 365/450/515 nm (1:1:3) at 700 lx.
  - Cold-white LED at 2,450 lx.
  - Fluorescent (Philips TL-D 36 W/840) at 3,700 lx.
- The LED mix gave the most eggs (27.1 mg per female with sugar and water). It was "9–10 times more effective than the other light sources" in eggs per lux.
- The authors conclude that nutrition drove output more than light, with light playing "an important secondary role".
- Hatchability was 43–56% across light × water treatments.
- Source: [Macavei et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7182258/)

**Colour temperature (Klüber et al. 2020)**
- Broad-spectrum LED panels at 3,000 K, 4,000 K and 6,500 K all produced mating and fertilised egg clusters. Total oviposition and oviposition period did not differ. Time to peak oviposition increased with colour temperature — [Klüber et al. 2020, J. Econ. Entomol. 113:2549](https://doi.org/10.1093/jee/toaa182) (abstract)

**Tuned LED, 2025 (Zhang et al. 2025, Insect Science)**
- A home-made 50 W LED was built with 365 nm, 450 nm and 520 nm chips plus 5,700 K and 7,500 K white chips in a 2:3:1:3:5 ratio, and compared with a commercial 50 W LED. Conditions: 12:12 (06:00–18:00), 27 ± 1 °C, 60–70% RH, lamp 10 cm above the cage.
- The tuned lamp attracted 71 mating pairs versus 29 and gave 78 g versus 33 g of eggs over 30 days, with higher hatch.
- Adding a gut-yeast attractant (*Trichosporon asahii*, 2,5-dimethylpyrazine) raised output to 235 g versus 60 g.
- Source: [Zhang et al. 2025, Insect Science 33:1581](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13441017/)

**Other LED successes and a caution**
- LED strips (380–780 nm) at 40 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ on the cage floor supported reproduction at 26–27 °C and 60% RH in 45 cm cages — [Hoc et al. 2019](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0216160)
- A domesticated Danish strain mated within minutes under full-spectrum horticultural LED bars 8 cm above small containers. The authors note that "adaptation to artificial lighting instead of sunlight could potentially have occurred during rearing in captivity" (citing Barrett et al. 2023) — [Jensen et al. 2025 preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.09.09.611978.full.pdf)
- Practitioner factsheet (Eawag, Switzerland; not peer reviewed):
  - A SolarRaptor 70 W metal-halide reptile spot lamp (about 127,000 lx and 210 µW cm⁻² UV at 60 cm) gave good mating and oviposition.
  - An ExoTerra Natural Light 26 W (510 lx) gave no mating.
  - Adding an ExoTerra UVB100 26 W (200 lx, 80 µW cm⁻² UVA+UVB) produced mating and eggs, but the eggs were infertile.
  - The authors attribute the failures to insufficient light quantity.
  - Source: [Eawag Sandec factsheet](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/schwerpunkte/swm/Practical_knowhow_on_BSF/bsf_factsheet_rearing_artifical_light.pdf)
- Commercial (vendor bias): EVO Conversion Systems' "JM GREEN" BSF breeding LEDs (50 W and 150 W) are described as "especially high in blue light", at about 3,000–6,000 lx — [Eawag factsheet](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/schwerpunkte/swm/Practical_knowhow_on_BSF/bsf_factsheet_rearing_artifical_light.pdf). The vendor claims 18.6 g of eggs in one day from a 1×1×2 m cage under 150 W, 84% hatch, and recommends 8:16 L:D. No spectra or intensities are given — [EVO blog](https://www.evoconsys.com/blog/black-soldier-fly-breeding-led)

**Intensity thresholds**
- "Minimum light intensity for mating is 63 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ with most mating occurring at over 200 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹" (J.K. Tomberlin and D.C. Sheppard, unpublished data, sunlit greenhouse) — [Sheppard et al. 2002](https://forensicentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2016/11/sheppard-colony-methods-2002.pdf)
- Schneider 2020 used a 100 W white LED chip with peaks near 440 and 540 nm, varied distance to set irradiance, and ran 14L:10D. Cumulative mating probability rose from about 23% at 0.92 W m⁻² to 70% at 431 W m⁻². Mean time to mating fell from 6.4 h to 3.4 h after lights-on. 92% of matings occurred in the first photophase after the sexes were combined at lights-on. The author concludes mating can be "dramatically increased" by light rich near 440 and/or 540 nm "with an irradiance that is an appreciable fraction of full sunlight intensity" — [Schneider 2020, JIFF 6:111](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/335432026_Effects_of_light_intensity_on_mating_of_the_black_soldier_fly_Hermetia_illucens_Diptera_Stratiomyidae) (abstract)
- Fertile eggs have been reported at much lower light than the 63 µmol "minimum":
  - 59 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ — [Heussler et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0197896)
  - 40 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ — [Hoc et al. 2019](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0216160)
  - 700 lx of UV-blue-green LED — [Macavei et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7182258/)
- This conflicts with the 63/200 µmol figures, which come from sunlit wild-derived colonies.
- The tropical sunlit peak of mating (11:00–12:00) occurred at a mean of about 6,000 lx, and hourly light ranged 437–13,618 lx — [Julita et al. 2020](https://scialert.net/fulltext/?doi=je.2020.117.127). (6,000 lx is far below direct sun; the site was screened and likely shaded.)

### Inferences
- **Blue alone is not a mating light.** The only pure short-wave source tested (Zhang's 350–450 nm rare-earth lamp at 160 µmol) gave zero mating. Every source that worked had substantial green (about 500–550 nm). For Tryento, the "visible/warm" channel is doing essential work, not just the blue channel. Warm-white LEDs (about 3,000 K) have a blue pump near 450 nm and a broad phosphor band that includes green, and Klüber found 3,000 K panels sufficient. An effectively blue-only period (for example, if the warm channel were ever off while blue is on) would be expected to support little mating.
- **Measure what the flies see.** Illuminance (lux) is weighted by human photopic sensitivity (peak 555 nm). It under-counts 440 nm and UV and over-counts yellow-red, so lux is a poor index of BSF-effective light. As an approximation, daylight is about 54 lx per µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ of PAR, and white LEDs are commonly in the 60–80 lx per µmol range (general photometry, spectrum-dependent, not a BSF source). On that basis 40–60 µmol corresponds very roughly to 2,500–4,500 lx of white LED. Tryento should measure each channel at cage-floor and mid-cage height with a PAR quantum meter (or a spectrometer), not only a lux meter, and log it per cage.
- **Flicker is a real confounder.** Liu et al. 2020 detected flicker effects on mating success. If the Raspberry Pi dims LEDs by PWM, or cheap drivers ripple at mains frequency, the treatment and control cages may differ in flicker as well as spectrum. Use constant-current drivers or high-frequency (≥ several kHz) PWM, identical across cages, or switch fully on and off only.
- Heat from lamps shortens adult life (Heussler's halogen result). The LED channels should be checked for local heating of the cage top when both channels are on, because the DHT22s may not sit in the lamp's heat plume.

### Gaps
- Liu et al. 2020 full text (the per-source insemination percentages, the BSFLED spectrum and intensity, the photoperiods tested) was paywalled. Only the abstract was retrieved.
- Oonincx et al. 2016 LED intensities and cage conditions were not retrieved beyond abstract-level data.
- Schneider 2020's spectral split (µmol by band) was not retrieved. The results are in W m⁻² of broadband white LED, which is not directly comparable to µmol PAR.
- No study isolated green-only versus blue-plus-green versus UV-plus-blue-plus-green at equal photon flux with fertility as the endpoint. The relative contribution of each band is inferred from photoreceptor data and multi-band lamp comparisons.
- The role of UV is unresolved. Oonincx and Macavei used UV LEDs and did well, and the Eawag UVB lamp alone gave infertile eggs. No study tested UV-A presence versus absence at otherwise equal blue and green.

## 3. Photoperiod, "is long blue harmful?", timing of light within the day, and ramps (with evaluation of Tryento's cage-1 design)

### Takeaway
Across 6–18 h photoperiods, BSF reproduction is fairly insensitive to day length. Liu et al. 2022 found no simple effect of 8, 12 or 16 h on per-female output, mating success, hatch or survival. Longer days raised neonate output only at high density, and at a cost in neonates per watt. Hoc et al. 2019 found 6 h efficient. A model fitted to Hoc's data predicts −19% eggs at 6 h and −53% at 2 h relative to 16 h. There is **no BSF-specific evidence** that long blue exposure is harmful. The claim rests on *Drosophila* studies of 12 h/day blue LED over weeks of adult life. BSF studies running blue-rich LEDs 16 h/day found LED gave the *longest* adult half-life of the sources tested. Mating concentrates in the morning, about 2–6 h after lights-on, and happens faster under brighter light. No BSF study was found on dawn or dusk ramps.

### Cited Findings
**Photoperiod effects**
- Liu et al. 2022 tested density (10, 25 or 50 pairs per 30×30×30 cm cage, i.e. 370, 926 or 1,852 pairs m⁻³) against light regime (8:16, 12:12, 16:8) under the BSFLED. Reproductive output rose with density but "was not affected by light regimes per se". At the highest density, more than 20,000 neonates were produced per cage in 10 d. There, longer photoperiod increased neonates but decreased neonates per watt. Mating success, per-female output, hatch rate, survival and oviposition timing were unaffected by light as a simple effect. Density × light interactions affected the first oviposition peak, per-female output and survival — [Liu et al. 2022, J. Econ. Entomol. 115:37–45](https://doi.org/10.1093/jee/toab225) (abstract via Europe PMC); [bioone](https://bioone.org/journals/journal-of-economic-entomology/volume-115/issue-1/toab225/Reproduction-of-Black-Soldier-Fly-Diptera--Stratiomyidae-Under-Different/10.1093/jee/toab225.short)
- Hoc et al. 2019 tested 2, 6, 12 and 18 h of LED light daily (40 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹). They recommend 6:18 L:D with a female-dominant population at ≥ 6,500 ind m⁻³. Longer light shortened the oviposition period and brought the oviposition peak forward (day 3–5 depending on light duration) — [Hoc et al. 2019](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0216160)
- A model fitted to Hoc et al. (2019) predicts that, relative to 16 h/day, 6 h cuts egg production by 19% and 2 h by 53%. Dynamic optimisation (less light while flies are immature, more during the mating phase) gave "9.4% more egg mass in 1 day less, with −41% light input and +3.7% heat input" — [Kobelski et al. 2024, Front. Bioeng. Biotechnol.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11150853/)
- The best mating in Liu et al. 2020 was under BSFLED at 12L:12D — [search summary of Liu et al. 2020](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Mating-success-of-the-black-soldier-fly,-Hermetia-Liu-Najar-Rodriguez/d760bec9d41e7d089a1db744e8d5d01599118db1)
- Under sunlight, mating and oviposition occurred in every month, "even during the shortest days of winter if the sun was not obscured by clouds" (Tifton, Georgia, about 31°N) — [Sheppard et al. 2002](https://forensicentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2016/11/sheppard-colony-methods-2002.pdf). Reproduction was also normal under natural 10:14 L:D in Italy — [Bertinetti et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6369865/)
- Holmes et al. 2017 tested 0, 8 and 12 h light on *immature* development. In darkness, development to adult needed 39% more degree-hours than 8 h and 38% more than 12 h, and larval survival was 72% versus 96–97%. This bears on larvae and pupae, not adult mating — [Holmes, VanLaerhoven & Tomberlin 2017, Environ. Entomol.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29069348)
- Vendor guidance recommends 8:16 L:D (commercial source, vested interest) — [EVO Conversion Systems blog](https://www.evoconsys.com/blog/black-soldier-fly-breeding-led)

**Is long blue exposure harmful?**
- *Drosophila*: daily cycles of 12 h blue LED and 12 h dark significantly reduced longevity compared with constant darkness or white light with blue blocked. The flies showed retinal damage, brain neurodegeneration and impaired locomotion, and the brain effects occurred even in genetically eyeless flies — [Nash et al. 2019, npj Aging Mech. Dis.](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41514-019-0038-6)
- *Drosophila*: susceptibility to blue light is strongly age-dependent, with larger effects on survival and neurodegeneration in old flies than young flies — [npj Aging 2022, "Age-dependent effects of blue light exposure…"](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41514-022-00092-z)
- BSF: Heussler et al. 2018 ran a blue-peaked LED (peak 438 nm, 59 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) **16 h per day**. It gave the longest male and female half-life of the three lamps, significantly longer than fluorescent or halogen — [Heussler et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0197896)
- BSF: Macavei et al. 2020 ran a UV + 450 nm blue + 515 nm green LED mix **16 h per day**. With sugar and water, adults lived about 27 d, the longest of the treatments — [Macavei et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7182258/)
- No peer-reviewed BSF study was found that tested blue duration, or blue versus no-blue at equal photon flux, on adult longevity or fertility. The one search result asserting that "continuous 24 h light is not optimal" could not be traced to a primary study — [search result pointing to an academia.edu review](https://www.academia.edu/108276105/Influences_of_artificial_light_on_mating_of_black_soldier_fly_Hermetia_illucens_a_review) (unverified)

**Timing of light within the day**
- Under natural light, mating peaks in the morning:
  - 10:00 with 85% of mating before noon, about 4.5 h after sunrise — [Zhang et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3029228/)
  - 11:00–12:00 in the Indonesian tropics — [Julita et al. 2020](https://scialert.net/fulltext/?doi=je.2020.117.127)
  - time of day plus intensity explains mating — [Tomberlin & Sheppard 2002](https://meridian.allenpress.com/jes/article/37/4/345/77716/Factors-Influencing-Mating-and-Oviposition-of)
- Under artificial light switched on abruptly, mean latency to mating was 3.4–6.4 h after lights-on depending on irradiance (brighter is faster) — [Schneider 2020](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/335432026_Effects_of_light_intensity_on_mating_of_the_black_soldier_fly_Hermetia_illucens_Diptera_Stratiomyidae). Under close LED, the median was 7 min (1♂:4♀) to 54 min (1♂:1♀) after lights-on — [Jensen et al. 2025 preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.09.09.611978.full.pdf)
- In 0.93 m³ cages under 12:12 LED (06:00–18:00), perching was female-biased most strongly in the early morning (07:00 totals: 501 female versus 208 male perching events) and declined through the day. Males swarmed near the light in the early hours — [Lemke et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11508282/)
- Oviposition peaks in the early afternoon (13:00–14:00) under tropical sun — [Julita et al. 2020](https://scialert.net/fulltext/?doi=je.2020.117.127). Oviposition correlates with time of day, temperature and humidity — [Tomberlin & Sheppard 2002](https://meridian.allenpress.com/jes/article/37/4/345/77716/Factors-Influencing-Mating-and-Oviposition-of)

**Ramps**
- No BSF study was found that compares gradual dawn or dusk ramps with on/off switching. (Searched; none located.)

### Inferences — evaluation of Tryento's cage-1 design (visible 06:00–17:30, blue 08:30–14:30)
- **Biological plausibility of the schedule is good.** Blue from 08:30 to 14:30 covers every reported mating peak (10:00, 11:00–12:00). Visible light from 06:00 gives early-morning cues and a 2.5 h "pre-period" before blue. Afternoon visible light supports oviposition (peak 13:00–14:00). If blue makes the cage substantially brighter in BSF-effective photons, mating latency should shorten after 08:30 (Schneider's intensity–latency relation).
- **The premise that blue must be capped at 6 h is not supported by BSF evidence.** It is an extrapolation from *Drosophila*, whose adults live weeks to months. There the blue effect was cumulative and worst in old flies. BSF adults live about 8–27 d and do essentially all their mating in days 1–4. Two BSF studies ran blue-rich LEDs 16 h/day and recorded the *best* longevity among light sources. The 6 h cap is a reasonable precaution, but it should be framed as a hypothesis being tested, not an established fact.
- **The main design problem is confounding, not biology.** Cage 1 differs from the controls in several ways at once:
  - total photoperiod (11.5 h versus the control's 8 h or 14 h);
  - blue duration (6 h versus 8 or 14 h);
  - timing (visible light now starts at 06:00, whereas the old windows were centred on noon);
  - daily light integral;
  - possibly heat load.
  
  Any difference cannot be attributed to the "blue cap" alone. With one treatment cage, cage identity (position, fan airflow, sensor bias, light leaks) is fully confounded with treatment.
- **Expected effect sizes are small.** Photoperiod changes within 6–16 h produced no significant per-female difference (Liu 2022) or at most about 19% (Hoc-derived model). Differences between Tryento's schedules may therefore be ≤ 20%. That is below what one or two cycles in one cage can detect (see Section 6).
- **A cleaner design:**
  - Hold the visible schedule identical in all cages.
  - Vary only blue duration (for example 6 h nested versus 11.5 h co-extensive with visible).
  - Rotate treatments among cages across successive pupae batches (crossover or Latin square), splitting each pupae batch across cages.
  - Include at least one "no-blue" arm if the question is "is blue needed at all".
- Because mating needs about 2+ h of light before it starts (latency) and peaks in the morning, removing blue before about 08:00 costs little. Removing it during 09:00–13:00 is where losses would be expected if blue contributes much of the effective photon flux.

### Gaps
- No BSF data on 20–24 h photoperiods for adults, on blue-duration effects on adult longevity or fertility, or on dawn/dusk ramps.
- Liu 2022's full per-photoperiod numbers (µmol, egg mass per female per regime) were not retrieved.
- Holmes 2017 reportedly also measured adult longevity across day lengths (a ResearchGate figure title mentions it), but the values were not retrieved.
- Blue irradiance in the *Drosophila* studies was not retrieved, so it cannot be compared with Tryento's blue channel.

## 4. Temperature and humidity for adults (and evaluation of 30 °C ±1 / 72% RH ±3)

### Takeaway
Adult reproduction works over roughly 20–35 °C (lab) and 24–40 °C (greenhouse observations). Lifetime fecundity peaks near 28–30 °C. Longevity is highest in the mid-20s and falls sharply above 35 °C. So **30 °C is at the fecundity optimum**. 32–33 °C, which Tryento tried earlier, trades adult lifespan and egg output for a slightly shorter pre-oviposition period. Humidity of 60–80% is standard, and 30–90% has supported mating. What matters most is free water (misting or wet sponges) and dry egg traps. There is no evidence that ±1 °C or ±3% RH precision improves output. Successful tropical sunlit systems cycle about 23–31 °C and 73–91% RH daily.

### Cited Findings
- Chia et al. 2018 tested adults at nine constant temperatures from 10 to 42 °C, with 70 ± 2% RH, 12:12 L:D, pairs in 15×15×10 cm cages and sugar water:
  - Lifetime fecundity was 516 eggs (diet 1) and 475 eggs (diet 2) per female at 30 °C. It was significantly lower at 15 °C and 37 °C, and reproduction was confined to about 20–35 °C.
  - Pre-oviposition was 16 d at 20 °C and 5 d at 35 °C.
  - Egg hatch was highest at 30 °C (80%) and 35 °C (75%), and <12% at 15, 37 and 40 °C.
  - Above 35 °C, female longevity was 4–5× shorter than at 15 °C.
  - Source: [Chia et al. 2018, PLOS ONE 13:e0206097](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0206097)
  - Note: the page summary also stated "longest lifespan at 30 °C", which conflicts with the model fit below. Treat the longevity optimum as uncertain.
- A curve fit to Chia et al. 2018 and Nakamura et al. 2016 puts the fecundity optimum at 28.6 °C, −23% at 35 °C and −54% at 20 °C. Longevity peaks at 24 °C and falls 29% (12.4 → 8.8 d) at 35 °C. Pre-oviposition is 4.3 d at 25 °C, 3.1 d at 30 °C and 2.46 d at 35 °C (Bertinetti 2019; Binsin 2023). The authors say RH should be "sufficiently high and at constant level" and found no gain from dynamic humidity control — [Kobelski et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11150853/)
- In a sunlit greenhouse colony, adults "typically mated and oviposited at temperatures of 24 °C up to 40 °C or more", and 30–90% RH supported mating and oviposition. 99.6% of field oviposition occurred at 27.5–37.5 °C (Booth & Sheppard 1984) — [Sheppard et al. 2002](https://forensicentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2016/11/sheppard-colony-methods-2002.pdf)
- Monthly colony data at Tifton (greenhouse, ambient day length):
  - Dec: 25.2 °C, 55.7% RH, 11.1 egg-filled flutes/day
  - Jan: 22.4 °C, 53.1% RH, 18.8 flutes/day
  - Feb: 24.2 °C, 52.4% RH, 39.6 flutes/day
  - Mar: 27.8 °C, 61.0% RH, 42.7 flutes/day
  - May–June (temperature not recorded): 66.7–71.9 flutes/day
  - Flute counts also track adult emergence (124–494 per day), so they are not a pure climate effect.
  - Source: [Sheppard et al. 2002 Table 1](https://forensicentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2016/11/sheppard-colony-methods-2002.pdf)
- "Optimal conditions for mating … include moderately high temperatures between 26 to 29 °C and a high relative humidity of 65 to 85%" (citing Giunti 2018; Julita 2020) — [Jensen et al. 2025 preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.09.09.611978.full.pdf)
- Tropical sunlit reproduction (Indonesia, 679–770 m altitude) worked at a daily mean of 27.9 °C (range 22.9–30.5 °C) and 80.2% RH (range 73.1–91.1%), with 569–650 eggs per female — [Julita et al. 2020](https://scialert.net/fulltext/?doi=je.2020.117.127)
- Under summer sunlight, matings were negatively correlated with temperature and positively with humidity — [Chiabotto et al. 2024](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Observation-of-mating-behavior-using-marked-flies-Chiabotto-Grosso/b0506b5830b6884f8e47a9554bf24786236d009d)
- Holmes et al. 2012 tested egg eclosion at 25, 40, 50, 60 and 70% RH and adult emergence at 25, 40 and 70% RH. Eclosion and emergence success increased with RH, and 25% RH gave higher egg mortality than 70% — [Holmes, VanLaerhoven & Tomberlin 2012, Environ. Entomol. 41:971](https://academic.oup.com/ee/article/41/4/971/448259) (abstract; adult longevity values not retrieved)
- A relative humidity around 70% "guarantees successful egg-laying and increases the longevity of adult flies" — search summary of rearing literature, primary source not identified ([Hoc et al. 2019 context](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0216160); unverified attribution)
- Water provision methods:
  - Misting on screen and artificial plants twice daily (10:00–11:00 and 15:00–16:00) with 0.8 gal/h fog nozzles at 100 psi. Adults drank droplets and "made no effort to avoid" dew accumulating on them — [Sheppard et al. 2002](https://forensicentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2016/11/sheppard-colony-methods-2002.pdf)
  - A moist sponge in a water-filled cylinder — [Hoc et al. 2019](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0216160)
  - Water-soaked cotton — [Macavei et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7182258/)
  - About 100 mL of RO water at dawn, noon and dusk — [Lemke et al. 2024 bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.08.13.607807v1.full)
- Water adds about 2–3 d of adult life over nothing (≈9.5 → 11.4 d in females) — [Bertinetti et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6369865/). Water plus sugar gives about 27 d versus about 8 d with nothing — [Macavei et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7182258/)
- Wet oviposition substrates are less attractive to ovipositing females, and females lay in *dry* crevices above moist decomposing matter (Booth & Sheppard 1984) — [Sheppard et al. 2002](https://forensicentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2016/11/sheppard-colony-methods-2002.pdf)
- Lamp heat: a halogen lamp raised the cage from 27 to 29 °C and roughly halved adult half-life — [Heussler et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0197896)
- In a greenhouse, humidity gradients influenced where females oviposited, and heat on traps pushed oviposition into cage material — [Lemke et al. 2024 bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.08.13.607807v1.full); [Lemke et al. 2026 preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.08.26.672371v3.full.pdf)

### Inferences
- **30 °C is well supported as a target.** It sits at the fecundity and hatch optimum (Chia) and just above the 28.6 °C curve optimum. Tryento's earlier 32 °C and 33 °C runs sit on the declining side toward 35 °C, where fecundity is about −23% and longevity about −29%. They would be expected to do worse unless the pre-oviposition speed-up matters operationally. 28 °C is also near-optimal and costs less heater energy. In the Venezuelan lowlands, ambient conditions may rarely require heating.
- **The ±1 °C band is tighter than the biology requires.** Reproduction is robust across about 24–35 °C, and successful tropical systems cycle 8 °C per day. Chasing ±1 °C with a heater and fans may waste energy and create on/off micro-oscillations. The more important control is to avoid excursions above about 34–35 °C, especially at cage tops near lamps, in the early afternoon.
- **72% ±3 RH is within the supported range (60–85%).** The ±3% band is unnecessarily tight. Misting mainly matters for water droplets the flies can drink. Chiabotto's positive humidity–mating correlation and Julita's 80% mean suggest the upper part of the range (70–85%) is not harmful. Keep egg traps dry: position them away from mist plumes, or mist on a schedule that avoids wetting the traps.
- **DHT22 caveats.** Accuracy is about ±0.5 °C and ±2–5% RH per the manufacturer's typical spec (not a BSF source), and it drifts at sustained high RH. A ±3% RH control band is at or below sensor uncertainty. Cross-check sensors against each other and a reference hygrometer periodically, and log the sensor pair difference as a data-quality flag.

### Gaps
- Holmes 2012 adult longevity by RH was not retrieved.
- No study was found that directly compares constant versus diel-cycling temperature for adult mating output.
- Addeo et al. 2022 (adult thermal preference by age, size and sex, JIFF 8:129) is cited in the literature but was not retrieved.
- Chia 2018 longevity by temperature: extraction conflicted (30 °C "longest" versus the model fit's 24 °C optimum). Verify against the paper's Table.

## 5. Cage design: volume, shape, density, sex ratio, resting surfaces, mesh, emergence, removal of dead adults; eggs per female and per kg pupae

### Takeaway
Small cages work for domesticated strains under LED: 27–45 cm cubes, and even 3 L boxes for single pairs. Output per cage rises with density up to at least 6,500–8,500 flies m⁻³ in 91 L cages and 1,852 pairs m⁻³ in 27 L cages. Commercial practice cites about 7,000–40,000 flies m⁻³. Same-age cohorts produce far more fertile eggs than mixed-age cages. Female-biased sex ratios raise total eggs, while male-biased ratios raise eggs per female and fertility. Perches do not change fitness but do add space. Typical benchmarks are about 400–650 eggs per female, or about 10–28 mg of eggs per female in cage studies, at about 0.025–0.028 mg per egg. No peer-reviewed "eggs per kg of pupae" benchmark was found.

### Cited Findings
**Cage size and density**
- Historical sunlit cages:
  - 2×2×4 m (16 m³) Lumite screen cage (7.1×5.5 mesh per cm) inside a 7×9×5 m greenhouse — [Sheppard et al. 2002](https://forensicentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2016/11/sheppard-colony-methods-2002.pdf)
  - Earlier (Tingle 1975): mating occurred in 3×6.1×1.8 m and 0.76×1.14×1.37 m outdoor cages, but not in 53×91×53 cm or 38×46×38 cm cages (via Sheppard 2002)
- Modern small cages that worked under artificial light:
  - 39×28×28 cm with 30–40 pairs — [Heussler et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0197896)
  - 27×27×27 cm with 50 pairs (0.0050 flies cm⁻³, Nakamura 2016); 10 pairs in Oonincx 2016 — via [Heussler et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0197896)
  - 32.5 cm cube with 15 pairs — [Macavei et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7182258/)
  - 30 cm cube with 10–50 pairs — [Liu et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/jee/toab225)
  - 3 L box with 1–5 flies — [Jensen et al. 2025 preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.09.09.611978.full.pdf)
- Hoc et al. 2019 used 45×45×45 cm nylon cages (91 L) at 500–8,500 ind m⁻³ (45–775 flies per cage). Egg output increased with density, and they recommend ≥ 6,500 ind m⁻³. The maximum was 9.0 ± 0.2 g eggs per cage at 8,500 ind m⁻³ with a female-dominant population — [Hoc et al. 2019](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0216160)
- Liu et al. 2022: output increased from 370 to 1,852 pairs m⁻³, giving more than 20,000 neonates per 27 L cage in 10 d at the top density — [Liu et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/jee/toab225)
- A patent claims sustained high egg production at about 7,000–40,000 flies m⁻³ (patent, commercial interest) — [US patent 12213467 via search summary](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/12213467)
- Egg numbers and total egg weights increased with adult density (4, 8 and 10 kg pupae per nylon cage) independently of cage dimensions — [search summary of "Effect of adult population density on egg production in the BSF"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/314857399_Effect_of_adult_population_density_on_egg_production_in_the_black_soldier_fly_Hermetia_illucens_Diptera_Stratiomyidae) (abstract-level; cage volumes and yields not retrieved)
- Industrial breeding cages cited at 2.88–59.42 m³ — [Lemke et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11508282/)

**Sex ratio**
- Male-dominant (M/F 1.80): 22.5 ± 0.8 mg eggs per female and 96.5% hatch. Female-dominant (M/F 0.64): 16.6 ± 1.0 mg per female and 93.5% hatch, but higher total yield per cage. Recommendation: a female-dominant progenitor population — [Hoc et al. 2019](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0216160)
- Males mated faster and more often when given four females rather than one (100% versus 80% mated; median latency 7 versus 54 min) — [Jensen et al. 2025 preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.09.09.611978.full.pdf)
- Protandry: males emerge before females — [Lemke & Puniamoorthy 2026](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/eva.70249). In Hoc et al.'s transport-tank sex-ratio work, the sex ratio shifted with prepupal harvest mass, with balance (0.5) reached at 700 g of self-harvested prepupae — [Hoc et al. 2019](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0216160)

**Cohort age structure and attractant timing (cage management)**
- Same-age cohorts (males 2–4 d, females 1–3 d) versus mixed ages (1–16 d), in 0.84×0.84×1.33 m cages with 100♂+100♀:
  - same-age cohorts mated 2.32× more;
  - laid 6.58× more eggs in traps;
  - produced eggs 1.17× more fertile.
- Delaying the oviposition attractant until 1 day after peak mating raised trap egg weight 1.25× in same-age cages. Hatch was about 60% versus 50%.
- Recommendations: keep the age spread to ≤ 4 d, release flies within 1–2 d of emergence, and remove older cohorts before adding new batches.
- Caveats: the extracted cage conditions (reported as "16:10 L:D" and "27% RH") look garbled, so verify them. The attractant was 500 g Gainesville diet with about 1,000 larvae.
- Source: [Lemke, Li, Dickerson et al. 2024 bioRxiv, published JIFF 2025, doi 10.1163/23524588-bja10275](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.08.13.607807v1.full)
- The industrial process places one or several batches of pupae directly in the cage, or adjacent to it, to emerge. Continuous additions produce mixed-age cages, especially since post-reproductive adults can live several weeks when fed — [Lemke et al. 2026 preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.08.26.672371v3.full.pdf)

**Resting surfaces, colour, mesh, materials**
- Adding artificial *Monstera* leaves (0–0.34 m² in 0.93 m³) did not change mating, oviposition, egg weight or hatch (all p > 0.5). Perching is female-biased. The model says 1 m² of extra surface accommodates about 45.6 more flies at equal perching rates — [Lemke et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11508282/)
- Of four love-cage configurations (internal surface-to-volume 3.33, 3.99, 5.11 and 7.17 m⁻¹), 3.99 m⁻¹ gave significantly more eggs, attributed to less male competition for lek space. White surfaces beat green or mixed, attributed to light reflection aiding mate detection — [“Biomimicry in love-cage design” 2025](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/393616826_Biomimicry_in_love-cage_design_boosting_black_soldier_fly_mass_production) (abstract-level)
- Examples of materials and fixtures:
  - Lumite screen at 7.1×5.5 mesh per cm, two plastic ivy "plants" (50 cm globes) on blocks under fog nozzles — [Sheppard et al. 2002](https://forensicentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2016/11/sheppard-colony-methods-2002.pdf)
  - 1.5 mm mesh screen cages (60 cm cube; 3×3×3 and 4×4×4 m screen houses) — [Julita et al. 2020](https://scialert.net/fulltext/?doi=je.2020.117.127)
  - BugDorm nylon cages — [Macavei et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7182258/)
- Attachment to surfaces: female cuticle is hydrophobic (contact angle 139°). Females generated more friction on hydrophilic glass (49.7 versus 26.1 mN mm⁻² in males) — [Rebora et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13396126/)

**Emergence and pupae introduction**
- Prepupae were left in drying media under mosquito netting. Containers were moved into the greenhouse cage and uncovered once adult emergence began. Adults emerged 10–14 d after pupation — [Sheppard et al. 2002](https://forensicentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2016/11/sheppard-colony-methods-2002.pdf)
- Adults in holding cages before release had delayed mating, which confounded results — [Lemke et al. 2024 bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.08.13.607807v1.full)
- Automated counting of adults entering breeding cages (FlyCount, neuromorphic sensor) has been proposed — [Lemke et al. 2026 preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.08.26.672371v3.full.pdf)

**Oviposition substrates (affects egg capture)**
- Corrugated cardboard with 3 flutes per cm, 2.5×5 cm blocks, taped 2–5 cm above wet Gainesville diet in a 5 L bucket. More than 100 egg-filled flutes collected in one day, with 603–689 eggs per flute — [Sheppard et al. 2002](https://forensicentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2016/11/sheppard-colony-methods-2002.pdf)
- Only 10–22% of eggs landed in the cardboard, and the rest on sponges, walls and lids — [Heussler et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0197896)
- Three pine planks (12×4.5×4 cm) over 250 g of 7-day fermented carrot "greatly improves egg collection and avoids dispersion" — [Hoc et al. 2019](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0216160)
- A *Trichosporon asahii* culture producing 2,5-dimethylpyrazine (100 µg mL⁻¹) induced aggregative oviposition — [Zhang et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13441017/)
- Fatty acids from oviposition systems guide females to egg-deposition sites — [Klüber et al. 2024, Insect Science](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1744-7917.13287) (title-level)

**Benchmarks: eggs per female, per clutch, per cage**
- Eggs per female:
  - 569–650 (tropics, sunlight) — [Julita et al. 2020](https://scialert.net/fulltext/?doi=je.2020.117.127)
  - 475–516 lifetime at 30 °C — [Chia et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0206097)
  - 348 mg per "female per cycle" at 28 °C and 16 h in the model paper. This figure is implausible per female (it would be about 14,000 eggs) and is probably a per-cage or per-group figure — [Kobelski et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11150853/)
- Eggs per clutch or mass:
  - 603–689 per flute — [Sheppard et al. 2002](https://forensicentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2016/11/sheppard-colony-methods-2002.pdf)
  - 408–493 per mass (10.8–13.1 mg) — [Macavei et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7182258/)
  - 412–478 per mass — [Bertinetti et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6369865/)
- Egg mass per female:
  - 16.6–22.5 mg — [Hoc et al. 2019](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0216160)
  - 6.9–27.1 mg — [Macavei et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7182258/)
  - Heussler reported 61.2–79.9 mg per female, but with clutch masses of only 1.9–2.3 mg. These units differ from the other studies and look inconsistent, so verify them — [Heussler et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0197896)
- Single egg mass: 0.025 mg — [Hoc et al. 2019](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0216160); 0.0256 mg — [Bertinetti et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6369865/); 0.028 mg — [Macavei et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7182258/)
- Commercial claim: 1 g of eggs gives 20,000–35,000 larvae (vendor source) — [Kimmy Farm](https://kimmyfarm.com/en/how-many-grams-of-bsf-larvea-after-growing)
- Per cage: 9.0 g of eggs from a 91 L cage at 8,500 ind m⁻³ — [Hoc et al. 2019](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0216160); more than 20,000 neonates per 27 L cage per 10 d — [Liu et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/jee/toab225); 78 g of eggs over 30 d with a tuned LED — [Zhang et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13441017/)
- Vendor claims (commercial bias): 18.6 g eggs per day from a 1×1×2 m cage under a 150 W LED — [EVO blog](https://www.evoconsys.com/blog/black-soldier-fly-breeding-led)

### Inferences
- At 0.025–0.028 mg per egg, 1 g of eggs is about 36,000–40,000 eggs. The vendor's 20,000–35,000 larvae per g implies about 55–95% hatch.
- A kg-of-pupae benchmark can be built locally as: (pupae per kg) × (emergence fraction) × (female fraction) × (fraction of females that oviposit) × (eggs per ovipositing female) × (hatch fraction). Tryento should measure each term itself rather than rely on a published figure (see Gaps).
- **Cohort management may matter more than lighting for Tryento's output.** Loading one pupae batch per cage per cycle and clearing old adults gave 2–6× effects (Lemke), much larger than any photoperiod effect reported. If the cages are topped up continuously, the light experiment will be confounded by age structure, so each experimental cycle should start with a single-batch, single-age cohort.
- White interior surfaces and a moderate amount of perch area are cheap, evidence-backed tweaks. Keep them identical across cages during the light experiment.

### Gaps
- **Eggs per kg of pupae**: no peer-reviewed figure was found. The "4, 8, 10 kg pupae per cage" density study (ResearchGate) had no accessible yields.
- **Dead-adult removal**: no quantitative protocol or evidence on its effect on egg output was found. Lemke et al. recommend removing older cohorts, but give no method for dead flies.
- Mesh size effects, cage shape (tall versus cubic) and cage-material effects: no controlled studies found.
- Nakamura et al. 2016 (low-cost, year-round small cage rearing) full text was not retrieved. Its LED specifications are known only second-hand.

## 6. Measuring mating success and designing a rigorous small-farm light experiment

### Takeaway
Researchers score mating as the number of copulating pairs at fixed intervals (every 10 min to hourly, dawn to dusk). They also use cumulative mating probability, time to first mating, percent inseminated females (by dissection), egg mass or clutch counts in traps, oviposition outside traps, and hatch rate and neonates per cage (per watt). Egg mass alone is not proof of mating, because unmated or poorly mated females can lay infertile eggs, so fertility (hatch) must be measured. Mating predicts trap eggs 2 days later and egg weight and hatch 3 days later. For Tryento, the biggest threats to validity are the single treatment cage, cage-position effects, pupae-batch and age-structure differences, flicker and heat differences between light channels, and ambient light leaks.

### Cited Findings
**Metrics used in the literature**
- Pairs counted over the day in sunlight versus lamps: [Zhang et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3029228/)
- Mating status every 10 min for two photophases, analysed as cumulative mating probability and mean time to mating: [Schneider 2020](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/335432026_Effects_of_light_intensity_on_mating_of_the_black_soldier_fly_Hermetia_illucens_Diptera_Stratiomyidae)
- Percent inseminated females and fertile clutches: [Liu et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jphotobiol.2020.111815)
- Neonates per cage and neonates per watt: [Liu et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/jee/toab225)
- Pre-oviposition period, oviposition period, clutch mass, daily peak egg mass, half-life: [Heussler et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0197896)
- Eggs per lux ("OEA/LUX") as an efficiency metric: [Macavei et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7182258/)
- Hourly counts of mating and oviposition from sunrise to sunset for 7 days; daily trap collection at noon, weighed; hatch assessed after 5 days incubation: [Lemke et al. 2026 preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.08.26.672371v3.full.pdf)
- Individually marked flies (acrylic paint on the thorax) to track matings and ovipositions per individual: [Chiabotto et al. 2024](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Observation-of-mating-behavior-using-marked-flies-Chiabotto-Grosso/b0506b5830b6884f8e47a9554bf24786236d009d); [Muraro et al. 2024](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Mating-and-oviposition-of-a-breeding-strain-of-fly-Muraro-Lalanne/f069cf05550a772b0f847d69c9249ce18720a8ac)
- Time to mating logged by an observer with a timer; clutches reared to count larvae per clutch: [Jensen et al. 2025 preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.09.09.611978.full.pdf)

**Lags and statistical structure**
- Mating peaks on day 1 after introduction and trap oviposition on day 3–4. Cross-correlation is maximised at a 2-day lag for trap oviposition (97.5%) and a 3-day lag for egg weight (60.3%) and hatch (75.2%). Count data outside the peaks are highly zero-inflated. Observations must run for several days. The authors suggest Spearman correlations, and rolling 7-day windows or ARIMA for continuous cages — [Lemke et al. 2026 preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.08.26.672371v3.full.pdf)
- In the 2024 cage study, mating counts showed "no significant relationship" with downstream metrics (unlagged). The authors also flagged spatial cage effects and zero-inflated data as limitations — [Lemke et al. 2024 bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.08.13.607807v1.full)

**Infertile eggs and false positives**
- With some lamps, females mated and laid eggs that were not fertile (ExoTerra UVB100 plus Natural Light) — [Eawag factsheet](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/schwerpunkte/swm/Practical_knowhow_on_BSF/bsf_factsheet_rearing_artifical_light.pdf)
- Only 4% of isolated virgin females laid, so oviposition was "a good but not perfect indicator of mating" — [Jensen et al. 2025 preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.09.09.611978.full.pdf)
- Later clutches of multiply-ovipositing females can be smaller and infertile (Nakamura 2016) — [Lemke et al. 2026 preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.08.26.672371v3.full.pdf)

**Known confounders**
- Colony (strain) effects and light-flicker effects on mating success — [Liu et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jphotobiol.2020.111815)
- Density × light interactions on first oviposition peak, per-female output and survival — [Liu et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/jee/toab225)
- Cohort age structure: 2.3–6.6× effects — [Lemke et al. 2024 bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.08.13.607807v1.full); adult age at pairing: 2.2–2.7× effects — [Dickerson et al. 2024](https://academic.oup.com/jee/article/117/4/1225/7679433)
- Adult nutrition often outweighs light: "production parameters are influenced mainly by nutrition rather than light factor" — [Macavei et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7182258/)
- Humidity gradients and heat on traps shift where eggs are laid (traps versus cage material) — [Lemke et al. 2024 bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.08.13.607807v1.full)
- Egg capture efficiency varies hugely (10–22% of eggs in cardboard) — [Heussler et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0197896)
- Seasonal ambient differences: Sheppard's colony output tracked month-to-month temperature, RH and emergence — [Sheppard et al. 2002](https://forensicentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2016/11/sheppard-colony-methods-2002.pdf)

### Inferences — what Tryento should log and how to structure the experiment
**Per cage per cycle (a cycle is one pupae batch):**
- Pupae batch ID, pupae mass and count loaded, and emergence date.
- Estimated sex ratio (sample 50–100 dead flies at the end).
- Adult density per m³.
- Cage position in the room.

**Continuously (the Raspberry Pis already do most of this):**
- Temperature and RH from both DHT22s at 1–5 min resolution.
- Actuator states (heater, fans, atomizer duty).
- LED channel on/off state with timestamps.
- Ideally a light sensor inside each cage, to detect channel failures and ambient leaks (for example sunlight through windows or room lights at night).

**Light characterisation, once per setup and after any change:**
- Photon flux (µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) per channel, measured at cage floor, mid-height and top.
- If possible, a spectrum per channel.
- Surface temperature under the lamps with both channels on.
- The driver/PWM frequency.

**Behaviour:**
- Scan counts of copulating pairs, for example every 30 min from 06:00 to 17:30 on days 1–4 of each cohort, or at minimum at 08:00, 10:00, 12:00, 14:00 and 16:00.
- A fixed-position camera with time-lapse is a low-cost way to get unbiased counts.

**Output:**
- Daily egg mass in traps plus eggs laid outside traps (scrape and weigh or estimate).
- Hatch rate on a subsample of each day's eggs (count neonates from a weighed egg aliquot, or eggs versus empty chorions).
- Neonates per cage per cycle.
- Energy use per channel, for neonates per kWh.

**Mortality:**
- Daily dead-adult count by sex (this also yields half-life).

**Design:**
- Change one factor at a time (for example blue duration only).
- Randomise and rotate treatments across the three cages over successive pupae batches (a 3×3 Latin square over 3 cycles is the minimum).
- Split each pupae batch equally across cages so that batch is a blocking factor.
- Analyse per-cage-cycle totals with batch and cage as random or block effects, using mating counts lagged 2 days against trap eggs and 3 days against hatch.

**Power (rough, inference):**
- If cage-cycle egg output has a coefficient of variation around 20–30%, detecting a 20% difference with a simple two-group comparison (α = 0.05, power 0.8) needs roughly 16–36 cage-cycles per arm. The formula is n ≈ 2(1.96 + 0.84)² / (0.2/CV)².
- Blocking on batch and cage reduces this substantially, but a one-cage, one-or-two-cycle comparison can detect only very large (more than 2×) effects.
- The CVs here are assumptions: Macavei's best treatment had about 17% CV and other treatments exceeded 100%.

**Pre-register the decision rule**, for example: "keep a 6 h blue cap if egg mass and hatch are within 10% of 11.5 h blue".

### Gaps
- No published protocol specifically for small-farm (3-cage) BSF light trials was found. The recommendations above are adapted from the lab-study methods cited.
- No peer-reviewed validation of camera-based mating counts in BSF cages was found. FlyCount counts adults entering the cage, not matings.
- A typical between-cage CV of egg output under identical conditions was not found for a small farm. Tryento would need to run a uniformity trial (all three cages under identical settings for 1–2 cycles) to estimate it.
