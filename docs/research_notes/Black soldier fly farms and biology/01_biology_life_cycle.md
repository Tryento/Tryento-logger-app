# Black soldier fly (Hermetia illucens): biology, life cycle and developmental physiology

Scope: the immature stages (egg → 6 feeding larval instars → prepupa → pupa → adult emergence) and whole-organism physiology. Adult mating, light and oviposition are covered only briefly. Farm context used for interpretation: Tryento (Venezuela, tropical lowland). Adult cages at 30 °C ±1 / 72% RH ±3; larvae in trays on bagasse/spent grain, wheat bran and dairy waste; 24 h fast before harvest; oven-drying.

Research note: about 50 searches and fetches (September 2026). Several key papers were paywalled or came back as unparseable PDFs (Holmes 2013 pupation substrate, Tomberlin 2002, Harnden & Tomberlin 2016, Barrett 2023 full text, the Eawag BSF manual). Where a number came only from a search-engine abstract or a secondary summary, the bullet says so.

## 1. Taxonomy, native range, global spread, presence in Venezuela / northern South America, wild vs domesticated strains

### Takeaway
*Hermetia illucens* (Diptera: Stratiomyidae) is native to the Americas and most likely originated in central-east South America, so Venezuela is inside its native range. Nearly all industrial strains worldwide descend from one wild North American founding event (Georgia, USA, 1998). That stock has lost about 39% of its heterozygosity and belongs to only one of two deeply divergent cryptic lineages. Local Neotropical wild populations hold far more genetic diversity.

### Cited Findings
- Classification: order Diptera, family Stratiomyidae (soldier flies), subfamily Hermetiinae; described by Linnaeus in 1758. It is a cosmopolitan saprophagous fly whose larvae live in decaying organic matter — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermetia_illucens); [UF/IFAS EENY-461](https://ask.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/IN830)
- A global microsatellite study (15 markers, 2,862 individuals, 150 wild and captive populations, 57 countries) found **16 genetic clusters**. South America is the origin: central-east South American populations (cluster 12) are the most diverse and ancestral, with successive northward range expansions through Central and North America — [Kaya et al. 2021, BMC Biology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8101212/)
- South American populations had the most private alleles and the highest allelic diversity in the world. Twelve of the 150 samples held one third of all population-private alleles — [Kaya et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8101212/)
- Dates of human-mediated spread: South Africa 1914, Malta 1926, Hawaii 1930, Liberia 1945, the Mediterranean in the 1950s, temperate Europe in the late 1980s — [Kaya et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8101212/)
- The widespread captive lineage traces to about **20,000 wild-collected pupae from Alma, Georgia, USA (1998)**, "almost 200 generations ago". It has since spread to Europe, Africa and Asia. Domesticated North American strains (clusters 1–4) show low allelic diversity from founder effect plus artificial selection — [Kaya et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8101212/). Rearing of this colony from wild eggs collected in July 1998 is described in [Sheppard et al. 2002, J. Med. Entomol.](https://academic.oup.com/jme/article/39/4/695/904029)
- Introgression from traded domesticated strains into wild populations is documented in Africa and Asia. The authors warn that it can erode local adaptation around farms and recommend using regional genetic resources for local breeding — [Kaya et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8101212/)
- A whole-genome study found **two cryptic lineages ("a" and "b") that diverged about 3.9–13.8 Mya** (whole genome) or 4.1–9.7 Mya (mtDNA). That is comparable to divergence between *Drosophila* species, but laboratory crosses showed little evidence of incompatibility. Domesticated stocks derive mainly from lineage a (the Sheppard / North American origin) — [Generalovic et al. 2026, Genome Biol. Evol.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13171150/)
- Captive "Sheppard" populations show a **39% loss of heterozygosity** compared with wild populations (0.031 vs 0.051). South American captive populations kept higher diversity (0.087). Domesticated strains carry six clustered runs of homozygosity, and introgressed regions are enriched in **cytochrome P450 genes**, which may be adaptive to mycotoxin exposure in waste streams. The authors warn that a single founding stock "severely increases the risk of disease susceptibility". No Venezuelan, Colombian, Ecuadorian or Brazilian wild samples were included — [Generalovic et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13171150/)
- Domesticated lines diverge fast. A RADseq study of 11 populations (1 long-term domesticated line, 5 selectively bred lines, 3 wild-derived, 2 commercial) found that lines sharing an origin diverged markedly **within 6 years** of selective breeding — [BMC Biology 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12797794)
- Commercial selection works. At Hendrix Genetics / Protix, a line selected for larval body weight gained **22–53% in larval weight after 10–16 generations**, an estimated **17.5–21.5% annual gain** under production settings. The paper cites heritability of body mass in insects of 0.2–0.6 from earlier studies — [Facchini et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9240604/)
- A selectively inbred Wuhan (China) line had eclosion of 96.8% vs 85.2% for its parent strain, and 1.5× higher total egg output — [Wuhan strain morphometrics, Life 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9227254/)
- Lines have been selected for thermal tolerance. After **9 generations at 40 °C**, larvae gained body weight, survival and substrate reduction — [JIFF 2025, heat-tolerant selection](https://doi.org/10.1163/23524588-00001439). A cold-tolerant selection line is described in [Waste Management 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0956053X24000928) (title and abstract only).
- **Venezuela:** a formal "first record" was published only in 2020, from four federal entities: Distrito Capital (Caracas), Falcón (Coro), La Guaira, and Lara (Tarabana) — [Revista Nicaragüense de Entomología No. 200, 2020](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/342201793_Primer_registro_de_Hermetia_illucens_L_Diptera_Stratiomyidae_en_cuatro_entidades_federales_de_Venezuela)
- Neighbouring Neotropical work: genetic diversity and waste-degrading capacity of wild *H. illucens* from the evergreen lowland forest of the Equatorial Chocó in Ecuador — [PMC9901308](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9901308/) (title only). Genomic approaches to BSF in Colombia — [Universidad del Norte thesis](https://manglar.uninorte.edu.co/bitstream/handle/10584/12169/1140825089.pdf?sequence=1&isAllowed=y) (title only). Wild BSF life history on a swine carcass in Manaus, Brazil (Central Amazon) — [Barros et al. 2019](https://academic.oup.com/jme/article/56/3/681/5316738)

### Inferences
- A "first record" in 2020 reflects collecting effort, not a recent arrival. Venezuela lies between the ancestral centre of diversity in South America and the Caribbean/north-western South American clusters, so wild BSF there are almost certainly native, not introduced.
- Tryento's colony origin matters. A locally trapped wild colony carries native diversity and probably local thermal adaptation, but has not been selected for captive traits. An imported commercial line (Sheppard-derived) is genetically narrow, possibly more disease-prone, and a source of introgression into wild Venezuelan populations if adults escape. The farm should record the colony's origin and generation count.

### Gaps
- No study found that genotyped Venezuelan populations or placed them in a Kaya cluster. No published life-history data for Venezuelan wild strains.
- No peer-reviewed side-by-side comparison of development rates between named commercial lines and Neotropical wild strains under identical conditions was found.

## 2. Duration of each stage, degree-day models, developmental thresholds, thermal optimum and lethal limits

### Takeaway
At about 30 °C on a good diet the full egg-to-adult cycle takes about 5–6 weeks: egg about 3–4 d, feeding larva about 13–18 d, prepupa about 8–10 d, pupa about 7–14 d. On poor diets the larval period can stretch to months. Linear degree-day models give lower thresholds of **about 12–15 °C**. Nonlinear models put the **development-rate optimum at about 31–36 °C**, but population growth (fitness) is best at **about 30 °C**. Survival collapses between about **36 and 40 °C**.

### Cited Findings

**Degree-day (thermal constant) model** — [Chia et al. 2018, PLOS ONE](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6211680/). Kenyan wild-derived colony (icipe, about 2 years in culture), 9 constant temperatures from 10 to 42 °C, 70 ±2% RH, L12:D12. D1 = brewers' spent grain + brewer's yeast; D2 = spent grain + water; both at about 70% moisture.

| Stage | Lower threshold Tmin (°C) | Thermal constant K (degree-days) | Brière-1 Topt (°C) | Brière-1 Tmax (°C) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Egg | 13.65 | 50.7 | — | — |
| Larva | 12.25 (D1) / 11.67 (D2) | 250.2 (D1) / 333.3 (D2) | 35.99 (D1) / 34.20 (D2) | 43.23 / 41.22 |
| Prepupa | 13.29 (D1) / 14.63 (D2) | 142.86 (D1) / 125.0 (D2) | 31.26 / 32.25 | 37.18 / 38.02 |
| Pupa | 13.29 (D1) / 12.22 (D2) | 142.86 (D1) / 111.1 (D2) | 34.39 / 36.44 | 41.21 / 43.99 |

- Observed durations in the same study: larva **12.8 d at 30 °C** (D1) vs 61.6–65 d at 15 °C. Prepupa **8–10 d at 30 °C** vs 83–86 d at 15 °C. At 37 °C prepupae failed to reach the pupa stage. Larva-to-adult took **28 d (D1) / 31 d (D2) at 30 °C** vs 181–184 d at 15 °C — [Chia et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6211680/)
- Life table: the intrinsic rate of increase peaked at **30 °C** (rm 0.127 D1 / 0.122 D2), with doubling time 5.5–5.7 d, R0 187–196 and λ 1.13. At 20 °C doubling time was 16–20 d. The authors conclude that 30 °C is the most favourable temperature for population growth — [Chia et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6211680/)
- A second, dynamic growth model (Logan-10) gives a larval lower threshold of **15.95 °C**, an upper lethal threshold of **39.77 °C** and an optimum range of **30–35 °C**. Its development sums are about 265.5 "h" to the end of feeding and 297.5 "h" to the prepupal transition (model units) — [Padmanabha et al. 2020, PLOS ONE](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7500678/)
- Egg incubation: **3.5 d at 30 °C** and 102–105 h (4.3 d) at 24 °C, with red eye spots visible at 72 h. In the field: 4–6 d (Argentina), 5 d (New Zealand, February), 7–14 d (New Zealand, April) — [Sheppard et al. 2002](https://academic.oup.com/jme/article/39/4/695/904029)
- Larval period "2–3 wk" at 30 °C on Gainesville diet (50% wheat bran, 30% alfalfa, 20% corn meal). Adults emerge **10–14 d after pupation**. Mating starts about 2 d after emergence and oviposition at about 4 d of age — [Sheppard et al. 2002](https://academic.oup.com/jme/article/39/4/695/904029)
- Diet can stretch the larval period far more than temperature can. Kenyan colony at 70% RH: larval development **24.7 d at 30 °C on spent grain** vs **85.2 d on cow dung**. At 25 °C: 32.8 d vs 86.8 d. At 20 °C: 96 d vs 180 d. At 15 °C: 206 d vs 239 d. A fitted model put the fastest development at **30.3 °C (spent grain, 16.1 d)** and 31.9 °C (cow dung, 77.5 d) — [Shumo et al. 2019, Animals](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6466135/)
- Pupal duration (Shumo, spent grain): 13.0 d at 30 °C, 17.5 d at 35 °C, 22.3 d at 25 °C, 61.5 d at 20 °C — [Shumo et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6466135/)
- Survival to adult of 4–6-day-old larvae on a grain diet was **74–97% at 27 and 30 °C but 0.1% at 36 °C**. Development at 27 °C took about 4 d (11%) longer than at 30 °C — [Tomberlin et al. 2009, Environ. Entomol.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19508804/)
- Wild strain under tropical ambient conditions (Manaus, swine carcass): full cycle **53–82 d**, six larval instars; only 11% reached adulthood — [Barros et al. 2019](https://academic.oup.com/jme/article/56/3/681/5316738)
- Wuhan strain at 27–30 °C, 60–70% RH on wheat bran and flour: larval + prepupal period **20–24 d**, generation time **38–45 d** — [Life 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9227254/)
- Upper lethal limits: larval **heat knockdown temperature 46.6–47.9 °C**, higher on synthetic diets — [Larval diet and thermal tolerance, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39099549/). Egg hatch was 9.8% at 37 °C, 9.4% at 40 °C and **0% at 42 °C**. Larval survival was 28–34% at 40 °C, with no development beyond the larva — [Chia et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6211680/)
- Lower lethal limits: supercooling points range from **−7.3 °C (late larvae) to −13.7 °C (pupae)**. Prepupae and pupae are the most cold-hardy stages; acclimated prepupae survive 23 d at 5 °C (LTime50). The species is predicted not to overwinter in north-western Europe — [Spranghers et al. 2017, J. Econ. Entomol.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28525620/)
- Adult lifespan (brief): 8.4–9.2 d at 30 °C, 7.1–7.4 d at 35 °C, 13–14 d at 15 °C, without food, 70% RH — [Shumo et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6466135/). Adults need no food but water is essential (misting) — [Sheppard et al. 2002](https://academic.oup.com/jme/article/39/4/695/904029). Mated adults given sugar water lived about 14 d vs about 8 d unfed, per the Faunalytics summary of Barrett et al. 2023 — [Faunalytics](https://faunalytics.org/black-soldier-fly-welfare-considerations/)

### Inferences
- **Degree-day forecasts from Chia's D1 linear constants (my calculation; the linear model is valid only below about 30–32 °C):**
  - Egg: 3.8 d at 27 °C, **3.1 d at 30 °C**, 2.6 d at 33 °C.
  - Larva: 17.0 d at 27 °C, **14.1 d at 30 °C**, 12.1 d at 33 °C. With the lower-quality D2 diet: 21.7, 18.2 and 15.6 d.
  - Prepupa: 10.4 d at 27 °C, **8.6 d at 30 °C**, 7.2 d at 33 °C.
  - Pupa: 10.4 d at 27 °C, **8.6 d at 30 °C** (D2: 6.2 d).
  - Total egg to adult at 30 °C: **about 31–34 d**.
  
  The farm can use degree-days to predict harvest dates from logged tray temperatures: sum (T − 12.25 °C) per day and harvest near 250–333 DD for larvae. Log **substrate** temperature, not only room temperature (see question 8).
- There are two "optima". Development is fastest at about 34–36 °C (Brière larval Topt), but survival, fecundity and population growth are best at about 30 °C. For a production colony the ~30 °C figure is the relevant one.

### Gaps
- The per-stage values at 20 and 25 °C in Chia et al. are in a figure that was not extracted.
- No full-text numbers were obtained for Harnden & Tomberlin 2016 (temperature × diet), for the Holmes et al. "Lower temperature threshold" paper, or for the 2025 paper "Shortening the BSF life cycle by temperature" ([Wiley](https://iadns.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/efd2.70110)).
- No peer-reviewed meta-analysis of BSF thermal performance curves was found in this pass.

## 3. Effects of temperature (20–35 °C) and relative humidity on survival, development, weight and emergence; where 30 °C and 33 °C sit

### Takeaway
30 °C is at or very near the fitness optimum in every data set found: highest egg output, fastest population growth and heaviest prepupae on spent grain. 33 °C is still inside the tolerable range and close to the development-rate optimum. It sits on the "shoulder", however, only about 2–4 °C below the cliff where pupal and egg survival crash (35–37 °C), and one study found near-total mortality at 36 °C. High RH (≥60–70%) clearly improves egg hatch, adult emergence and adult longevity. The farm's 72% RH setpoint matches the best-performing treatment.

### Cited Findings

**Temperature × survival** — [Chia et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6211680/), 70% RH:

| °C | Egg hatch | Larval survival (D1/D2) | Prepupal survival | Pupal survival |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 15 | 10.6% | 82.8/87.0% | 58.7/65.3% | 62.0/49.3% |
| 20 | 59% | 74.6/82.4% | 68.4/61.0% | 59.4/61.6% |
| 30 | **80%** | **92.6/90.4%** | **82.2/77.2%** | **74.8/77.1%** |
| 35 | 74.8% | 90.8/92.2% | 75.2/79.0% | 54.1/65.6% |
| 37 | 9.8% | 89.4/84.0% | 24.1/5.4% | 19.6% (D2) |
| 40 | 9.4% | 34/27.6% | no development | — |
| 42 | 0% | — | — | — |

- Contradictory evidence at 35–36 °C: [Tomberlin et al. 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19508804/) recorded **0.1% survival to adult at 36 °C** (US lab colony, grain diet), whereas Chia's Kenyan colony survived reasonably at 35 °C. The cliff is steep and may depend on strain and diet.
- **Temperature × weight** (Chia): 5th-instar wet weight ranged from 0.159 g (15 °C) to 0.216 g (37 °C, D1). Prepupal, pupal and adult weights peaked at 35 °C: prepupa 0.186/0.152 g, pupa 0.145/0.126 g, adult 0.078/0.077 g (D1/D2). The yeast-supplemented diet was consistently heavier — [Chia et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6211680/)
- In contrast, [Shumo et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6466135/) found prepupal weight peaked at **30 °C** (spent grain 0.156 g; cow dung 0.106 g) and fell at 35 °C (0.137 / 0.086 g). Their values at 25 °C were 0.153 / 0.088 g and at 20 °C 0.132 / 0.082 g. Their optimum range for larval development and emergence was **25–30 °C**.
- **Temperature × reproduction** (brief): eggs per batch peaked at **30 °C** (spent grain 1,230; cow dung 916) and fell at 35 °C (752 / 669). No eggs were laid at 15 °C on cow dung — [Shumo et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6466135/). Lifetime fecundity was **475–516 eggs/female at 30 °C** and significantly reduced at 37 °C. Pre-oviposition lasted 16 d at 20 °C vs 5 d at 35 °C — [Chia et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6211680/)
- Oviposition in the field occurs at 27.5–37.5 °C, and in the greenhouse from 24 °C to 40 °C or more — [Sheppard et al. 2002](https://academic.oup.com/jme/article/39/4/695/904029)
- "Hotter is smaller": at low air temperature large larval populations performed best, and at high temperature small populations did. The heaviest individual larvae and best feed conversion came from **10,000 larvae at 20 °C** or **100 larvae at 30 °C**, because aggregation heat stacks on top of air temperature — [Li et al. 2023, Insect Science](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37189246/); discussed in [Insect School](https://www.insectschool.com/processing/hotter-is-smaller-industrial-mass-rearing-black-soldier-fly-larvae/)
- **Relative humidity** — [Holmes, Vanlaerhoven & Tomberlin 2012, Environ. Entomol.](https://academic.oup.com/ee/article/41/4/971/448259). Egg trials at 27 °C; adult-emergence trials at 25 °C. Values marked "≈" were read from figures by the extraction tool.
  - **Egg hatch:** 6.84% at 25% RH, ≈27% at 40%, ≈60% at 50%, ≈73% at 60%, ≈87% at 70%.
  - **Adult emergence:** 16% at 25% RH, 59% at 40%, **≈91% at 70%**.
  - **Adult longevity:** 5.1–5.3 d at 25% RH, 6.5–6.7 d at 40%, **7.8–8.0 d at 70%**.
  - Postfeeding and pupal stages were longest at low RH, where desiccation was the main cause of mortality.
  - The authors recommend **≥50% RH** as a minimum.
- Barrett et al. 2023, as summarized by Faunalytics, recommend RH of at least 70% and temperatures kept between 19 and 30 °C to reduce mortality. This is a secondary summary — [Faunalytics](https://faunalytics.org/black-soldier-fly-welfare-considerations/)
- Substrate moisture (a different variable from air RH) is covered by the growth model:
  - optimum **48–75%** water on a wet basis;
  - growth stops below **32.9%**;
  - maximum ingestion at ≥69%;
  - oxygen diffusion becomes limiting above **76%**;
  - larvae die above **83.3%**.
  
  — [Padmanabha et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7500678/)
- Temperature and diet interact: brewer's spent grain produced 51–65% heavier prepupae than cow dung at the same temperatures — [Shumo et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6466135/)

### Inferences
- **30 °C (the adult-cage setpoint):** at the peak of the fitness curve for fecundity, egg hatch, population growth and adult emergence. The ±1 °C tolerance keeps the cage safely away from the 35–37 °C decline. Adult longevity at 30 °C is short (about 8–9 d unfed), so cage turnover is weekly.
- **33 °C:** development about 12–15% faster than at 30 °C (by degree-days) with probably acceptable larval survival. Pupal survival and fecundity start to fall as temperature approaches 35 °C, and 36 °C was lethal in one study. As an **air** temperature for larval trays, 33 °C is risky because the larval mass adds about 10 °C or more of its own heat (question 8). As a **substrate-core** temperature it is near ideal.
- **72% RH:** matches the ~70% RH treatment that gave about 87% egg hatch and about 91% emergence in Holmes et al. Dropping below about 50% RH would cost hatch and emergence steeply. In the tropical lowlands, the more likely RH problem is excess: condensation and substrate moisture above 76% causing anoxia and mould.

### Gaps
- No study measured exactly 33 °C with full survival and weight data. Values were interpolated between 30 and 35 °C.
- Humidity effects on the feeding larval stage (as opposed to substrate moisture) are poorly quantified.
- The mid-RH values from Holmes et al. are approximate because the full text was read by an extraction model.

## 4. Larval growth curve, instars, weights, what determines final size, sexual size dimorphism

### Takeaway
Larvae go through **6 feeding instars** (some authors count a 7th non-feeding, migrating "stadium", which is the prepupa). Growth is sigmoid: about 2 mg by day 8 and a peak of **about 140–230 mg fresh weight** per larva on good diets, versus about 80–110 mg on poor diets such as cow dung. Final size is set mainly by diet quality (protein and digestible carbohydrate), then temperature, density and genetics. Females are about 16–19% heavier than males and develop slightly more slowly.

### Cited Findings
- Head-capsule frequency analysis shows **six instars**. Reported head-capsule widths:
  - L1: 0.05–0.08 mm
  - L2: 0.09–0.24 mm
  - L3: 0.25–0.45 mm
  - L4: 0.46–0.57 mm
  - L5: 0.58–1.04 mm
  - L6: 1.05–1.14 mm
  
  Values are from a search-engine abstract of [Barros et al. 2019, Manaus](https://academic.oup.com/jme/article/56/3/681/5316738); full table not viewed.
- Head capsules show **6 actively feeding stadia followed by a non-feeding, migrating 7th stadium**. Growth is **sigmoid**: slow in early stadia, decelerating in the last. Development is fast until stadium 6, then slows. Mass-specific metabolic rate falls from about **12 to about 2 µW/mg** between the 3rd and the last instar while larval mass rises from about 1 to about 90 mg — [Gligorescu et al. 2019, J. Appl. Entomol.](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/jen.12653)
- Wuhan strain at 27–30 °C on wheat bran and flour, molt timing:

  | Molt | Day | Larval length |
  |---|---|---|
  | 1st | 1–2 | 1.5–2 mm |
  | 2nd | 4–5 | 4–5 mm |
  | 3rd | 6–7 | about 8 mm |
  | 4th | 11–12 | about 15 mm |
  | 5th | 18–20 | >20 mm |

  At day 14, larvae measured 22 mm long × 5.8 mm wide and weighed **23.1–24.6 g per 100 larvae (231–246 mg each)** — [Life 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9227254/)
- 8-day-old larvae weighed about **2 mg** each. The model's asymptotic **dry** mass is 115 mg — [Padmanabha et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7500678/)
- Mature larvae weigh "0.14–0.18 g or more" at 30 °C on Gainesville diet — [Sheppard et al. 2002](https://academic.oup.com/jme/article/39/4/695/904029). Last-stage larvae reach up to 27 mm and 220 mg. Larvae eat 25–500 mg of fresh matter per larva per day, depending on substrate — [Feedipedia](https://www.feedipedia.org/node/16388)
- Diet effect on final weight:
  - Kenyan colony: prepupal weight 128–156 mg on spent grain vs 76–106 mg on cow dung, across 15–35 °C — [Shumo et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6466135/)
  - Adding brewer's yeast to spent grain raised larval, prepupal and adult weights at every temperature — [Chia et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6211680/)
- Larval density and dietary nutrient concentration together control performance, body protein and fat — [Barragán-Fonseca et al. 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30449896/) (abstract/title level). Density × temperature interacts through aggregation heat — [Li et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37189246/)
- Genetics: selection raised larval weight 22–53% in 10–16 generations — [Facchini et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9240604/)
- **Sexual dimorphism:** at 27 and 30 °C, **females weighed 17–19% more than males** and took 0.6–0.8 d (3.0–4.3%) longer to finish larval development — [Tomberlin et al. 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19508804/). Adult fresh mass: **female 54.18 ±1.1 mg, male 46.51 ±1.7 mg** — [Georgescu et al. 2020, Eur. J. Entomol., fig. 5](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/The-weights-and-lengths-of-the-males-and-females_fig5_340986704). Adult body length: males 12–17 mm, females 13.5–18 mm — [Life 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9227254/)
- The largest larvae produced adults with the best egg-clutch quality. Body weight correlated positively with both weight loss and clutch parameters — [Georgescu et al. 2020](http://www.eje.cz/artkey/eje-202001-0023_body_weight_loss_of_black_soldier_fly_hermetia_illucens_diptera_stratiomyidae_during_development_in_non-feed.php)

### Inferences
- Tryento's substrates (spent grain/bagasse, wheat bran, dairy waste) resemble the "good diet" groups of Shumo (spent grain) and Sheppard (wheat bran), so larvae of about 140–200 mg are a realistic benchmark at about 30 °C. If harvest larvae average under about 100 mg, suspect diet dilution (fibrous bagasse), overcrowding, excess moisture or overheating.
- Because larger larvae give more fecund adults, the breeding stock should be drawn from the heaviest, best-fed trays, not from leftovers.

### Gaps
- Fresh weight of a neonate at hatch was **not found in any peer-reviewed source**. It must be below the egg mass (about 13–27 µg; see question 10), i.e. roughly 0.01–0.03 mg, but that is an inference.
- Per-instar durations at a given constant temperature were not obtained in full text (Barros et al. 2019, Kim et al. 2010).

## 5. The prepupal stage: behaviour, gut emptying, cuticle darkening, chitin/calcium changes, diapause-like states

### Takeaway
The prepupa is the final larval instar after feeding stops. It has reduced mouthparts, darkens to brown-black, empties its gut, and wanders away from wet food towards dark, drier shelter. This drive is what self-harvesting ramps exploit. Its cuticle is heavily biomineralised with calcium carbonate (calcite), which makes it about 4–6 times harder and stiffer than larval cuticle. Development is highly temperature-plastic, but no true diapause has been documented.

### Cited Findings
- Prepupae stop feeding and seek shelter to pupate. They have dark pigmentation and **reduced mouthparts**. When disturbed, **76% show tonic immobility** vs 1–5% of younger larvae. They crawl with a continuous sinusoidal movement, whereas 6th-instar larvae use head-pinning — [Giannetti et al. 2022, Insects](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8879302/)
- Migration is triggered specifically in prepupae by **low light (6 lux) plus high substrate moisture**; heat and light stress affected larvae and prepupae alike. A self-harvesting set-up collected **87 ±11% of prepupae in 45 min with only 4 ±3% contamination** by younger instars. Colony reared at 29 ±1 °C; tests at 26 °C, 50% RH — [Giannetti et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8879302/)
- Commercial facilities use prepupal migration for self-collection up a ramp into a collecting vessel — [Feedipedia](https://www.feedipedia.org/node/16388)
- Prepupal composition at harvest: 44% dry matter; on a dry basis 42% protein and about 35% fat — [Sheppard et al. 2002](https://academic.oup.com/jme/article/39/4/695/904029)
- **Cuticle biomineralisation** — [The armoured cuticle of BSF, Sci. Rep. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10719276/):
  - CaCO₃ is present in young larval cuticle mainly as amorphous calcium carbonate; calcite increases through development, with about a 76% increase in calcite from larva to pupa.
  - In the prepupa, calcite blocks grow from about 2 µm thick (young larva) to about **20 µm thick × 60 µm wide** on hexagonal tiles; crystallisation occurs within about 48 h after a molt.
  - Hardness rises from 10 ±4 MPa (larva) to 46 ±9 MPa (prepupa, ventral), and elastic modulus from 128 ±32 to 812 ±130 MPa.
  - Calcium is stored as 1–5 µm CaCO₃ microgranules (with P, Mg and K) in **two specialised Malpighian tubules** ("lime glands").
  - Resilin keeps the cuticle flexible.
  - Suggested functions: abrasion resistance, predator defence and possibly antimicrobial protection.
- The cuticle becomes progressively more opaque, pigmented and sclerotised through the prepupal and pupal stages — [search summary of the armoured-cuticle and chitin extraction literature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-49549-5). Chitin has been extracted and characterised separately from larvae, prepupae and pupae — [Waste Biomass Valor. 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12649-019-00924-2); [Sci. Rep. 2022](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-022-10423-5) (titles only)
- Temperature plasticity: prepupal duration was **8–10 d at 30 °C but 83–86 d at 15 °C**, and at 37 °C prepupae did not pupate — [Chia et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6211680/)
- Cold tolerance: acclimated prepupae were the most cold-tolerant stage (LTime50 23 d at 5 °C). Prepupae from a high-nutrient diet had a lower supercooling point (−14.1 vs −12.4 °C) — [Spranghers et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28525620/)
- Significant weight loss occurs across the non-feeding prepupal, pupal and adult stages in every larval weight class — [Georgescu et al. 2020](http://www.eje.cz/artkey/eje-202001-0023_body_weight_loss_of_black_soldier_fly_hermetia_illucens_diptera_stratiomyidae_during_development_in_non-feed.php)

### Inferences
- The prepupa's heavier mineralised cuticle raises ash and calcium and lowers digestibility. Harvesting as late larvae (before full darkening) instead of prepupae gives a less mineralised, more digestible meal. Whether that trade-off is worth it depends on the farm's product specification.
- The 24 h pre-harvest fast mimics the natural gut-emptying step for feeding larvae, reducing gut contents (substrate and microbes) in the dried product.
- Prepupae self-sort towards darkness and away from light, so a dark, moist-to-dry gradient in the tray or ramp design can be used for separation without sieving.

### Gaps
- No quantitative data were found on how long gut emptying takes or how much mass is lost from the gut at the larva → prepupa transition.
- No calcium content of prepupae (% dry matter) was obtained from full text.
- No paper documented a true hormonally regulated diapause in BSF. The long prepupal persistence at low temperature looks like quiescence, but this is not confirmed.

## 6. Pupation and metamorphosis: conditions for good emergence, emergence benchmarks, pupal-to-adult weight

### Takeaway
Pupation happens inside the hardened last-larval skin (the puparium). It needs a darkish, not-too-wet, loose shelter substrate and temperatures of about 25–32 °C. At about 30 °C metamorphosis takes about 7–14 d. With good practice emergence reaches **about 75–97%**. An adult weighs roughly **55–60% of its pupal mass** (derived from Chia's 35 °C means; see Inferences).

### Cited Findings
- Adults emerge **10–14 d after pupation** in a greenhouse colony — [Sheppard et al. 2002](https://academic.oup.com/jme/article/39/4/695/904029). Pupal duration was 13.0 d at 30 °C and 22.3 d at 25 °C on spent grain — [Shumo et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6466135/)
- A search-engine abstract linked to the intra-puparial development study reports that adults are fully formed by day 6 and start emerging on day 8. Conditions and attribution were not verified — [Barros-Cordeiro et al. 2014, J. Insect Sci.](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4212870/)
- Emergence benchmarks:
  - Pupal survival **74.8–77.1% at 30 °C**, 54–66% at 35 °C, about 20% at 37 °C — [Chia et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6211680/)
  - Eclosion **85.2% (Wuhan strain) and 96.8% (inbred line)** at 27–30 °C, 60–70% RH — [Life 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9227254/)
  - Adult emergence **≈91% at 70% RH** vs 59% at 40% and 16% at 25% RH (25 °C) — [Holmes et al. 2012](https://academic.oup.com/ee/article/41/4/971/448259)
  - Survival from 4–6-day-old larvae to adult was 74–97% at 27–30 °C — [Tomberlin et al. 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19508804/)
- Pupation substrate affects both the length of the postfeeding and pupal stages and emergence success. Holmes et al. aimed to define an optimal pupation substrate; numeric results were paywalled — [Holmes et al. 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23575028); [substrate table](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Pupation-substrate-effects-on-length-of-postfeeding-and-pupal-development-and-successful_tbl1_236191545). A secondary summary of Barrett et al. 2023 names **wood shavings** as the best-supported pupation substrate — [Faunalytics](https://faunalytics.org/black-soldier-fly-welfare-considerations/)
- A 2025 preprint reports that pupation substrate **and prepupal handling** affect eclosion rate, timing and adult morphology. The full text could not be parsed — [Durosaro et al. 2025, bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.09.15.676317.full.pdf)
- Pupal and adult weight, and the ratio between them: at 35 °C, pupae weighed 145/126 mg and adults 78/77 mg (D1/D2) — [Chia et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6211680/). Pupal weight and length correlate positively, as do adult female weight and length. The largest larvae produced adults with the best clutch quality — [Georgescu et al. 2020](http://www.eje.cz/artkey/eje-202001-0023_body_weight_loss_of_black_soldier_fly_hermetia_illucens_diptera_stratiomyidae_during_development_in_non-feed.php)
- Sex ratio is about 1:1: 0.49 in a wild Manaus population — [Barros et al. 2019](https://academic.oup.com/jme/article/56/3/681/5316738); 0.95 (as reported) in the Wuhan lines — [Life 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9227254/)

### Inferences
- From Chia's 35 °C means: prepupa → pupa keeps about **78–83%** of mass (145/186 and 126/152), and pupa → adult about **54–61%** (78/145 and 77/126). An adult therefore weighs about **42–51%** of its prepupal mass. These ratios come from a single study at one temperature; farm-specific calibration is advisable.
- For Tryento's emergence area, the evidence supports: temperature about 27–30 °C; RH about 70%; a dry-to-slightly-moist loose substrate such as wood shavings; darkness or low light (by analogy with prepupal photophobia); and minimal handling of prepupae. A benchmark of ≥80% emergence is achievable; below about 70% points to desiccation, excess moisture, parasitoids or overheating.

### Gaps
- Holmes 2013's numeric emergence rates by substrate (sand, soil, wood shavings, none), burial-depth effects, and quantitative light-vs-dark emergence trials were not retrieved.

## 7. Nutritional physiology: diet, digestive enzymes, gut microbiome (pathogen reduction, bioconversion), protein/lipid accumulation, diet-dependent fatty acids, mineral bioaccumulation

### Takeaway
BSF larvae are generalist saprophages with a regionalised midgut. The pH runs from 5.9 (anterior) to 2.1 (middle) to 8.3 (posterior); amylase and lipase work in the anterior part, and lysozyme plus the acidic middle section kill ingested microbes. The gut microbiota depends on diet and gut region. Larvae can lower Salmonella and E. coli in some manures, but only in some substrates. Larval body composition tracks diet: protein 37–63% and fat 7–39% of dry matter. Lauric acid (C12:0) is synthesised de novo from dietary sugars and starch and can exceed 50% of fatty acids. Cadmium bioaccumulates (BAF about 2–5); lead does not (BAF <1).

### Cited Findings
- **Midgut anatomy:** foregut, midgut and hindgut, with the midgut divided into three functional regions. Luminal pH is **5.9 (anterior), 2.1 (middle) and 8.3 (posterior)**. The anterior midgut has secretory columnar cells where soluble **amylases** start polysaccharide breakdown and **lipase** hydrolyses lipids; protein digestion is also region-specific. Significant **lysozyme** activity in the anterior and middle regions, together with the very acidic middle midgut, is proposed to kill pathogenic microbes ingested with the substrate — [Bonelli et al. 2019, Front. Physiol.](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2019.00204/full); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30906266/)
- The larval gut microbiota is **affected by diet and differs between midgut regions** — [Bruno et al. 2019, AEM](https://journals.asm.org/doi/full/10.1128/aem.01864-18). It is structured by gut region, life stage and diet — [PMC10469785](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10469785/). Community dynamics along the gut on industrial residual streams — [Front. Microbiol. 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/microbiology/articles/10.3389/fmicb.2023.1276187/full) (titles and abstracts only)
- Amylase activity varies across larval development and feeding substrate, and larvae may digest starch externally in the substrate — [Animal 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S175173112400274X) (title only)
- **Pathogen reduction:**
  - In chicken manure, larvae cut **Salmonella Enteritidis by about 2.5 log** vs controls over 3 d.
  - They accelerated inactivation of *E. coli* O157:H7 in chicken manure, more so at 27 or 32 °C than at 23 °C.
  - They had **no effect in cow manure and enhanced E. coli survival in hog manure**.
  
  — [Erickson et al. 2004, J. Food Prot.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15083719/)
- Gut *Bacillus* isolates (seven strains) helped larvae reduce *Salmonella*, and bioaugmentation with gut microbiota is being explored — [PubMed 41428602](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41428602/) (abstract level)
- **Body composition:** crude protein **37–63% of DM** and crude fat **7–39% of DM**, depending on diet and stage — [Barragán-Fonseca et al. 2017, JIFF](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/317221082_Nutritional_value_of_the_black_soldier_fly_Hermetia_illucens_L_and_its_suitability_as_animal_feed). Prepupae on Gainesville diet: 42% protein, 35% fat (DM), 44% DM — [Sheppard et al. 2002](https://academic.oup.com/jme/article/39/4/695/904029)
- **Fatty acids:** on vegetable substrates **lauric acid (C12:0) can exceed 50% of total fatty acids**, with myristic acid (C14:0) another 7–9%. Larval fat is mainly lauric and other saturated fatty acids **synthesised by the larvae whatever the diet**. Diet (e.g. marine by-products) can shift the profile only within limits — [Ewald et al. 2020, Waste Management](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31655329/). Lauric acid production is linked to available non-fibre carbohydrates. Larvae use carbohydrates first for fatty-acid synthesis and turn to dietary fatty acids when carbohydrates run low — [Insects 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9963906/). Marine substrates give low lauric acid; fruit, vegetables and some cereals give the highest (Spranghers et al. 2017, as cited in the same search summary) — [Frontiers Anim. Sci. 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/animal-science/articles/10.3389/fanim.2023.1204767/full)
- **Carbon use:** larvae used **53–58% of assimilated carbon for growth**; the rest was respired as CO₂ — [mass-balance study, J. Cleaner Prod. 2020](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S095965262032535X)
- **Heavy metals:**
  - Prepupal cadmium BAF was **2.32–2.94**, independent of dietary concentration; lead BAF was **0.25–0.74** (below 1) — [Diener et al. 2015, JIFF](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/Bioaccumulation_heavy_metals.pdf)
  - Another study found Cd BAF up to **5.00** in prepupae on food waste. Bioaccumulation was more likely on **brewery waste**, although concentrations stayed below regulatory limits — [Int. J. Trop. Insect Sci. 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s42690-021-00427-5)
- Calcium is actively concentrated in the cuticle as CaCO₃ and stored in the Malpighian "lime glands" (see question 5) — [Sci. Rep. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10719276/)

### Inferences
- Tryento's carbohydrate-rich by-products (wheat bran, spent grain, lactose-rich dairy waste) will very likely give larval fat dominated by lauric acid. Fibrous bagasse contributes little digestible carbohydrate and mostly dilutes the diet.
- Spent grain (brewery waste) was the substrate most associated with Cd bioaccumulation in one study. Periodic Cd testing of both substrate and dried larvae would be prudent.
- Pathogen reduction by larvae depends on the substrate and is not a guaranteed kill step. The oven-drying step should still be validated as the actual microbial control point.

### Gaps
- Primary-source numbers for protease and trypsin activities by gut region, and the dominant bacterial genera of the core gut microbiome, were not extracted.
- No data on lactose or dairy-waste digestion by BSF larvae were found in this pass.

## 8. Larval thermoregulation and group behaviour: feeding aggregations, metabolic heat, O₂/CO₂/ammonia tolerance

### Takeaway
BSF larvae aggregate and feed collectively. Their metabolic heat raises substrate temperature **at least 10 °C above air temperature**, so a tray in a 30 °C room can reach the upper-30s °C in its core. Larvae prefer substrate already colonised by conspecifics. Waterlogged substrate (above about 76% moisture) limits oxygen diffusion. Ammonia is released after the CO₂ peak, but peer-reviewed lethal gas thresholds for BSF larvae were not found.

### Cited Findings
- **Metabolic heat:** larval activity raised substrate temperature **by at least 10 °C above air temperature**. Doubling density (0, 1.25, 2.5 and 5 larvae/cm²) added **0.6–2.4 °C**. The heaviest individuals and best feed conversion came from 10,000 larvae at 20 °C or 100 larvae at 30 °C — [Li et al. 2023, Insect Science](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37189246/)
- Larval density drives thermogenesis and also changes the microbiota and physical properties of the substrate — [iScience 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2589004225010557) (title and abstract only)
- **Aggregation:** larvae strongly prefer substrate previously colonised by conspecifics over identical uncolonised substrate — [Kortsmit et al. 2023, J. Appl. Entomol.](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jen.13112)
- **Heat tolerance:** larval heat knockdown occurs at 46.6–47.9 °C and is diet-dependent; thermal preference was also measured — [2024 study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39099549/). The Brière larval Tmax is 41–43 °C — [Chia et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6211680/)
- **Oxygen and moisture:** oxygen diffusion is limited above 76% substrate moisture and larvae die above 83.3% — [Padmanabha et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7500678/)
- **Gases:** ammonia emissions started **after the CO₂ production peak** — [J. Cleaner Prod. 2020](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S095965262032535X). Substrate energy and nutrient composition determine life-stage-specific CO₂ and NH₃ output — [Bioresour. Technol. 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41412400/) (title only)
- A sensor vendor (not peer-reviewed) claims prolonged exposure to **20 ppm ammonia** is too high for BSF, **50 ppm** too high for immediate exposure, and suggests a CO₂ alarm at 900 ppm — [Crodeon blog](https://www.crodeon.com/blogs/news/farming-bsf). Treat these as unverified.
- The same vendor says overly wet substrate can suffocate larvae because they breathe through spiracles in the cuticle — [Crodeon](https://www.crodeon.com/blogs/news/farming-bsf) (secondary)

### Inferences
- At Tryento's air temperatures (tropical lowland, 28–34 °C ambient), **tray-core overheating is a bigger risk than cold**. A 30 °C room plus more than 10 °C of larval heat can put the core above the 40 °C area where Chia saw survival collapse and prepupae fail. Mitigations: shallower substrate, lower density, more airflow, and measuring core temperature with probes (logged in the app).
- The "hotter is smaller" result means that in hot conditions lower density or cooler air can give larger individuals.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed LC50 or tolerance thresholds for NH₃, CO₂ or low O₂ in BSF larvae were found.
- No study quantified tray-core temperatures under tropical ambient conditions specifically.

## 9. Biological risks (pathogens, parasitoids, mites, outbreaks) and welfare/sentience

### Takeaway
BSF is susceptible to entomopathogenic bacteria, viruses, fungi, nematodes, protozoa, parasitoids and mites. The best-documented tropical threat is pupal parasitoid wasps (*Dirhinus* spp.), which killed **52–76%** of pupae in African and Indian facilities. The genetically narrow commercial stock is considered a disease-risk multiplier. On welfare, sentience is unresolved, and reviews apply the precautionary principle. They rank oven-baking live larvae among the less humane killing methods; instantaneous methods such as blanching, grinding or liquid-nitrogen freezing are preferred.

### Cited Findings
- A dedicated review of insect-pathogen risks for BSF and guidelines for reliable production — [Joosten et al. 2020, Entomol. Exp. Appl.](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/eea.12916). Per the search summary, BSF is susceptible to entomopathogenic bacteria, viruses, fungi, nematodes, protozoa, parasitoids and mites, and disease outbreaks are becoming more common under mass rearing.
- **Parasitoids:**
  - The generalist pupal parasitoid ***Dirhinus giffardii*** killed **up to 71% of pupae in a batch (mean 52%)** in a Ghanaian facility — [Parasitoids of BSF: a minor problem or the tip of the iceberg? (2024)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/383490134_Parasitoids_of_the_black_soldier_fly_Hermetia_illucens_-_a_minor_problem_or_the_tip_of_the_iceberg) (search abstract)
  - *Dirhinus* sp. parasitism of **71.95–76.30%** in large-scale production — [Indian J. Entomol.](https://indianentomology.org/index.php/ije/article/view/2985)
  - Hymenopteran parasitoids of BSF on chicken farms in India, including two new species — [J. Asia-Pac. Entomol. 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1226861523001048)
- **Viruses:** known and novel virus sequences detected in BSF, with host antiviral pathways expressed — [Viruses 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11359925/). A solinvivirus induces an antiviral response and **shortens adult lifespan** — [bioRxiv 2025](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.09.19.677374.full.pdf) (preprint). Overview: "Diseases of black soldier flies: a future challenge for production?" — [2023](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/375404680_Diseases_of_black_soldier_flies_Hermetia_illucens_L_a_future_challenge_for_production)
- **Genetic risk:** a single founding stock of similar genetic background "severely increases the risk of disease susceptibility" — [Generalovic et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13171150/)
- **Welfare review:**
  - More than **200 billion BSF** are farmed each year. Sentience data are "unclear and contentious", so the authors apply the precautionary principle.
  - Factors reviewed: disease/parasites, temperature, humidity/moisture, aeration, light, pupation substrate, adult space, nutrition, injury and crowding, handling, selective breeding, contaminants, and slaughter.
  
  — [Barrett, Chia, Fischer & Tomberlin 2023, JIFF 9(2):119–148](https://dx.doi.org/10.3920/JIFF2022.0041)
- Per the Faunalytics summary of Barrett et al.:
  - Slaughter methods ranked from most humane (death within about 1 s): boiling/blanching, liquid-nitrogen freezing, grinding. Less humane: sand roasting, microwaving, **oven baking**, air freezing, asphyxiation.
  - Larvae are soft and easily injured by sharp materials, handling and cannibalism.
  - Current practices often involve starvation.
  - Mating cages larger than 3 m³ are suggested.
  
  — [Faunalytics](https://faunalytics.org/black-soldier-fly-welfare-considerations/)
- A November 2023 scientific declaration on insect sentience and welfare — [Eurogroup for Animals](https://www.eurogroupforanimals.org/files/eurogroupforanimals/2024-06/2023_11_efa_scientific%20declaration%20on%20insect%20sentience%20and%20welfare_scientific%20statement_eng.pdf). "Challenges in farmed insect welfare: beyond the question of sentience" — [PubMed 38487436](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38487436) (titles only)

### Inferences
- Tryento's practice of **oven-drying live larvae** would count as a less humane method under Barrett et al.'s ranking. Blanching or boiling for a few seconds before drying is the obvious welfare upgrade, and it also has a microbial-safety benefit.
- The 24 h fast is short relative to the natural non-feeding prepupal period, but welfare reviews flag starvation as an issue, so keep fasting to the minimum that achieves gut clearance.
- *Dirhinus* is a tropical and subtropical genus, so parasitoid exclusion (fine mesh on pupation and emergence rooms) is relevant in Venezuela (inference; no Venezuelan records found).

### Gaps
- No records of BSF pathogen or parasitoid outbreaks in Latin American facilities were found.
- Mite taxa affecting BSF colonies, and nociception experiments on BSF specifically, were not retrieved in detail.

### Inferences
- **Counts per kg or per gram** (1,000,000 mg divided by the mean individual mass; my arithmetic):

  | Stage | Mean mass | Count |
  |---|---|---|
  | Mature larva | 140–230 mg | ≈4,300–7,100 per kg |
  | Prepupa (good diet) | 128–186 mg | ≈5,400–7,800 per kg |
  | Prepupa (poor diet) | 76–106 mg | ≈9,400–13,200 per kg |
  | Pupa | 126–145 mg | ≈6,900–7,900 per kg |
  | Freshly emerged adult, 1:1 sex ratio (Georgescu) | ≈50 mg | ≈19,900 per kg |
  | Freshly emerged adult (Chia, 35 °C) | ≈78 mg | ≈12,900 per kg |
  | 8-day larva | ≈2 mg | ≈500 per g |

- **The 50,000 adults/kg assumption (20 mg/adult)** is inconsistent with the published fresh weights of live adults. If the farm uses it to convert **live or just-emerged adult mass to counts, it likely overestimates adult numbers by roughly 2–4×**.
  - Adults do lose weight during their roughly 8-day life because they do not feed; females also lay about 4–6 mg of eggs, about 10% of female mass.
  - Dead adults collected from cages will have lost water. The 20 mg figure might fit dead, spent or desiccated adults, but no source verifying this was found.
  - Recommended check: weigh three replicates of 100 counted adults, split by live/dead and male/female, at Tryento's own conditions (30 °C, 72% RH).
- **Eggs:** the two egg-mass figures (13 µg vs 27 µg) differ by 2×. Tryento should calibrate eggs per gram by counting (e.g. photograph and count 3 × 10 mg samples) before using egg mass to set tray seeding.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed neonate (just-hatched) larval mass was found.
- No peer-reviewed dry weight or dead-adult weight was found for BSF adults.
- No peer-reviewed eggs-per-gram figure was found directly. The 77,000/g figure is derived from Wuhan clutch data; the 37,000/g figure is from a vendor.
- Adult weights at 30 °C (as opposed to 35 °C) from Chia et al. were not extracted.
