# Black soldier flies convert waste into protein

**Tryento BSF knowledge base, version 0.1 (2026-09-29).** Companion to [the lab specification](../LAB_SPEC.md), which records how Tryento's lab is configured. This document records what the published evidence says about the animal and the industry, and where Tryento's practice sits against it.

Black soldier fly (*Hermetia illucens*, BSF) farming is a managed cycle in which non-feeding adults mate under bright, blue-green-rich light, lay roughly **400 to 650 eggs per female** in dry crevices, and the eggs hatch in about **3.5 days at 30 °C**. The larvae then eat organic by-products through six feeding instars, reaching **about 140 to 230 mg** each on a good diet, before a dark, hard-cuticled prepupa wanders off to pupate. Egg to adult takes **about five to six weeks at 30 °C** ([Chia et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6211680/); [Sheppard et al. 2002](https://academic.oup.com/jme/article/39/4/695/904029)). The farming problem is therefore two problems: keep a small breeding unit inside a narrow climate and light window, and keep the bulk larval trays from overheating, because a crowded tray runs **at least 10 °C above the room** ([Li et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37189246/)). The industry around this biology is in a shakeout: the European and North American mega-factory model has largely failed (Ÿnsect liquidated December 2025, ENORM bankrupt 2025), while lean, semi-automated, tropical producers survive, which favours a Venezuelan lowland farm that does not copy European capital intensity ([AgFunderNews, Mar 2026](https://agfundernews.com/insect-ag-recalibrates-after-a-brutal-shakeout-where-are-the-key-players-now)). Against this evidence, Tryento's 30 °C and 72% RH cage setpoints are well supported, but four working assumptions need correcting or validating: the **50,000 individuals/kg** population factor (about 2.5 to 10 times too high), the egg-count basis (about **38,000 eggs/g**, not 77,000), direct oven-drying of live larvae (worse than a 40 s blanch on microbes, oxidation and welfare), and the 180-day best-before (unvalidated for tropical storage). The largest caveat on everything below is that **no study on Venezuelan strains, substrates or climate was found**, and much of the adult-light literature rests on preprints and abstracts.

## 1. A generation takes five to six weeks at 30 °C, and heat sets the pace

### A native species with a narrow captive gene pool

*Hermetia illucens* (Diptera: Stratiomyidae) is native to the Americas and most likely originated in central-east South America, so Venezuela lies inside its native range ([Kaya et al. 2021, BMC Biology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8101212/)). A global microsatellite study of 2,862 individuals from 150 populations in 57 countries found 16 genetic clusters, with South American populations holding the most private alleles and the highest allelic diversity, and successive northward expansions through Central and North America ([Kaya et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8101212/)). Human-mediated spread reached South Africa in 1914, Hawaii in 1930 and temperate Europe in the late 1980s ([Kaya et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8101212/)). Venezuela's formal "first record" was published only in 2020, from Caracas, Coro, La Guaira and Tarabana ([Revista Nicaragüense de Entomología 2020](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/342201793_Primer_registro_de_Hermetia_illucens_L_Diptera_Stratiomyidae_en_cuatro_entidades_federales_de_Venezuela)); this reflects collecting effort, not a recent arrival.

Nearly all industrial and academic stocks descend from about **20,000 wild pupae collected at Alma, Georgia, USA, in 1998**, roughly 200 generations ago ([Kaya et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8101212/); [Sheppard et al. 2002](https://academic.oup.com/jme/article/39/4/695/904029)). A whole-genome study found two deeply divergent cryptic lineages (about 3.9 to 13.8 Mya), with domesticated stocks derived mainly from one; captive "Sheppard" populations show a **39% loss of heterozygosity** (0.031 against 0.051 in wild populations), while South American captive samples kept 0.087, and introgressed regions are enriched in cytochrome P450 genes ([Generalovic et al. 2026, Genome Biol. Evol.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13171150/)). That study included no Venezuelan, Colombian, Ecuadorian or Brazilian wild samples, and the authors warn that a single founding stock "severely increases the risk of disease susceptibility". Domesticated lines also diverge fast: lines sharing an origin differed markedly within six years of selective breeding ([BMC Biology 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12797794)). Selection works commercially: a line selected for larval body weight at Hendrix Genetics/Protix gained **22 to 53% in larval weight over 10 to 16 generations**, an estimated 17.5 to 21.5% per year ([Facchini et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9240604/)). A selectively inbred Wuhan line reached eclosion of 96.8% against 85.2% for its parent strain, with 1.5 times the egg output ([Life 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9227254/)), and lines adapted to heat (nine generations at 40 °C) gained weight, survival and substrate reduction ([JIFF 2025](https://doi.org/10.1163/23524588-00001439)).

*Implication for Tryento.* The colony's origin matters and is not recorded in the evidence base. A locally trapped wild colony carries native diversity and probably local thermal adaptation but no captive selection. An imported commercial line is genetically narrow, possibly more disease-prone, and a source of introgression into wild Venezuelan populations if adults escape. Record origin and generation count (the `F6`, `F7` labels in the production log) as fields that can be analysed. No study genotyped Venezuelan populations or compared development of named commercial lines with Neotropical wild strains under identical conditions.

### Stage durations, degree-days and lethal limits

At about 30 °C on a good diet, the egg lasts about 3 to 4 days, the feeding larva about 13 to 18 days, the prepupa about 8 to 10 days and the pupa about 7 to 14 days. Diet can stretch the larval period far more than temperature: a Kenyan colony needed **24.7 days at 30 °C on spent grain but 85.2 days on cow dung** ([Shumo et al. 2019, Animals](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6466135/)). The most complete thermal data set is [Chia et al. 2018, PLOS ONE](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6211680/) (Kenyan wild-derived colony, nine constant temperatures from 10 to 42 °C, 70 ±2% RH, L12:D12; D1 = brewers' spent grain plus brewer's yeast, D2 = spent grain plus water, both about 70% moisture).

| Stage | Lower threshold Tmin (°C) | Thermal constant K (degree-days) | Brière optimum Topt (°C) | Brière Tmax (°C) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Egg | 13.65 | 50.7 | not fitted | not fitted |
| Larva | 12.25 (D1) / 11.67 (D2) | 250.2 (D1) / 333.3 (D2) | 35.99 / 34.20 | 43.23 / 41.22 |
| Prepupa | 13.29 (D1) / 14.63 (D2) | 142.86 / 125.0 | 31.26 / 32.25 | 37.18 / 38.02 |
| Pupa | 13.29 (D1) / 12.22 (D2) | 142.86 / 111.1 | 34.39 / 36.44 | 41.21 / 43.99 |

Observed in the same study: larval duration of **12.8 days at 30 °C** against 61.6 to 65 days at 15 °C; prepupal duration of 8 to 10 days at 30 °C against 83 to 86 days at 15 °C; larva-to-adult of **28 days (D1) or 31 days (D2) at 30 °C**; and prepupae that failed to pupate at 37 °C ([Chia et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6211680/)). A second, dynamic model puts the larval lower threshold at 15.95 °C, the upper lethal threshold at 39.77 °C and the optimum at 30 to 35 °C ([Padmanabha et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7500678/)). Egg incubation is **3.5 days at 30 °C** and 102 to 105 hours at 24 °C, with red eye spots at 72 hours ([Sheppard et al. 2002](https://academic.oup.com/jme/article/39/4/695/904029)). Adults emerge 10 to 14 days after pupation, mate from about day 2 and lay from about day 4 ([Sheppard et al. 2002](https://academic.oup.com/jme/article/39/4/695/904029)). In the tropics, unmanaged wild flies on a swine carcass in Manaus needed **53 to 82 days** for the full cycle, with only 11% reaching adulthood ([Barros et al. 2019](https://academic.oup.com/jme/article/56/3/681/5316738)). A Wuhan strain at 27 to 30 °C had a generation time of 38 to 45 days ([Life 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9227254/)).

Two optima must not be confused. Development is fastest at about 34 to 36 °C (larval Brière Topt), but survival, fecundity and population growth peak at **about 30 °C**: the intrinsic rate of increase was highest at 30 °C (rm 0.127 on D1), with a doubling time of 5.5 to 5.7 days, against 16 to 20 days at 20 °C ([Chia et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6211680/)). Survival collapses between about 36 and 40 °C. Survival of 4 to 6-day-old larvae to adult was **74 to 97% at 27 and 30 °C but 0.1% at 36 °C** ([Tomberlin et al. 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19508804/)); egg hatch was 9.8% at 37 °C and 0% at 42 °C; larval survival was 28 to 34% at 40 °C with no development past the larva ([Chia et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6211680/)). The two studies disagree at 35 to 36 °C (Chia's Kenyan colony survived reasonably at 35 °C), so the cliff is steep and probably depends on strain and diet. Larval heat knockdown occurs at 46.6 to 47.9 °C, higher on synthetic diets ([2024 study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39099549/)). At the cold end, supercooling points run from −7.3 °C (late larvae) to −13.7 °C (pupae), and acclimated prepupae survive 23 days at 5 °C ([Spranghers et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28525620/)).

Applying Chia's linear D1 constants gives the forecasts below (valid only below about 30 to 32 °C; my calculation, not a published table).

*Implication for Tryento.* The farm can forecast harvest dates by summing (T − 12.25 °C) per day from **logged substrate temperature** (not room temperature) and expecting harvest near 250 to 333 degree-days for larvae. The 2024 IVIC result on Venezuelan cafeteria waste, a **71-day** life cycle against about 35 days at ideal 22 to 30 °C ([IVIC via Telecom](https://telecom.gob.ve/resultados-de-estudio-con-mosca-soldado-negra-indican-que-es-un-potencial-para-alimento-balanceado-de-animales/)), and UCV/MIZA's reported **41-day** cycle ([FENAVI, Feb 2024](https://fenavi.com.ve/la-mosca-soldado-negra-revoluciono-la-produccion-agroindustrial/)) show how far local practice can sit from the 31 to 34 day laboratory ideal.

### Where 30 °C, 33 °C and 72% RH sit on the performance curves

Thirty degrees is at or near the fitness optimum in every data set. In Chia's data egg hatch was 80% at 30 °C against 59% at 20 °C and 10.6% at 15 °C; larval, prepupal and pupal survival peaked at 30 °C (92.6%, 82.2%, 74.8% on D1); and lifetime fecundity was **475 to 516 eggs per female at 30 °C** ([Chia et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6211680/)). Shumo et al. found egg batches peaking at 30 °C (1,230 eggs on spent grain, 752 at 35 °C), prepupal weight peaking at 30 °C (0.156 g on spent grain), and no eggs at 15 °C on cow dung ([Shumo et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6466135/)). Chia's prepupal, pupal and adult weights, however, peaked at 35 °C (prepupa 0.186 g, pupa 0.145 g, adult 0.078 g on D1), and brewer's yeast added to spent grain raised weights at every temperature. A model fitted to Chia and Nakamura data puts the **adult fecundity optimum at 28.6 °C**, with fecundity 23% lower at 35 °C and 54% lower at 20 °C, and adult longevity peaking at 24 °C and falling 29% (12.4 to 8.8 days) at 35 °C ([Kobelski et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11150853/)). Thirty-three degrees is tolerable and about 12 to 15% faster by degree-days, but it sits only 2 to 4 °C below the cliff where pupal and egg survival crash, so the 32 to 33 °C periods in Tryento's setpoint history are on the declining side.

Relative humidity acts mainly on eggs, emergence and adult longevity. At 27 °C (egg trials) and 25 °C (emergence trials), egg hatch was **6.84% at 25% RH, about 27% at 40%, about 60% at 50%, about 73% at 60% and about 87% at 70%**; adult emergence was 16% at 25% RH, 59% at 40% and **about 91% at 70%**; adult longevity was 5.1 to 5.3 days at 25% RH and 7.8 to 8.0 days at 70% (mid-RH values read from figures by an extraction tool) ([Holmes, Vanlaerhoven & Tomberlin 2012](https://academic.oup.com/ee/article/41/4/971/448259)). The authors recommend at least 50% RH, and a secondary summary of Barrett et al. 2023 recommends at least 70% RH and 19 to 30 °C ([Faunalytics](https://faunalytics.org/black-soldier-fly-welfare-considerations/)). The substrate moisture window is a separate variable: optimum **48 to 75% water**, growth stops below 32.9%, oxygen diffusion limits above 76%, and larvae die above 83.3% ([Padmanabha et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7500678/)). In the humid lowlands the likelier RH problem is excess (condensation, mould, anoxic trays), not shortage.

### Growth, instars and what determines final size

Head-capsule analysis shows **six feeding instars** followed by a non-feeding, migrating seventh stadium (the prepupa); growth is sigmoid, and mass-specific metabolic rate falls from about 12 to 2 µW/mg between the third and last instar ([Gligorescu et al. 2019](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/jen.12653)). Head-capsule widths reported for a wild Manaus population run from 0.05 to 0.08 mm (L1) to 1.05 to 1.14 mm (L6) (abstract only; [Barros et al. 2019](https://academic.oup.com/jme/article/56/3/681/5316738)). For a Wuhan strain at 27 to 30 °C on wheat bran and flour, molts fell on days 1 to 2, 4 to 5, 6 to 7, 11 to 12 and 18 to 20, and 14-day larvae measured 22 mm by 5.8 mm and weighed **231 to 246 mg** ([Life 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9227254/)). An 8-day-old larva weighs about 2 mg ([Padmanabha et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7500678/)); mature larvae weigh 0.14 to 0.18 g at 30 °C on Gainesville diet ([Sheppard et al. 2002](https://academic.oup.com/jme/article/39/4/695/904029)) and up to 220 mg and 27 mm at the extreme ([Feedipedia](https://www.feedipedia.org/node/16388)). Final size depends first on diet: prepupae weighed 128 to 156 mg on spent grain but 76 to 106 mg on cow dung across 15 to 35 °C ([Shumo et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6466135/)). Density and nutrient concentration together shape body protein and fat ([Barragán-Fonseca et al. 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30449896/)), and genetics matter (above).

Females are larger and slightly slower: at 27 and 30 °C they weighed **17 to 19% more than males** and took 0.6 to 0.8 days longer ([Tomberlin et al. 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19508804/)); adult fresh mass was 54.18 ±1.1 mg (female) and 46.51 ±1.7 mg (male) ([Georgescu et al. 2020](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/The-weights-and-lengths-of-the-males-and-females_fig5_340986704)). The largest larvae give adults with the best clutch quality, and egg output correlates with female body weight ([Georgescu et al. 2020](http://www.eje.cz/artkey/eje-202001-0023_body_weight_loss_of_black_soldier_fly_hermetia_illucens_diptera_stratiomyidae_during_development_in_non-feed.php); [Zhang & Puniamoorthy 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11857012/)). Neonate fresh mass was not found in any peer-reviewed source (it must be below the egg mass of roughly 25 µg, an inference).

*Implication for Tryento.* Tryento's by-product substrates resemble the "good diet" groups, so larvae of about 140 to 200 mg are a realistic benchmark at 30 °C on a rich diet; a harvest average below about 100 mg points to diet dilution (fibrous bagasse), overcrowding, excess moisture or overheating. Breeding stock should come from the heaviest, best-fed trays.

### The prepupa, pupation and emergence

The prepupa stops feeding, has reduced mouthparts, darkens to brown-black, empties its gut and leaves wet food for dark, drier shelter. When disturbed, **76% show tonic immobility** against 1 to 5% of younger larvae; migration is triggered by **low light (6 lux) plus high substrate moisture**, and a self-harvesting set-up collected **87 ±11% of prepupae in 45 minutes with 4 ±3% contamination** by younger instars ([Giannetti et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8879302/)). The prepupal cuticle is heavily biomineralised: calcite blocks grow from about 2 µm to about 20 µm thick, hardness rises from 10 ±4 MPa (larva) to 46 ±9 MPa (prepupa), elastic modulus from 128 to 812 MPa, and calcium is stored as microgranules in two "lime gland" Malpighian tubules ([Sci. Rep. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10719276/)). Prepupal composition at harvest was 44% dry matter, 42% protein and about 35% fat on a dry basis ([Sheppard et al. 2002](https://academic.oup.com/jme/article/39/4/695/904029)). No true hormonally regulated diapause has been documented; the long prepupal persistence at low temperature (83 to 86 days at 15 °C) looks like quiescence. Non-feeding stages lose mass: 12.75%, 18.88% and 20.56% during the prepupal stage in three weight classes ([Georgescu et al. 2020](http://www.eje.cz/artkey/eje-202001-0023_body_weight_loss_of_black_soldier_fly_hermetia_illucens_diptera_stratiomyidae_during_development_in_non-feed.php)).

Pupation happens inside the hardened last larval skin and needs a darkish, not-too-wet, loose substrate at 25 to 32 °C. Emergence benchmarks: pupal survival **74.8 to 77.1% at 30 °C**, 54 to 66% at 35 °C and about 20% at 37 °C ([Chia et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6211680/)); eclosion of 85.2% (Wuhan) and 96.8% (inbred line) ([Life 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9227254/)); and, in a 2025 preprint at 25.8 °C and 71% RH, eclosion by day 30 of 82.2% on corn-cob grits, 81.5% on wood chips and 72.1% on frass, where **daily 20-second sieving of prepupae cut eclosion from 92.5% to 61.9%** and delayed it 3.3 to 3.5 days ([Durosaro et al. 2025, bioRxiv preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.09.15.676317)). Wood shavings are the best-supported pupation substrate in a secondary summary of Barrett et al. ([Faunalytics](https://faunalytics.org/black-soldier-fly-welfare-considerations/)). Sex ratio is about 1:1 (0.49 in wild Manaus flies). From Chia's 35 °C means, the prepupa-to-pupa step keeps about 78 to 83% of mass and pupa-to-adult about 54 to 61%, so an adult weighs roughly 42 to 51% of its prepupal mass (my calculation from one study at one temperature).

*Implication for Tryento.* The emergence area should run at 27 to 30 °C and about 70% RH with a dry-to-slightly-moist loose substrate such as wood shavings, low light, and minimal prepupal handling. A benchmark of at least 80% emergence is achievable; below about 70% points to desiccation, excess moisture, parasitoids or overheating.

### Digestion, gut microbes, fat and metals

Larvae are generalist saprophages with a three-region midgut whose luminal pH runs **5.9 (anterior), 2.1 (middle) and 8.3 (posterior)**; amylase and lipase act anteriorly, and lysozyme plus the very acidic middle midgut are proposed to kill ingested microbes ([Bonelli et al. 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2019.00204/full)). Gut microbiota varies with diet and gut region ([Bruno et al. 2019](https://journals.asm.org/doi/full/10.1128/aem.01864-18)). Pathogen reduction is substrate-specific: larvae cut *Salmonella* Enteritidis by about 2.5 log in chicken manure over 3 days, but had no effect in cow manure and enhanced *E. coli* survival in hog manure ([Erickson et al. 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15083719/)). Eawag frames risk reduction as coming "mainly through material reduction (~80%) rather than through pathogen inactivation" ([Dortmans et al. 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)). Body composition is 37 to 63% crude protein and 7 to 39% fat on a dry-matter (DM) basis ([Barragán-Fonseca et al. 2017](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/317221082_Nutritional_value_of_the_black_soldier_fly_Hermetia_illucens_L_and_its_suitability_as_animal_feed)). Lauric acid (C12:0) can exceed 50% of fatty acids on vegetable substrates and is synthesised de novo from dietary non-fibre carbohydrate whatever the diet ([Ewald et al. 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31655329/); [Insects 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9963906/)). Larvae used 53 to 58% of assimilated carbon for growth, respiring the rest ([J. Cleaner Prod. 2020](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S095965262032535X)).

Metals matter for a product sold as feed. Cadmium bioaccumulates in prepupae (BAF 2.32 to 2.94 in one study, up to 5.00 on food waste, above 6 in another), whereas lead is mostly below 1 to 1.4 and arsenic about 0.5 to 0.6 ([Diener et al. 2015](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/Bioaccumulation_heavy_metals.pdf); [Int. J. Trop. Insect Sci. 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s42690-021-00427-5); [Van der Fels-Klerx et al. 2016](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0166186)). Bioaccumulation was more likely on brewery waste, though concentrations stayed below regulatory limits in the study reported ([Int. J. Trop. Insect Sci. 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s42690-021-00427-5)).

*Implication for Tryento.* Carbohydrate-rich by-products (bran, spent grain, lactose-rich dairy) will very likely yield lauric-dominated fat. Spent grain is the substrate most associated with Cd uptake, so test Cd (and Pb) in substrate and dried larvae at least once per source. Larval pathogen reduction is not a validated kill step; oven-drying must be validated as the microbial control point. No data on lactose or dairy-waste digestion by BSF larvae were found.

### Larval groups make their own heat

Larvae aggregate, prefer substrate already colonised by conspecifics ([Kortsmit et al. 2023](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jen.13112)), and warm their own substrate: activity raised substrate temperature by **at least 10 °C above air**, and the heaviest individuals and best feed conversion came from 10,000 larvae at 20 °C or 100 larvae at 30 °C, the "hotter is smaller" result ([Li et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37189246/)). In small boxes, each doubling of density (0, 1.25, 2.5, 5 larvae/cm²) added 0.6 to 2.4 °C, with peaks around 31 to 32.3 °C, but industrial-scale work in Portugal saw substrate at **21 to 47 °C while ambient air was 21.8 to 29.2 °C**, and earlier large trials reported up to 50 °C at 2.9 larvae/cm² ([Klammsteiner et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12205609/); [Oliveira et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13116064/)). Ammonia emissions begin after the CO₂ peak ([J. Cleaner Prod. 2020](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S095965262032535X)). A sensor vendor claims 20 ppm NH₃ is too high for prolonged exposure and suggests a 900 ppm CO₂ alarm, but no peer-reviewed lethal thresholds for NH₃, CO₂ or low O₂ were found ([Crodeon](https://www.crodeon.com/blogs/news/farming-bsf), vendor, unverified).

### Disease, parasitoids and welfare

BSF is susceptible to entomopathogenic bacteria, viruses, fungi, nematodes, protozoa, parasitoids and mites, and outbreaks are becoming more frequent under mass rearing ([Joosten et al. 2020](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/eea.12916), summary level). The best-documented tropical threat is the pupal parasitoid *Dirhinus*: *D. giffardii* killed up to 71% of pupae in a batch (mean 52%) in Ghana, and *Dirhinus* sp. parasitism reached 71.95 to 76.30% in large-scale Indian production ([Devic & Maquart 2015](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/286346095_Dirhinus_giffardii_Hymenoptera_Chalcididae_parasitoid_affecting_Black_Soldier_Fly_production_systems_in_West_Africa); [Indian J. Entomol.](https://indianentomology.org/index.php/ije/article/view/2985)). Poultry red mite (*Dermanyssus gallinae*) invaded a colony, preying on larvae and prepupae and quickly degrading production ([Mahmoud et al. 2023](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/361745265_Poultry_red_mite_Dermanyssus_gallinae_poses_a_risk_in_the_rearing_of_black_soldier_fly_Hermetia_illucens)). A solinvivirus shortens adult lifespan (bioRxiv 2025 preprint). No records of outbreaks in Latin American facilities were found.

On welfare, more than **200 billion BSF** are farmed each year, sentience data are "unclear and contentious", and Barrett et al. apply the precautionary principle, judging last-instar dipteran larvae to have "substantial evidence for sentience" ([Barrett et al. 2023, JIFF](https://dx.doi.org/10.3920/JIFF2022.0041)). A secondary summary ranks death within about one second (boiling/blanching, liquid-nitrogen freezing, grinding) as most humane and lists sand roasting, microwaving, **oven baking**, air freezing and asphyxiation as less humane, flags starvation and injury, and suggests mating cages larger than 3 m³ ([Faunalytics](https://faunalytics.org/black-soldier-fly-welfare-considerations/)). The primary ranking table was not verified by the note-takers.

The farm's factor of **50,000 individuals per kg implies 20 mg per individual**, which is lighter than every published fresh pupa or adult. If biomass is recorded as pupae (the usual case) the population is overestimated by about **7 to 8 times**; if as live adults, about **2.5 to 4 times**; it would fit only dead or desiccated adults, for which no published weight was found. Adults also lose mass in their roughly eight days of life, and females lay about 4 to 6 mg of eggs (about 10% of body mass), but not enough to reach 20 mg. Weigh three replicates of 100 counted individuals at every colony start and store the measured mean.

## 2. Adults mate only under bright, blue-green-rich light and live about a week

### Lekking, coupling and remating

Adult BSF have a lek-like system. Wild males hold territories and quest in the air, sometimes in swarms of 4 to 12 near light, and defend perches against other males ([Tomberlin & Sheppard 2001](https://journals.flvc.org/flaent/article/view/75030); [Julita et al. 2020](https://scialert.net/fulltext/?doi=je.2020.117.127)). In 0.93 m³ cages under 12:12 LED, males concentrated near the light in aerial swarms while females mostly perched (female perching was 2.56 times higher than male in the morning), which the authors read as partial preservation of the lek in captivity ([Lemke, Rollison & Tomberlin 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11508282/)). A male grasps a flying female, the pair lands, and mating is 90% end-to-end "reverse coupling" ([Julita et al. 2020](https://scialert.net/fulltext/?doi=je.2020.117.127)). In an ethogram at 28 ±3 °C, grasping led to mounting in 65% of cases and mounting to copulation in 72%, and only males wing-fan ([Rebora et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13396126/)). Copulation lasts **about 30 to 36 minutes** (mean 36 minutes, range 28 to 48, under tropical sunlight) ([Julita et al. 2020](https://scialert.net/fulltext/?doi=je.2020.117.127)). Domesticated strains no longer need a lek: a single male and a virgin female mated readily in a 3.0 L box at 28 °C and 70% RH under LED bars 8 cm above the lid (bioRxiv preprint; [Jensen et al. 2025](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.09.09.611978.full.pdf)).

The old view that females mate once has been overturned. Both sexes can mate several times: one male with four virgin females for 4 hours gave 100% mating at least once, 96% twice, 88% three times and 52% four times, with a median gap of 2 min 43 s between matings and no sign of sperm depletion, although female remating was "very limited" (preprint; [Jensen et al. 2025](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.09.09.611978.full.pdf)). Females tagged individually can be polygynandrous and multi-mated females lay repeatedly ([Muraro et al. 2024](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Mating-and-oviposition-of-a-breeding-strain-of-fly-Muraro-Lalanne/f069cf05550a772b0f847d69c9249ce18720a8ac), abstract level); mating raises female longevity and lowers male longevity ([Harjoko et al. 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S000334722300060X), abstract level).

### Timing after emergence and adult lifespan

**Sixty-nine percent of mating occurred 2 days after eclosion and 70% of oviposition 4 days after eclosion** ([Tomberlin & Sheppard 2002](https://meridian.allenpress.com/jes/article/37/4/345/77716/Factors-Influencing-Mating-and-Oviposition-of)). In a net tent at 28.1 °C and 70.8% RH, no mating occurred before day 2 and no oviposition before day 4, daily egg output peaked on day 5, and 99.3% of eggs were laid between days 4 and 10 in the unfed control ([Bertinetti et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6369865/)). Age at pairing matters: two-day-old pairs mated **2.2 times more** than 2-day males with 6-day females, and 2.7 times more than 6-day pairs ([Dickerson et al. 2024](https://academic.oup.com/jee/article/117/4/1225/7679433)). In batch cages of 1 to 4-day-old flies, mating peaked on day 1 after introduction and trap oviposition peaked about two days later, so mating correlates best with trap eggs lagged **2 days** and with egg weight and hatch lagged 3 days (preprint; [Lemke, Li & De Smet 2026](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.08.26.672371v3.full.pdf)). Only 4% of 247 isolated virgin females laid a clutch, so oviposition is a good but imperfect indicator of mating (preprint; [Jensen et al. 2025](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.09.09.611978.full.pdf)).

Adults need no food but need water. A colony was maintained for 3 years on misted water only ([Sheppard et al. 2002](https://forensicentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2016/11/sheppard-colony-methods-2002.pdf)). At 28.1 °C females lived 9.49 days unfed and 11.43 days with water (13.09 with agar, 14.58 with milk), and the oviposition period lasted 6.4 days unfed against 17.2 days with milk ([Bertinetti et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6369865/)). At 27 °C flies given sugar and water lived about 27 days against 7 to 8 days with nothing, and per-female egg output was 27.1 mg with sugar and water against 17.2 mg with water alone ([Macavei et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7182258/)). Adult lifespan under tropical sunlight was 12 to 14 days ([Julita et al. 2020](https://scialert.net/fulltext/?doi=je.2020.117.127)). At 30 °C unfed adults lived 8.4 to 9.2 days and at 35 °C 7.1 to 7.4 days ([Shumo et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6466135/)).

### Why light is required and which wavelengths matter

"BSF mating is visually mediated, artificial illumination is critical for successful indoor breeding" ([Liu et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jphotobiol.2020.111815), abstract). Electroretinograms show UV, blue and green photoreceptors over about 332 to 535 nm, with the ventral eye most sensitive to blue and the dorsal eye to blue-green (504 nm) ([Oonincx et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27751886/)). The decisive sunlight-versus-lamp comparison: sunlight gave about 70 mating pairs per day; a 500 W quartz-iodine lamp (350 to 2,500 nm, 135 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) gave about 40 (61% of sunlight); and a 450 W rare-earth lamp (350 to 450 nm, 160 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) gave **no mating at all** ([Zhang et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3029228/)). Earlier failures included a 40 W Gro-Lux and a 430 W "Pro Ultralight" ([Zhang et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3029228/)). Green (about 500 to 550 nm) is therefore essential; blue or UV alone is not a mating light.

LEDs have matched or beaten other lamps in every peer-reviewed comparison since 2016. At an equal 59 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, LED (peak 438 nm), fluorescent and halogen all gave fertile eggs with no difference in pre-oviposition (2 to 4 days), oviposition period (8 to 13 days) or clutch mass, but adult half-life was longest under LED (males about 15+ days, females about 13) and shortest under halogen (about 7 and 4 days) because halogen heated the cage from 27 to 29 °C ([Heussler et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0197896)). A purpose-built LED matched to BSF spectral sensitivity (UV 365 nm, blue 450 nm, green 515 nm, ratio 1:1:3) gave the most eggs, 9 to 10 times more per lux than cold-white LED or fluorescent, though adult nutrition drove output more than light did ([Macavei et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7182258/)). A tuned 2025 lamp (365/450/520 nm plus white chips) attracted 71 mating pairs against 29 and gave 78 g against 33 g of eggs over 30 days ([Zhang et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13441017/)). Colour temperature of broad-spectrum LEDs (3,000, 4,000, 6,500 K) did not change total oviposition ([Klüber et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/jee/toaa182), abstract). Naming corrections verified by the note-takers: the density-by-photoperiod study is Liu, Najar-Rodriguez, Morel & Minor (2022), not Hoc; Hoc et al. (2019) is the density, sex-ratio and photoperiod optimisation; Macavei et al. (2020) is the light-source-by-nutrition study; the "four artificial light sources" paper is Liu et al. (2020).

Intensity requirements conflict. Early sunlit-greenhouse work put the **minimum for mating at 63 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ with most mating above 200** (unpublished data cited in [Sheppard et al. 2002](https://forensicentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2016/11/sheppard-colony-methods-2002.pdf)), but fertile eggs have since come from 59 µmol ([Heussler et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0197896)), 40 µmol ([Hoc et al. 2019](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0216160)) and 700 lx of UV-blue-green LED ([Macavei et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7182258/)). More light speeds mating: cumulative mating probability rose from about 23% at 0.92 W m⁻² to 70% at 431 W m⁻², and mean time to mating fell from 6.4 to 3.4 hours after lights-on ([Schneider 2020](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/335432026_Effects_of_light_intensity_on_mating_of_the_black_soldier_fly_Hermetia_illucens_Diptera_Stratiomyidae), abstract). Practitioner reports (not peer reviewed) give a 70 W metal-halide reptile lamp (about 127,000 lx at 60 cm) good mating and low-lux fluorescent or UVB lamps mating without fertile eggs ([Eawag factsheet](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/schwerpunkte/swm/Practical_knowhow_on_BSF/bsf_factsheet_rearing_artifical_light.pdf)). Vendors of breeding LEDs claim "especially high in blue light" fixtures and 8:16 L:D (vendor; [EVO blog](https://www.evoconsys.com/blog/black-soldier-fly-breeding-led)).

### Photoperiod, blue exposure and timing within the day

Across 6 to 18 hour photoperiods, reproduction is fairly insensitive to day length. Liu et al. 2022 tested 10, 25 and 50 pairs per 30 cm cube (370 to 1,852 pairs m⁻³) against 8:16, 12:12 and 16:8 and found output rose with density but "was not affected by light regimes per se", with more than 20,000 neonates per cage in 10 days at the top density; longer light raised neonates only at high density and lowered neonates per watt ([Liu et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/jee/toab225), abstract). Hoc et al. tested 2, 6, 12 and 18 hours of 40 µmol LED and recommend 6:18 L:D with a female-dominant population at 6,500 individuals m⁻³ or more; longer light shortened the oviposition period and pulled the peak forward to day 3 to 5 ([Hoc et al. 2019](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0216160)). A model fitted to Hoc predicts **6 hours cuts egg production by 19% and 2 hours by 53%** against 16 hours, and dynamic control (less light while flies are immature) gave 9.4% more egg mass in one day less with 41% less light ([Kobelski et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11150853/)). Mating and oviposition occurred in every month of the year in Georgia sunlight ([Sheppard et al. 2002](https://forensicentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2016/11/sheppard-colony-methods-2002.pdf)) and normally under natural 10:14 in Italy ([Bertinetti et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6369865/)). Darkness matters for immatures: development to adult needed 39% more degree-hours in darkness than with 8 hours of light, and larval survival was 72% against 96 to 97% ([Holmes et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29069348)).

Timing: under sun, **85% of mating occurred in the morning, peaking at 10:00** (sunrise about 05:30) ([Zhang et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3029228/)); in the Indonesian tropics mating ran 06:00 to 18:00 and peaked 11:00 to 12:00, with oviposition peaking 13:00 to 14:00 ([Julita et al. 2020](https://scialert.net/fulltext/?doi=je.2020.117.127)). Time of day and intensity together explained mating (r² = 0.49) and time of day, temperature and humidity explained oviposition (r² = 0.58) ([Tomberlin & Sheppard 2002](https://meridian.allenpress.com/jes/article/37/4/345/77716/Factors-Influencing-Mating-and-Oviposition-of)). Under close LED after abrupt lights-on, median latency to mating was 7 minutes with four females per male and 54 minutes with one ([Jensen et al. 2025 preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.09.09.611978.full.pdf)); 92% of matings occurred in the first photophase after the sexes were combined ([Schneider 2020](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/335432026_Effects_of_light_intensity_on_mating_of_the_black_soldier_fly_Hermetia_illucens_Diptera_Stratiomyidae)). No BSF study of dawn or dusk ramps was found.

Is long blue exposure harmful? The concern comes from *Drosophila*: 12 hours of blue LED a day reduced longevity and caused retinal damage and neurodegeneration, worse in old flies ([Nash et al. 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41514-019-0038-6); [npj Aging 2022](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41514-022-00092-z)). In BSF the evidence points the other way: a blue-peaked LED (438 nm) run **16 hours a day** gave the longest adult half-life of three lamps ([Heussler et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0197896)), and a UV-blue-green LED run 16 hours a day gave the longest lifespans (about 27 days with sugar) ([Macavei et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7182258/)). No peer-reviewed BSF study tested blue duration, or blue against no-blue at equal photon flux, on adult longevity or fertility. The claim that continuous 24-hour light is suboptimal could not be traced to a primary study.

### Evaluating the cage-1 photoperiod experiment

The planned design gives cage 1 visible light 06:00 to 17:30 (11.5 hours) with blue nested at 08:30 to 14:30 (6 hours), with cages 2 and 3 as controls ([LAB_SPEC §5.2](../LAB_SPEC.md)). Biologically the schedule is plausible: blue covers every reported mating peak (10:00; 11:00 to 12:00), visible light from 06:00 provides early cues and afternoon visible light supports oviposition (peak 13:00 to 14:00). The premise that blue must be capped at six hours is **not supported by BSF evidence**; it extrapolates from *Drosophila*, whose adults live weeks to months, whereas BSF adults live 8 to 27 days and mate in days 1 to 4. The cap is a reasonable precaution, but should be framed as the hypothesis under test.

The main problem is confounding. Cage 1 differs from the controls in total photoperiod (11.5 hours against 8 and 14), blue duration, timing (control windows are noon-centred), daily light integral and possibly heat load, and cage identity (position, fan airflow, sensor bias, light leaks) is entirely confounded with treatment because there is one treatment cage. Expected effect sizes are small: photoperiods between 6 and 16 hours showed no per-female difference (Liu 2022) or at most about 19% (Hoc-derived model), below what one or two cycles in one cage can detect. A cleaner design holds the visible schedule identical in all cages and varies only blue duration (for example 6 hours nested against 11.5 hours co-extensive), splits every pupae batch across all three cages, rotates treatments across successive batches (a 3×3 Latin square over three cycles is the minimum), and includes a no-blue arm if the question is whether blue is needed at all. Cohort age structure has a bigger effect than any photoperiod effect published (2.3 to 6.6 times in [Lemke et al. 2024](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.08.13.607807), a preprint), so each cycle must start with a single-batch, single-age cohort.

For a rough power calculation (assumed CV of 20 to 30% for cage-cycle egg output), detecting a 20% difference at α = 0.05 and power 0.8 needs about **16 to 36 cage-cycles per arm** by the formula n ≈ 2(1.96 + 0.84)² / (0.2/CV)²; blocking on batch and cage reduces this, but a one-cage, one-or-two-cycle comparison detects only effects above about 2 times. Those CVs are assumptions: Macavei's best treatment had about 17% CV and others exceeded 100%. A uniformity trial (all three cages under identical settings for one or two cycles) would give Tryento's own CV. The decision rule should be pre-registered, for example "keep a 6-hour blue cap if egg mass and hatch are within 10% of 11.5-hour blue".

Other confounders to control: colony effects and **light flicker** affected mating success in [Liu et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jphotobiol.2020.111815), so LED drivers should be constant-current or high-frequency PWM and identical across cages; adult nutrition often outweighs light ([Macavei et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7182258/)); humidity gradients and heat on traps shift where eggs are laid; and egg-capture efficiency varies enormously (only 10 to 22% of eggs landed in cardboard in [Heussler et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0197896)). Lux under-counts blue and UV and over-counts yellow-red, so measure photon flux per channel (µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) at cage floor, mid-height and top with a PAR quantum meter, and a spectrum if possible; as a very rough guide 40 to 60 µmol corresponds to 2,500 to 4,500 lx of white LED (general photometry, not a BSF source). Warm-white LEDs contain a 450 nm pump and a broad phosphor band including green, so the visible channel carries the mating signal and a blue-only period would be expected to support little mating.

### Adult temperature and humidity

Adult reproduction works over roughly 20 to 35 °C in the laboratory and 24 to 40 °C in greenhouse observations, with 99.6% of field oviposition at 27.5 to 37.5 °C ([Sheppard et al. 2002](https://forensicentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2016/11/sheppard-colony-methods-2002.pdf); [Booth & Sheppard 1984](https://academic.oup.com/ee/article-abstract/13/2/421/2393178)). Fecundity peaks near 28 to 30 °C: lifetime fecundity 516 eggs (D1) and 475 eggs (D2) per female at 30 °C, significantly lower at 15 and 37 °C, pre-oviposition of 16 days at 20 °C and 5 days at 35 °C, and hatch highest at 30 °C (80%) and 35 °C (75%) ([Chia et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0206097)). Modelled pre-oviposition is 4.3 days at 25 °C, 3.1 at 30 °C and 2.46 at 35 °C ([Kobelski et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11150853/)). "Optimal conditions for mating" are quoted as 26 to 29 °C and 65 to 85% RH (citing Giunti 2018 and Julita 2020, via a preprint). A tropical sunlit system in Indonesia (679 to 770 m altitude) reproduced at a mean 27.9 °C (22.9 to 30.5 °C) and 80.2% RH (73.1 to 91.1%), with **569 to 650 eggs per female** ([Julita et al. 2020](https://scialert.net/fulltext/?doi=je.2020.117.127)). Under summer sunlight matings correlated negatively with temperature and positively with humidity ([Chiabotto et al. 2024](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Observation-of-mating-behavior-using-marked-flies-Chiabotto-Grosso/b0506b5830b6884f8e47a9554bf24786236d009d), abstract level). A curve fit found no gain from dynamic humidity control, only that RH should be "sufficiently high and at constant level" ([Kobelski et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11150853/)). Chia's page summary also said longevity was longest at 30 °C, which conflicts with the model fit's 24 °C optimum; the longevity optimum is uncertain.

Water provision methods in the literature include misting screens and artificial plants twice daily (10:00 to 11:00 and 15:00 to 16:00) ([Sheppard et al. 2002](https://forensicentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2016/11/sheppard-colony-methods-2002.pdf)), a moist sponge in a water-filled cylinder ([Hoc et al. 2019](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0216160)), water-soaked cotton ([Macavei et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7182258/)) and about 100 mL of water at dawn, noon and dusk ([Lemke et al. 2024](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.08.13.607807)). Wet oviposition substrates are less attractive; females lay in dry crevices above moist decomposing matter ([Sheppard et al. 2002](https://forensicentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2016/11/sheppard-colony-methods-2002.pdf)).

*Implication for Tryento.* **30 °C is well supported**, sitting at the fecundity and hatch optimum and just above the 28.6 °C curve peak; 28 °C is near-optimal and cheaper to hold. The earlier 32 to 33 °C runs sit on the declining side, where fecundity is about 23% lower and longevity about 29% lower at 35 °C. **72% RH is inside the supported 60 to 85% range**, matching the treatment that gave about 87% hatch and 91% emergence. The ±1 °C and ±3% RH bands are tighter than the biology requires (reproduction is robust across roughly 24 to 35 °C, and successful tropical systems cycle 8 °C a day) and about equal to DHT22 error (about ±0.5 °C and ±2 to 5% RH by the datasheet, about ±5% RH in an independent bench test, with a life of about a year for six units; see Section 6). The important control is to avoid excursions above 34 to 35 °C, especially at cage tops near lamps in the early afternoon, and to keep egg traps dry and out of mist plumes.

### Cage design, density and sex ratio

Small cages work for domesticated strains under LED: 27 to 45 cm cubes with 10 to 50 pairs, and even 3 L boxes for single pairs; historical sunlit cages were 2×2×4 m, and mating did not occur in 38×46×38 cm or 53×91×53 cm cages under sunlight in earlier work ([Heussler et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0197896); [Liu et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/jee/toab225); [Sheppard et al. 2002](https://forensicentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2016/11/sheppard-colony-methods-2002.pdf)). Output rises with density: Hoc et al. used 45 cm cages at 500 to 8,500 individuals m⁻³, recommended at least **6,500 m⁻³** with a female-dominant population, and recorded a maximum of 9.0 ±0.2 g of eggs per cage at 8,500 m⁻³ ([Hoc et al. 2019](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0216160)); commercial practice cites 7,000 to 40,000 flies m⁻³ (patent claim; [US 12213467](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/12213467)) and industrial cages of 2.88 to 59.42 m³ ([Lemke et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11508282/)). Sex ratio trades total against per-female output: a male-dominant population (M/F 1.80) gave 22.5 ±0.8 mg eggs per female and 96.5% hatch; a female-dominant one (M/F 0.64) gave 16.6 ±1.0 mg per female and 93.5% hatch but a higher yield per cage ([Hoc et al. 2019](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0216160)). Males mated faster with four females than one (100% against 80% mated; latency 7 against 54 minutes).

Cohort structure dominates. In 0.84×0.84×1.33 m cages with 100 males and 100 females, **same-age cohorts (males 2 to 4 days, females 1 to 3 days) mated 2.32 times more, laid 6.58 times more eggs in traps and produced eggs 1.17 times more fertile** than mixed ages (1 to 16 days) (preprint; [Lemke et al. 2024](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.08.13.607807)). Recommendations: keep the age spread to 4 days or less, release flies within 1 to 2 days of emergence, and remove older cohorts before adding new batches; holding adults, even in the dark, is associated with large productivity losses. Artificial leaves (0 to 0.34 m² in 0.93 m³) did not change mating, oviposition or hatch (all p > 0.5), though females perch more, and an interior surface-to-volume ratio of 3.99 m⁻¹ with white surfaces outperformed other love-cage configurations ([Lemke et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11508282/); [Biomimicry in love-cage design 2025](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/393616826_Biomimicry_in_love-cage_design_boosting_black_soldier_fly_mass_production), abstract level). No controlled studies of mesh size, cage shape or material, and no quantitative dead-adult removal protocol, were found. Female cuticle is hydrophobic (contact angle 139°) ([Rebora et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13396126/)).

### Measuring mating success

Researchers score copulating pairs at fixed intervals (every 10 minutes to hourly, dawn to dusk), cumulative mating probability, time to first mating, percent inseminated females (dissection), clutch counts and egg mass in traps, hatch rate, and neonates per cage or per watt ([Zhang et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3029228/); [Schneider 2020](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/335432026_Effects_of_light_intensity_on_mating_of_the_black_soldier_fly_Hermetia_illucens_Diptera_Stratiomyidae); [Liu et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/jee/toab225)). Egg mass alone is not proof of mating because some lamps produce infertile eggs ([Eawag factsheet](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/schwerpunkte/swm/Practical_knowhow_on_BSF/bsf_factsheet_rearing_artifical_light.pdf)), so hatch must be measured. Count data outside the peaks are highly zero-inflated; suggested analyses are Spearman correlations and rolling 7-day windows (preprint; [Lemke et al. 2026](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.08.26.672371v3.full.pdf)).

*Suggested logging for Tryento.* Per cage per cycle: pupae batch ID, mass and count loaded, emergence date, estimated sex ratio (sample 50 to 100 dead flies), adult density per m³ and cage position. Continuously: both DHT22 readings at 1 to 5 minute resolution, actuator states, LED on/off timestamps, and ideally a light sensor in each cage. Once per setup and after any change: photon flux per channel at three heights, spectrum if possible, surface temperature under the lamps with both channels on, and driver/PWM frequency. Behaviour: scans of copulating pairs every 30 minutes from 06:00 to 17:30 on days 1 to 4 (at minimum 08:00, 10:00, 12:00, 14:00, 16:00), or a fixed time-lapse camera. Output: daily trap egg mass plus eggs laid outside traps, hatch on a weighed aliquot, neonates per cage per cycle, energy per channel. Mortality: daily dead adults by sex. Analyse per-cage-cycle totals with batch and cage as block effects, mating counts lagged 2 days against trap eggs and 3 days against hatch.

## 3. Cohort management beats lighting for egg output

### Onset, site choice and clutch size

At 27 to 30 °C females mate from about day 2 and lay from about day 3 to 4; laying by a same-age cohort concentrates in the following week ([Sheppard et al. 2002](https://forensicentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2016/11/sheppard-colony-methods-2002.pdf); [Kobelski et al. 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/bioengineering-and-biotechnology/articles/10.3389/fbioe.2024.1404776/full)). Pre-oviposition was about 4 days at all densities at 26 to 27 °C and 60% RH, with an oviposition period of 11 to 15 days ([Hoc et al. 2019](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0216160)). A female lays roughly one clutch of about **400 to 1,000 eggs (10 to 30 mg)** into dry, narrow crevices (1 to 3 mm) close to a moist, smelly substrate: 998 eggs and 29.1 mg per mass ([Booth & Sheppard 1984](https://academic.oup.com/ee/article-abstract/13/2/421/2393178)), 603 to 689 eggs per cardboard flute ([Sheppard et al. 2002](https://forensicentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2016/11/sheppard-colony-methods-2002.pdf)), 412 to 478 eggs per clutch ([Bertinetti et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6369865/)), 408 to 493 per mass ([Macavei et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7182258/)), and 620 to 700 per clutch with about 93% hatch in wild Manaus flies ([Barros et al. 2019](https://academic.oup.com/jme/article/56/3/681/5316738)). Realised output is about **300 to 500 eggs per female**; the Eawag manual budgets 400 to 800 in theory and about 350 for its Indonesian reference unit ([Dortmans et al. 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)); Wuhan strains gave 331 ±54 (parent) and 492 ±92 (selected inbred line) ([Life 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9227254/)). Egg mass per female is 16.6 to 22.5 mg in Hoc and 6.9 to 27.1 mg in Macavei. Site choice is driven mainly by smell: traps above boxes with no attractant caught almost no eggs, and local humidity steers where eggs go (preprint; [Lemke et al. 2024](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.08.13.607807)). No estimate was found of how many clutches a female lays in mass cages, nor Latin American colony timing data.

### Egg traps and attractants

More than 90% of clutches went into 1 to 3 mm slots (1 mm 58.6%, 2 mm 16.4%, 3 mm 19.3%), and clutch mass rose with slot width (17.5 mg at 1 mm, 21.2 at 2 mm, 26.2 at 3 mm), so 2 to 3 mm slots and adjustable wooden boards (more reproducible than cardboard) are recommended ([Georgescu et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9319695/)). Traps work within about 3 cm above the attractant, and the attractant dry-matter to water ratio matters: at 1:1, 43.2% of females laid, against 6.8% at 1:0.5 and 24 to 25% at 1:1.5 and 1:2 ([Georgescu et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9319695/)). Sheppard's traps were three layers of double-faced corrugated cardboard, about 3 flutes per cm, in 2.5×5 cm blocks over Gainesville diet mixed 1:1 with water and made near-saturated so females lay in the dry cardboard rather than the medium; more than 100 egg-filled flutes were collected in a day ([Sheppard et al. 2002](https://forensicentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2016/11/sheppard-colony-methods-2002.pdf)). The Eawag "eggie" is a bundle of five thin wooden sheets (25×5×0.3 cm) with 1 to 2 mm gaps, ten per cage, over a shaded attractant, with plastic preferred when weighing eggs because wood and cardboard take up moisture ([Dortmans et al. 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)). Their attractant recipe is 100 g of dead flies, 200 g of nursery residue, 200 g of old attractant residue and 1 L of fermenting-fruit water per cage; the manual notes eggs already laid attract more females and so advises against harvesting every day.

Attractant comparisons: a MIX of chicken feed, frass, dead adults and water gave the highest egg weight among six attractants, and one collector per cage caught about as much as five ([Carbognin et al. 2025](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/eea.70022)). Fruit, legumes and vegetables were most attractive to mated females, with pineapple best ([PeerJ 2023](https://peerj.com/articles/15701/)); fermented fruit waste beat unfermented waste ([Makara J. Sci.](https://scholarhub.ui.ac.id/science/vol29/iss4/2/)); the substrates most preferred for oviposition are not the best for larval development ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362889347_Substrates_most_preferred_for_black_soldier_fly_Hermetia_illucens_Linnaeus_1758_Diptera_Stratiomyidae_oviposition_are_not_the_most_suitable_for_their_larval_development)). Bacteria mediate oviposition, so Lemke et al. inoculated each 500 g attractant box (70% moisture) with about 1,000 seven-day-old larvae. Timing (preprint): in same-age cohorts, putting the attractant in one day after peak mating raised trapped egg weight to 0.89 against 0.78 g/cage/day (about 1.22 to 1.25 times), hatch to 60% against 50%, and reduced laying in cage material; in mixed-age cohorts a delay gave no benefit ([Lemke et al. 2024](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.08.13.607807)). Traps in that study were changed daily at 12:00 to 13:00 and eggs brushed off with a size-2 filbert brush.

*Implication for Tryento.* For a cohort loaded as pupae, eggs appear about 3 days after first emergence, so attractant is due around day 2 to 3 after the first adults emerge (about 10 to 12 days after pupae loading at 30 °C); placing traps on day 0 costs little in a same-age cage. Change traps every 1 to 2 days and never beyond 2.5 days, because embryos show red eye spots at 2.5 days and hatch at about 3.5 days at 30 °C. A MIX-type attractant kept at about 1:1 dry matter to water and topped up with residue from active larval trays matches the best-supported recipes at no cost. No study measured the optimal horizontal distance between attractant and trap, or compared cardboard, wood and plastic for egg yield per cage.

### Egg harvest, eggs per gram and handling

Direct measurements put a fresh egg at 25 to 29 µg, or **about 34,000 to 40,000 eggs per gram**.

| Source | Egg mass | Eggs per g |
|---|---|---|
| Booth & Sheppard 1984 | 0.028 mg (998 eggs in 29.1 mg) | about 34,000 to 36,000 ([source](https://academic.oup.com/ee/article-abstract/13/2/421/2393178)) |
| Bertinetti et al. 2019 | 0.02557 mg at 28 °C | about 39,100 ([source](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6369865/)) |
| Hoc et al. 2019 | 0.025 ±0.003 mg | about 40,000 (35,700 to 45,500) ([source](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0216160)) |
| Eawag manual | about 25 µg | 40,000 ([source](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)) |
| SUSINCHAIN 2023 | 0.000027 g | about 37,000 ([source](https://susinchain.eu/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/Best-practice-storage-and-transport-of-BSF-eggs-and-neonates.pdf)) |
| Vendor (Kimmy Farm) | 0.027 mg | about 37,000 ([source](https://kimmyfarm.com/en/product/black-soldier-fly-eggs-10g), vendor) |
| Outlier: Cai et al. 2022 (Wuhan strains) | about 0.013 mg | about 77,000 ([source](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9227254/)) |

The 77,000/g figure is so far from every other measurement that a methods difference (counting from photographs, or weighing dried eggs) is the likeliest explanation, and it should not be used without local calibration. The working value is **1 g of fresh eggs = about 38,000 ±3,000 eggs**, or about 26,000 to 36,000 neonates at 70 to 95% hatch. Vendor claims of 20,000 to 35,000 larvae per gram imply about 55 to 95% hatch (vendor; [Kimmy Farm](https://kimmyfarm.com/en/how-many-grams-of-bsf-larvea-after-growing)).

Harvest traps every 1 to 2 days and either brush eggs off and weigh them, or weigh each whole trap against a standard tare and hatch the eggs on the trap (Eawag's method: full eggie minus empty eggie; touching eggs is discouraged because every touch can damage them). Eggs are sensitive to temperature swings and dehydration: at most 3 g of 24-hour-old eggs per 500 mL box on mesh so they do not clump, above 25 °C and 60% RH, and temperatures below 6 °C may kill them ([SUSINCHAIN 2023](https://susinchain.eu/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/Best-practice-storage-and-transport-of-BSF-eggs-and-neonates.pdf)). A weight-based hatch proxy (egg-mass loss after 7 days) overstates hatch through desiccation loss and understates it because some larvae never leave the cup, so it is only an estimate (preprint; [Lemke et al. 2024](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.08.13.607807)). Higher egg output does not always mean more viable larvae, since hatchability varied with larval diet ([Zhang & Puniamoorthy 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11857012/)). No data were found on egg-mass loss per day on dry cardboard at 30 °C and 72% RH, or on a farm-scale regression of grams of eggs seeded against five-day larvae recovered.

*Suggested calibration.* Count three 10 mg aliquots under a stereo microscope and hatch three 0.1 g aliquots on moist feed, counting larvae at day 5, once or twice a year. Weigh eggs the same way every time (net of a standard trap tare, or scraped) and log the trap interval.

### Incubation, hatching and cold storage

At 28 to 30 °C and about 60 to 80% RH, eggs hatch in about 3.5 to 4 days and **70 to 95% hatch**: 93.5 to 96.5% at 26 to 27 °C and 60% RH ([Hoc et al. 2019](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0216160)); 71.6 to 81.8% at 28 °C and 80% RH ([Bertinetti et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6369865/)); about 70% assumed at the egg-to-early-larva step in the Eawag flow ([Dortmans et al. 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)). The lower threshold for hatch lies between 12 and 16 °C, where hatching takes about 15 days ([Chia et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6211680/)). Eggs should be incubated above, not on, a moist starter so neonates drop into it: placing eggs directly on media gave poor hatch and neonate survival, suspected fungal, and a snug paper towel over the cup raised humidity and often led to fungus ([Sheppard et al. 2002](https://forensicentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2016/11/sheppard-colony-methods-2002.pdf)). Cold storage of eggs is poor: 1-day-old eggs held 10 days at 17 °C hatched 50.0%, whereas larvae kept 72.8% survival after 30 days at 15 °C and **prepupae kept 75.3% eclosion after 60 days at 14 °C**, and dietary cryoprotectants (4% proline plus 0.5% trehalose) improved cold tolerance ([Wei et al. 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41549582/)). Chilling eggs 24 hours at 10 °C paused development for about a day, with embryos least sensitive 38 to 58 hours after laying (summary only). A refrigeration patent (0 to 10 °C, 70 to 80% RH, 25 days, more than 30% hatch) is non-peer-reviewed ([CN105724365A](https://patents.google.com/patent/CN105724365A/en)). Neonates (2 days old) travel better than eggs but are density-sensitive (1 g per box with 20 g feed and 18 g water at 25 °C and 60% RH; transfers not beyond 4 to 5 days) ([SUSINCHAIN 2023](https://susinchain.eu/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/Best-practice-storage-and-transport-of-BSF-eggs-and-neonates.pdf)). To smooth weekly egg surpluses, hold prepupae or pupae at 14 to 15 °C; if eggs must be held, keep them a few days at 17 to 20 °C after about the first 1.5 days of development and log the storage days.

### Yield benchmarks and when to close a cage

Published trap yields range from **about 8 to 37 g of eggs per kg of pupae** per cage cycle in a Korean greenhouse density trial (pupae held at 26 °C, eggs collected 12 to 14 days after emergence, floral-foam traps replaced daily) ([Park et al. 2016](https://koreascience.kr/article/JAKO201606776010895.pdf)).

| Pupae loaded | Approx. count | Eggs collected | g eggs per kg pupae |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 kg | about 26,000 | 33.1 to 42.9 g | 8.3 to 10.7 |
| 8 kg | about 52,000 | 101.1 to 127.6 g | 12.6 to 16.0 |
| 10 kg | about 65,000 | 281.9 to 368.7 g | 28.2 to 36.9 |

Cage size (1×1 up to 2.5×2.5×2.5 m) had no significant effect; higher density increased output; Park recommended 1.5×1.5×2.5 m or 2×2×2.5 m cages with about 65,000 pupae loaded at regular intervals. For 10 kg over 12 to 14 days that is about 22 to 31 g/day, or 2.2 to 3.1 g/day per kg of pupae; the yield per kg rose with density, so small, sparse cages look worse per kg. The biological ceiling is about 6,500 pupae × 85 to 95% emergence × 50% female × 350 to 600 eggs × 26 µg, roughly **25 to 48 g of eggs per kg of pupae**, so values above about 45 g/kg suggest measurement error (wet eggs, trap-tare errors, under-weighed biomass) (my derivation). Hoc et al. recorded 7.6 g/cage (female-dominant) against 5.2 g (male-dominant) at 6,500 m⁻³, and 4.1 g at 2 hours of light against 6.8 g at 6 hours ([Hoc et al. 2019](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0216160)). Larval diet sets the laying period: 16+ days of egg production for chicken-feed and food-waste larvae against 15 days (okara) and 8 days (brewer's grain) at 30 to 32 °C ([Zhang & Puniamoorthy 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11857012/)). Adult longevity ranged 8.7 to 12.1 days (males) and 9.5 to 14.6 days (females) at 28 °C depending on diet ([Bertinetti et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6369865/)), and Eawag removes each love cage after 6 days because "most females die within one week". Vendor claims (700 to 811 g eggs per m² of breeding space per month; up to 2 million pupae per chamber) are marketing figures ([Soldier Fly Technologies](https://www.soldierfly.com/breeding); [Fly Farm Systems](https://flyfarmsystems.com/bsf-breeding-systems/)).

*Closing rule (management judgement).* Close a cage when three consecutive days of egg mass fall below about 10 to 15% of its peak day; with staggered emergence this lands 14 to 21 days after first emergence, about 25 to 30 days after pupae loading. Log the actual closing date against this rule. No full daily egg curve for a cage of 5 kg of pupae or more run to exhaustion, and no Latin American per-kg data, were found.

### Pupae management, synchronisation and colony scheduling

Pupae weigh 100 to 200 mg (Park: about 154 mg), giving about **5,000 to 7,000 pupae per kg**; Eawag sieves prepupae at 3 mm, weighs 2×200 for a mean weight, and places 500 per 60×40×12 cm container with 3 kg moist compost, filling containers daily for 5 days (about 40,000 prepupae per dark cage). Emergence starts about 10 days after placement, follows a bell curve, ends about day 25, and dark cages are dismantled after 2.5 to 3 weeks; flies stay inactive in the dark until lured by light into the love cage ([Dortmans et al. 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)). Sex ratio shifts with harvest order: the male ratio falls as cumulative self-harvested prepupal mass grows (MR = 0.7169 − 0.0003 × SPM), the first crawl-offs are about 72% male, and balance is reached at about 700 g of 1,562 g from 10,000 larvae ([Hoc et al. 2019](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0216160)); load across the whole crawl-off window. A 40:60 male:female ratio was best for egg production in an Indonesian study (abstract only; [Biodjati 2020](https://journal.uinsgd.ac.id/index.php/biodjati/article/view/9472)). Avoid daily sieving of pupation boxes (Section 1) and hold surplus cohorts at 14 to 15 °C to synchronise.

Eawag's continuous cycle fills a new love cage every 2 days from 3 to 4 dark cages (each love cage holds 6,000 to 10,000 flies, 10 eggies and two attractant boxes and is removed after 6 days), rotates egg traps daily over a 7-day "hatchling shower", and keeps 5-day-old larvae (5-DOL) flowing daily ([Dortmans et al. 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)). Industry splits between continuous release (labour saving, mixed ages) and batch cages (isolated cohorts, pest containment, tunable) ([Lemke et al. 2024](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.08.13.607807)).

A worked planning example (my arithmetic, not a published plan): target T = 200 larval trays per week seeded at G = 0.5 g of eggs (about 19,000 eggs) gives E = 100 g/week, plus a 25% buffer = 125 g/week. At a planning yield of 20 g eggs per kg of pupae per cage cycle, P = 125 / 20 = about **6.3 kg of pupae per week** (about 40,000 pupae). At two new cages per week (about 3.1 kg each, collected over the previous 3 to 4 days), each cage is productive about 2.5 weeks, so five or six cages are active at once. The breeding line needs 6.3 kg / (0.75 larva-to-pupa × 0.8 hatch) = about 68,000 eggs, about **1.8 g of eggs per week (about 1.5% of output)**, drawn from at least 3 or 4 cages and days. In formula form: E = T × G; P = E × (1 + buffer) / Y_trailing; breeder eggs = P × pupae_per_kg / (hatch × survival × eggs_per_g). No complete industrial breeding-unit calendar was found; the FAO/ICIPE manuals and the Eawag 2021 second edition were not reviewed.

### Breeding programme and genetics

Eawag keeps 2 to 5% of 5-DOL to regenerate the colony (nursed 2.5 weeks on 2 kg of 30% chicken feed to become prepupae) ([Dortmans et al. 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)). The real risk is a small effective population (Ne): a colony founded from wild flies lost diversity and **collapsed by the fifth generation**, most likely by inbreeding depression ([Rhode et al. 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32524667/)); a colony founded in 2012 had estimated Ne of 59.0 (F28), 56.7 (F48), 22.6 (F52) and 24.4 (F62), mated pairs highly related (0.455 to 1.000), and multiple paternity in 2 of 5 families; the authors warn that Ne below 100 carries inbreeding-depression risk and recommend trialling wild augmentation on a small subset first ([Hoffmann et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8224309/)). Inbreeding coefficients (F_IS) across 11 populations were 0.121 to 0.234, lowest in wild populations and highest in the two commercial ones ([Silvaraju et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12797794)). A counter-example: deliberately inbreeding from the single best clutch for 10 generations raised eggs per female 1.55 times (331 to 492) and eclosion from 85% to 97%, a single study ([Cai et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9227254/)). Practical judgement (not literature): breeders should be a random volumetric sample of eggs pooled from all active cages across several harvest days, or a large pooled cohort with the smallest 20 to 30% discarded; keep 2 to 3 parallel sub-lines and swap 5 to 10% of breeders every few generations; introduce wild-caught Venezuelan flies through quarantine for one or two generations and cross them into one sub-line first, never starting a production line from a small wild sample alone; and chart per-generation mean pupal mass, emergence %, g eggs per kg pupae, hatch %, sex ratio, larval development time and eggs laid outside traps, treating a sustained drop of 20% or more across 2 to 3 generations under constant conditions as a trigger for augmentation. At 30 °C expect 6 to 7 weeks and about 8 generations a year. No BSF estimate links per-generation loss of fecundity to Ne.

## 4. Larval trays are won or lost on moisture, depth and core temperature

### Nursery or direct seeding

The best-documented SOP (Eawag) hatches eggs over a moist mash of **30% dry chicken feed and 70% water** (3 kg per 60×40×12 cm container) covered with 0.5 to 1 cm of dry coco peat; larvae feed there five days, are sieved at 1 mm, counted by hand-counting two 2 g samples, and dosed into grow-out containers by count, with the planning rule of 600 to 800 larvae per kg of wet waste over the treatment ([Dortmans et al. 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)). A 2026 study found chicken-feed-nursed neonates heavier and better surviving than food-waste-nursed ones (final weight 0.205 g and 92.9% survival for chicken feed then chicken feed, against 0.156 g and 75.1% for food waste then food waste), and larvae adapted faster when fattening diet matched nursery diet ([Cerckel et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13534125/)). Handling six-day-old larvae (sampling, washing, weighing about 30) did not change duration, final weight (150 to 160 mg), survival (94.5 to 96.2%) or bioconversion ([Loiotine et al. 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/veterinary-science/articles/10.3389/fvets.2024.1330342/full)). No peer-reviewed head-to-head of eggs hatched directly on the grow-out substrate against a separate nursery, and no nursery stocking density, was found.

*Implication for Tryento.* A separate nursery lets grow-out trays be dosed by larval count rather than egg mass, which removes the contested eggs-per-gram and hatch-rate uncertainty from tray KPIs, and protects neonates from wet, acidic or fermenting substrates. Direct seeding is simpler, but the "g larvae per g eggs" KPI then combines hatch, neonate survival and grow-out performance. The starter is effectively an in-tray nursery: wheat bran (afrecho) moistened to about 70% behaves most like the Eawag mash; yogurt alone is liquid and acidic and risks drowning neonates (inference; no direct study); fibrous bagasse gives lighter larvae unless supplemented (spent grain without yeast needed about 33% more degree-days, 333 against 250, in [Chia et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6211680/)). The protocol now in use, per [LAB_SPEC §2.1](../LAB_SPEC.md), is a nursery in effect: eggs hatch on a starter paste at 70% moisture in an incubadora for 7 days before distribution into trays.

### Tray size, density and heat

The main benchmark (Eawag) is about **4 larvae/cm²**: 10,000 5-DOL per 40×60×17 cm crate on 15 kg of wet waste (75% water) over 12 days, 40,000 per m² fed 60 kg over 12 days, with the substrate layer at 5 cm or less, because deeper layers leave unprocessed waste at the bottom; too much feed builds heat and attracts filth flies, too little starves larvae ([Dortmans et al. 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)). Controlled studies put the optimum at about 5 larvae/cm² (5 on an "omnivorous" diet, 7.57 on a control diet; final weight 108 mg at 5/cm² against 98 mg at 15/cm²; survival 96.5 to 98.5%) ([Insects 2025, Part II](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11765738/)), citing Diener and Parra Paz. Barragán-Fonseca et al. (0.31 to 2.47 larvae/cm², 27 °C, fixed 0.6 g DM per larva) saw development to first prepupa in 13 to 27 days, fastest (13 d) on the most concentrated diet at the lowest density; feeding "3× weekly by visual estimation" left larvae food-limited ([Barragán-Fonseca et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6221057/)). At 27.5 °C ambient in 9.5 cm boxes, peak substrate temperatures were about 31 °C at 2.5/cm² and about 32.3 °C at 5/cm², substrate pH rose with density (5.7 to 6.6 at 1.25/cm² against 7.8 to 7.9 at 5/cm²), and the authors quote an upper thermal limit of about 44 °C and note that "constant exposure to environmental temperatures of 36 °C–37 °C already result in negative effects" ([Klammsteiner et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12205609/)). Moving larvae from 30 to 20 °C at about 10 days old increased production weight by 16% ([Li et al. 2023](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/1744-7917.13198)). No peer-reviewed conversion of kg of eggs per m² and no tropical-ambient density trial with substrate-temperature logging at farm scale were found.

*Implication for Tryento.* A 40×60 cm tray (2,400 cm²) at 4 to 5 larvae/cm² holds about 10,000 to 12,000 larvae, needing roughly 0.2 to 0.45 g of eggs per tray at 37,000 to 77,000 eggs/g, 70 to 80% hatch and 90% survival (assumptions to calibrate). The current v2 plan distributes about **25,000 larvae per tray** ([LAB_SPEC §2.1](../LAB_SPEC.md)); tray dimensions are not recorded, but 25,000 larvae at 5/cm² would need about 5,000 cm² (for example 50×100 cm), so the actual density in larvae/cm² is a field to measure. In a room at 28 to 32 °C, stay at or below 4 to 5 larvae/cm², keep layers at 5 cm or less, leave air gaps between stacked trays, and read core temperature daily; a core above about 40 °C is an alarm, and larvae massing at the surface or climbing walls are behavioural signs. The 36 °C bed limit the lab set for days 11 to 12 matches the chronic-harm threshold above and the 0.1% survival at a constant 36 °C ([Tomberlin et al. 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19508804/)).

### Feeding rate and pattern

The anchor study, Diener et al. 2009, found that **100 mg of chicken feed (60% moisture) per larva per day**, about 40 mg DM, gave the best trade-off between waste reduction (41.8%) and prepupal weight (48.0 mg DM), equal to 3 to 5 kg/m²/day of market waste and about 145 g DM of prepupae per m² per day ([Diener et al. 2009](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0734242X09103838)). Part II modelled optima of about 90 mg per larva per day (omnivorous diet) and 175 mg (control diet); final weight was 59.8 mg at 50 mg/larva/day against 160.1 mg at 200 mg/larva/day; FCR was 7.50 at 50 mg, 5.25 at the omnivorous optimum and 9.59 at the control optimum (basis apparently as-fed); substrate reduction was 79.6 to 85.5%; the literature range cited is 25 to 500 mg substrate per larva per day ([Insects 2025, Part II](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11765738/)). Eawag's regime is leaner, about 31 mg DM (125 mg wet) per larva per day in three equal feedings on days 1, 5 and 8, favouring waste reduction over size. Daily feeding gave the heaviest larvae (0.149 g) but needed longer (11.3 days) than batch feeding to reach the prepupa ([Meneguz et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0202591)). A practical band for Tryento is 30 to 50 mg DM per larva per day, converted to kilos of each feed by its DM% (indicative, unsourced DM values: afrecho about 88 to 90%, brewer's spent grain about 16 to 25%, yogurt about 12 to 15% assumed; measure on site at 105 °C for 24 hours, the Eawag method). Low rates maximise waste reduction but give small larvae (about 60 mg); high rates (150 to 200 mg wet per larva per day) give 150 to 200 mg larvae but leave residue and worsen FCR. For a larvae-as-product farm, incremental feeding keeps the layer shallow and limits heating and anaerobic pockets, while batch feeding shortens the cycle.

*Check on the v2 plan (my arithmetic, assumptions stated).* If a v2 tray carries 25,000 larvae and receives 5.5 kg of ensilaje in total (1.5 + 2.0 + 2.0 kg on days 7, 10 and 13, [LAB_SPEC §2.1](../LAB_SPEC.md)), that is **about 220 mg of wet ensilaje per larva across the grow-out, or about 31 mg wet per larva per day over seven feeding days**, at the very bottom of the published 25 to 500 mg range and far below Diener's 100 mg and Eawag's 125 mg wet. At a wet FCR of 5 to 8 that would support only about 27 to 44 mg of larva per larva unless the incubadora starter (its kilos are recorded per incubadora, but not in this calculation) supplies much of the growth. Confirm the larval count, the ensilaje dry-matter fraction and the starter mass, then compare the measured mean weight of harvested larvae with the 60 mg (50 mg/day) to 160 mg (200 mg/day) response in Part II.

### Substrates: what the evidence says about bagazo, afrecho and yogurt

Performance tracks protein and digestible non-fibre carbohydrate (NFC). Gold et al. 2020 formulated biowastes at a protein-to-NFC ratio of 1:1, 14 to 19% protein and 13 to 15% NFC (dry mass); vegetable canteen waste performed best and cow manure worst, and formulations reduced variability against single wastes ([Gold et al. 2020](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0956053X19306658)). Diets at protein-to-carbohydrate 1:2 with a summed 47% protein plus carbohydrate performed best ([Barragán-Fonseca et al. 2018, JEE](https://doi.org/10.1093/jee/toy270)); the most concentrated diet (14% protein, 46% non-cellulose carbohydrate) gave the shortest development (13 days) and the most dilute (3.5% protein, 12%) the slowest ([Barragán-Fonseca et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6221057/)). Waste-to-biomass conversion and development time depended mainly on total volatile solids and protein ([Lalander et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclepro.2018.10.017)).

*Terminology.* In Latin American animal-feed usage, brewer's spent grain (BSG) is "bagazo de cerveza/cebada", delivered at 75 to 80% moisture and spoiling within 2 to 5 days in warm climates ([produccion-animal.com.ar](https://www.produccion-animal.com.ar/tablas_composicion_alimentos/161-Bagazo_cebada.pdf); [Nutrinews](https://nutrinews.com/materias-primas-bagazo-de-cerveza/)); "afrecho" is also used regionally for brewer's grains, not only wheat bran ([Engormix](https://www.engormix.com/lecheria/miscellaneous/afrecho-cebada-como-conservarlo_f11459/)). In Venezuela, a sugarcane country, plain "bagazo" most commonly means sugarcane bagasse. The two behave completely differently, so log "bagazo_cerveza" and "bagazo_caña" separately. The v2 protocol identifies its feed as BSG ensilaje, partly answering this ([LAB_SPEC §2.1](../LAB_SPEC.md)).

*Brewer's spent grain.* In Italy at 25 °C (1,000 two-day-old larvae per 21×27 cm box, ad libitum), BSG (15.8% DM, 15.8% crude protein, 53.6% NDF) gave **98 mg/larva against 229 mg on a hen diet, 22 against 15 days, 95.9% survival, WRI 3.01 against 4.46%/day and ECI 0.25 against 0.27**; BSG larvae were 36.5% DM, 54.1% crude protein and 23.2% ether extract on a DM basis; okara gave the best ECI (0.36) ([Bava et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6617253/)). Brewery by-products reached the prepupa in 8.0 trial-days against 20.2 for winery by-products ([Meneguz et al. 2018, JSFA](https://doi.org/10.1002/jsfa.9127)). Brewer's yeast supplementation cut the larval thermal constant from 333 to 250 degree-days ([Chia et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0206097)), and increasing spent yeast improves bioconversion (Animal 2024, snippet). The slower development on BSG is attributed to cellulose, lignocellulose and lignin.

*Sugarcane bagasse.* It requires pretreatment: steam pretreatment plus enzymatic hydrolysis plus microbial biotreatment gave BCR 13.4%, and microbes without enzymes were "not feasible" ([Bothma et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1163/23524588-00001186)). Bagasse plus vinasse was the best sugarcane by-product mix but far below canteen leftovers; bagasse plus molasses was very poor (0.06 g against 3.15 g), probably because sticky sugars hamper movement; pure bagasse was not tested ([Zandi-Sohani & Tomberlin 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11508635/)). Fermenting sugarcane waste with *Bacillus subtilis*, *Enterococcus faecalis* and *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* raised larval weight, survival and conversion (abstract level; [PubMed 40460905](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40460905/)). It is essentially a bulking agent unless pretreated.

*Dairy.* Cheese whey "did not affect the growth of the larvae, on the contrary, it increased it" (final weight 0.252 ±0.003 g; larvae 42.5% lipid and 35.0% protein) ([Caltzontzin-Rabell et al. 2024](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/eng2.12853)); adding whey to spent coffee grounds raised waste reduction, digestibility and bioconversion, larval protein rising from 34.18% to 40.11% DM (snippet; [J. Agric. Appl. Biol. 2024](https://jaabjournal.org/index.php/jaab/article/view/371)). **No yogurt-specific study was found**; the inference is that yogurt is useful as a minority wet component mixed into a dry absorbent carrier (afrecho or BSG) and risky as the main or starter substrate because free liquid can drown neonates and cause sour anaerobic trays.

*pH and fermentation.* Initial substrate pH of 4.0, 6.1, 7.5 or 9.5 affected weight on days 1, 3 and 5 but not at the end, because larvae raised pH to 8.9 to 9.4 from day 4 ([Meneguz et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0202591)). Adding fruit fermentation broth or commercial lactic-acid-bacteria broth to kitchen waste increased growth 9.96% and 7.96% after 6 days and reduced ammonia emission ([Li et al. 2023, Insect Science](https://doi.org/10.1111/1744-7917.13185)); the nursery microbiome was dominated by Lactobacillaceae ([Cerckel et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13534125/)). This is relevant to the fermented ensilaje in v2 (fermentation placeholder of 14 days, to be confirmed).

*Other benchmarks.* Prepupal protein was 399 to 431 g/kg DM across chicken feed, vegetable waste, digestate and restaurant waste, but ether extract ranged from 218 g/kg (digestate) to 386 g/kg (restaurant waste) and ash from 27 to 197 g/kg ([Spranghers et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1002/jsfa.8081)). On pig manure only 12.5% of manure DM was stored in larvae ([Parodi et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.wasman.2021.04.001)). The Gainesville reference diet is 30% alfalfa meal, 20% corn meal and 50% wheat bran at 70% moisture ([Yang, Tomberlin & Jordan 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/microbiology/articles/10.3389/fmicb.2021.601253/full)). Other Latin American work exists (Colombia: Parra Paz 2015 and Barragán-Fonseca 2018; Mexico, Honduras, Brazil, Peru and Ecuador titles were found but not read), but **no Venezuelan substrate study, no wheat-bran-only performance data and no C:N or lignin ceiling** were found. A useful formulation target is roughly 15 to 20% crude protein and 25 to 45% starch/sugar on a DM basis, with low lignin (inference).

### Moisture, ventilation and gases

Keep the mixed substrate at **about 70 to 75% water**: above 80% it becomes a slurry that cannot be sieved and is anaerobic; below 45 to 60% growth slows. Eawag treats feed at 60 to 90% water, dewaters above 80 to 85% (cloth bag, 24 hours, then another 24 hours) or blends with dry bulk material (rice husk, wheat bran), adds clean water below 70% (squeeze test: a few drops should emerge), shreds feed to under 1 to 2 cm and ventilates with fans in the last days to a crumbly, sievable residue ([Dortmans et al. 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)). In food waste at 70, 75 and 80% moisture, a 2.36 mm sieve worked at 70 to 75% but "sieving of the residue was not feasible" at 80%, lower moisture slowed growth, and survival was at least 95% throughout ([Cheng et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.wasman.2017.05.046)). At 45 to 85% moisture, larvae became prepupae only at 45 to 75%; at 45% growth was fastest (0.73/day, 13 days) but maximal dry weight lowest (88 mg) with 22% of substrate carbon lost as microbial CO₂, against 3% at 75% ([Bekker et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.wasman.2021.04.028)). Fibres with higher water-holding capacity reduce mortality, and particles above 3 mm gave 42 g fresh yield against 26 g at 1 mm or less ([Yakti et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10054678/)). Industrial gas work finds CO₂ dominant and ammonia avoidable if larvae are harvested before the CO₂ peak ([Parodi et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclepro.2020.122488)); Parodi found 1% of dietary N and 24% of C lost as gases and 16.8 g CO₂-eq per kg dry larvae in direct CH₄ plus N₂O. No quantitative guideline for CO₂ or NH₃ ppm, air-change rates or oxygen thresholds was found.

*Implication for Tryento.* In humid lowland air evaporation from trays is slow, so wet feeds (yogurt, fresh BSG at 75 to 80% water) push trays past 80% unless blended with afrecho or dewatered. Mix at 70 to 75%, hold back wet additions when the surface is shiny, run fans over trays for the last two to three days, and take a daily mid-depth core temperature with a probe; if the core exceeds 38 to 40 °C, split the tray, remove feed, add dry bulking material or delay the next feeding. Ammonia smell and pH near 9 late in the cycle are normal; a strong peak means harvest is due.

Bosch et al. 2020 propose standardised reporting: BCR = (larval DM out − larval DM in) / substrate DM in, with colony origin, rearing procedures and reference substrates reported ([Bosch et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3920/JIFF2019.0004)). To compare a wet FCR with published dry FCR use **FCR_dry = FCR_wet × (DM_feed / DM_larvae)**, with larval DM about 0.35 to 0.39 (measure your own); conversion % (wet) = 100 / FCR_wet; BCR (DM) ≈ 100 / FCR_dry when initial larval mass is negligible. Worked examples at 0.36 larval DM: BSG (DM about 0.22) at wet FCR 8 gives dry FCR 4.9 and BCR about 20%; afrecho logged dry (DM about 0.88) at wet FCR 3 gives dry FCR 7.3 and BCR about 14%. A feed logged as dry makes the wet FCR look excellent while the true conversion is mediocre, so the FCR is comparable across trays only if every feed type has a stored DM% and the app computes FCR on a DM basis, with added water counted as zero-DM input.

A worked Eawag-type tray (my inferences): 0.18 to 0.43 g of eggs seeds about 9,000 to 9,500 survivors; 15 kg of wet feed at 75% water gives 3.75 kg DM (about 31 mg DM/larva/day); 1.1 to 1.7 kg of wet clean larvae results (0.4 to 0.6 kg DM at 36% DM); wet FCR 9 to 14, conversion 7 to 11%, dry FCR 6.2 to 9.4, BCR 11 to 16%, and about 2.6 to 9.4 kg of larvae per gram of eggs. To reach 150 to 200 mg larvae, feed closer to 40 to 60 mg DM per larva per day. There is no peer-reviewed benchmark for "g larvae per g eggs", which depends on eggs per gram, hatch and survival. The "g larvae per g starter" KPI mainly reflects starter dose, so include starter DM in total feed for FCR instead.

Seeding-to-fasting is roughly 17 to 24 days from egg seeding at 28 to 30 °C on good feed (Eawag: 5-day nursery plus 12-day grow-out, harvest about 17 to 18 days after hatching; 20+ days on BSG alone), and fasting to separation adds 1 day with a 24-hour fast. Tryento's v2 plan reaches harvest on day 16 counting from the incubadora start ([LAB_SPEC §2.1](../LAB_SPEC.md)), at the fast end of that range.

### Harvest timing, separation and frass

Harvest at the end of feeding (late fifth or early sixth instar), when larvae are at peak weight and before darkening: "After 12 days of waste treatment… the larvae have reached their maximum weight, but have not yet transformed into prepupae. Their nutritional value is, therefore, at its maximum" ([Dortmans et al. 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)). Crude fat rose from day 4 to 14, peaking at 28.4% DM, with protein at a minimum of 38% at the 12-day larva; fat then fell from 24.2% (early prepupa) to 8.2% (late pupa) while protein peaked at 46.2% in the early pupa ([Liu et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0182601)). Prepupae carry about 32% protein, 37% lipids, 19% minerals and 9% chitin on a DM basis in one report (snippet; [Food Res. Int. 2018](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0963996917308669)). Sieves: about 3 mm manual, 5 mm vibrating, 2.36 mm in one food-waste study, 1 mm for 5-DOL; a slurry can be wet-harvested by spreading it on a 5 mm screen in bright light so photophobic larvae crawl through, then rinsing and drying for about a day on coco peat or sawdust, which cleans the skin and lets the gut empty. Self-harvesting ramps leave "a great share of the prepupae" in the material, so Eawag recommends batch containers. Residue frass is immature: microbial activity "still very high" competes for oxygen and nitrogen and can damage plants, so compost about two months, vermicompost or send wet residue to biogas. Whole-facility labour at Eawag is 3 workers and about 40 m² for 21 t of waste per week, or 1 worker for 9 t on a Monday/Wednesday/Friday schedule. No labour time per tray, and no separation efficiency by mesh size, were found.

*Implication for Tryento.* Trigger harvest when the first few percent of larvae turn dark or feeding stops (typically 12 to 14 days after transfer on good feed); dry the residue with fans or a dry-bran top dressing and sieve at 3 mm, using the light-avoidance wet method for sloppy trays; log frass as a separate output (wet mass and DM) and compost it before sale or use.

### Fasting: is 24 hours right?

Fasting empties the gut of substrate. Berggreen et al. fasted larvae for 0, 12, 24, 48 and 72 hours: "In BSFL, most residues were eliminated within 24 hours, while mealworms required up to 72 hours", so fasting is "an effective post-harvest strategy to reduce pesticide residues" ([Berggreen et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1163/23524588-bja10386)). A food-safety review calls pre-killing starvation "understudied but promising", citing one study in which "6 hours in water or 48 hours without food" cleared the gut with "improved organoleptic and nutritional qualities and reduced microbial load"; a mealworm study found no starvation effect on microbes; rinsing was ineffective against persistent parasite stages, and blanching has "superior antimicrobial effects" but does not eliminate spore-formers ([PMC11171339](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11171339/)). Starving 11-day-old larvae for 24 and 48 hours significantly decreased gut microbial diversity ([Yang et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/microbiology/articles/10.3389/fmicb.2021.601253/full)). Eawag rests rinsed larvae about a day on dry coco peat or sawdust ("gives them time to empty their gut"), and blanching also triggers emptying. A 2026 study found a critical weight of 83 mg above which larvae irreversibly commit to metamorphosis, while smaller larvae survive starvation for months ([Ohhara et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.asd.2026.101535)).

The evidence supports **24 hours as the default**: it matches the pesticide-clearance data and Eawag practice, while 48 hours adds little documented benefit, costs weight and fat, and (an untested inference) pushes larvae above the critical weight toward prepupation. Welfare reviews flag starvation, so use the minimum that achieves clearance. Fast on a clean, dry, absorbent non-food medium so larvae do not re-ingest frass. **No peer-reviewed percentage weight loss for a 24 hour fast was found**, so Tryento's before-and-after weights are a useful dataset in themselves. Note that the v1 default is 24 hours (allowed 1 to 168 hours) while the v2 protocol sets 48 hours (`horas_ayuno` 48, days 14 to 15) ([LAB_SPEC §2.1](../LAB_SPEC.md)); this difference is not resolved by the literature and is a candidate for an internal test.

### Problems and biosecurity

Tray failures are mostly physical: over-wet or over-deep feed causing anaerobic, sour trays; excess feed causing heat and flies; core overheating above about 40 °C. Biological threats are filth flies in unconsumed feed, ants, mites, phorids, and pupal parasitoids. Spore-formers (*B. cereus*, *C. perfringens*) were found at 1.3 to 6.6 log CFU/g and yeasts and moulds at 1.3 to 4.4 log CFU/g in larvae from different wastes (snippet; [Waste Management 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0956053X25000194)), and pesticides (fluopyram, etofenprox) in the substrate reduced larval mass produced ([Berggreen et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1163/23524588-bja10386)).

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix (inferred from cited mechanisms) |
|---|---|---|
| Sour or rotten smell, grey wet layer, larvae crowding the surface or climbing out | Too wet and/or too deep; anaerobic | Stop wet feeds; top-dress with afrecho; split the tray; ventilate |
| Very hot core (above 40 °C), larvae fleeing to edges, sudden die-off | Overfeeding, high density, warm room | Reduce feed per feeding; spread to more trays; improve airflow; feed in cooler hours |
| House-fly or fruit-fly maggots, adult flies around trays | Unconsumed feed, especially fruit, yogurt or sugary material | Feed to consumption; bury wet feed under a dry top layer; screen rooms |
| Mould mats on the surface | Surplus dry feed not worked; low larval density | Avoid large surplus feedings; match feed to larval number; stir mats |
| Escapes | Excess moisture, heat, ammonia, or larvae reaching prepupa | Smooth-walled trays with lip; control moisture and heat; harvest before prepupa |

Eawag's hygiene practice: batch (all-in/all-out) containers so a failure does not take down a whole unit; a central "dirty" feeding station; pressure-washing and drying of containers, screens and buckets after each batch; lab coats and gloves; rejection of intake waste containing "acids, solvents, pesticides, detergents and heavy metals"; water-filled ant traps under every table leg ("Four ant traps per love cage table"); frames and tables sprayed with 95% alcohol ([Dortmans et al. 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)). Nets over trays are reported as the only effective option against parasitoids in high-volume systems, and quarantine of new stock and cleaning of bins, hands and tools address mites and nematodes (WUR welfare review, as surfaced in search; [edepot.wur.nl](https://edepot.wur.nl/583853)). Suggested SOP for Tryento: clean and dry each tray between cycles; keep feed storage (fresh BSG and yogurt spoil within days in the heat) apart from the larval room; screen windows and doors; put water-moat feet on racks; separate the colony and pupation room from grow-out; record feed batch per tray for traceability. No BSF-specific disinfectant protocol, mite outbreak data for trays, or Latin American hygiene regulation was found.

## 5. Blanching, drying and packaging decide whether a dried larva is safe and sellable

### Killing method: direct oven-drying against blanching

Larouche et al. 2019 compared ten killing methods (blanching in boiling water 40 s; desiccation in a 60 °C air oven 30 min; freezing at −20 or −40 °C for 1 h; liquid nitrogen 40 s; asphyxiation with CO₂, N₂ or vacuum; grinding; high hydrostatic pressure), freeze-drying all samples before analysis ([Larouche et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6523926/)).

| Killing method | Total viable count (log CFU/g) | Enterobacteriaceae | Yeasts/moulds |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blanching | 5.6 | below 1.1 | below 2.1 |
| High hydrostatic pressure | 6.3 | below 1.1 | below 2.1 |
| Desiccation, 60 °C 30 min | 7.7 | 4.1 | 5.1 |
| Freezing −20 °C | 8.3 | 4.5 | 6.3 |
| Freezing −40 °C | 8.5 | 5.3 | 6.4 |
| Grinding | 8.5 | 4.7 | 6.1 |
| CO₂ asphyxiation | 9.8 | 7.5 | 5.8 |
| N₂ asphyxiation | 9.6 | 7.3 | 5.3 |
| Vacuum | 9.6 | 6.9 | 4.2 |

Even after blanching, the total viable count (5.6 log) was about 1.6 log above the Health Canada guideline of below 4 log for ready-to-eat foods, and *Listeria* spp. and clostridia were detected, so further decontamination such as drying is needed. Lipid oxidation was lowest after blanching (abstract: primary oxidation 4.6 ±0.7 mg cumene hydroperoxide eq/kg; secondary TBARS 1.0 ±0.1 mg MDA/kg; the full-text table shows slightly different primary values, and the abstract is authoritative), against TBARS 2.5 for desiccation and 1.6 to 2.0 for others; the asphyxiation methods reached 18.6 to 19.4 in primary oxidation. Blanching gave pH 8.7 (against 6.1 to 6.3 after asphyxiation), ΔE 6.2 in colour, and grinding gave the largest colour change (ΔE 17.5) and the darkest product. Blanching destroys lipase, whereas freezing only reduces it (Laval summary, snippet). A 2026 review reports blanching (100 °C, 40 s) cut microbial load by 3 to 5 log CFU/g (snippet; [Eur Food Res Technol 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00217-026-05050-7)). Heat killing gave less browning than other procedures (snippet; [Applied Sciences 2020](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/10/17/6099)). Blanching (90 °C, 40 s, 1:10 w/v larvae to water) followed by oven drying at 65 °C for 24 hours and mechanical pressing gave the longest fat Rancimat induction time (23.9 h), while blanching plus oven-drying plus hexane extraction had the worst initial peroxide value (11.7 mEq O₂/kg) ([Foods 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10142283/)). Killed, blanched larvae needed 4 °C (not 15 °C) to keep acceptable counts after 6 days, and vacuum packing gave no benefit for undried larvae ([SUSINCHAIN](https://susinchain.eu/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Best-practice-Storage-and-transport-of-BSF-larvae.pdf)).

On welfare, Larouche et al. call blanching "an acceptable way to kill insects because of its rapidity", and Barrett et al. (2023) list oven baking, microwaving, sand roasting, freezing, boiling/blanching, asphyxiation and grinding as used methods and judge last-instar dipteran larvae to have "substantial evidence for sentience" ([Barrett et al. 2023](https://dx.doi.org/10.3920/JIFF2022.0041)); whether oven-baking of live larvae ranks last in their table was not verified. Grinding can be humane only if killing is instantaneous, with a dedicated study of grinder settings ([Animal Welfare, PMC10951668](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10951668/), title only).

*Implication for Tryento.* Live larvae going straight into the oven resemble Larouche's "desiccation" treatment, which left about 2 log more total viable count, about 3 log more Enterobacteriaceae, and 2.5 times the TBARS of blanching (the comparison is close, not identical: Tryento's run dries fully, whereas Larouche's 30 minute oven step was followed by freeze-drying). It is also the slow-heating route welfare reviewers rate poorly. Adding a 40 second boiling-water blanch, then draining or spinning off surface water, is the single change best supported on microbes, oxidation, colour and welfare; blanching probably also inactivates phenoloxidase for more consistent golden colour (inference). If blanched larvae cannot go into the oven the same day, hold them at 4 °C or below. Whether the oven step alone is a validated kill step for microbial safety has not been tested by Tryento and should be treated as unvalidated until Salmonella and Enterobacteriaceae results exist.

### Drying to golden and crunchy

Fresh larvae are about 70 to 77% water with aw of about 0.9; aw below 0.6 inhibits all bacteria and yeasts, and dried larvae are defined at no more than 10% moisture (AAFCO). A fruit-waste reference batch went from 76.5 ±3% moisture and aw 0.9 to 2.6 ±0.8% moisture and aw 0.4 ±0.1 (36.4% protein, 36.2% fat, 13.5% fibre, 7.0% ash, as-is) ([Eawag drying factsheet](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/schwerpunkte/swm/Practical_knowhow_on_BSF/bsf_factsheet_drying.pdf)). **Dry-to-fresh yield "lies typically between 25-35%"; "an output yield higher than 35% implies that the drying process is not yet completed"** (same source). Yield = (1 − fresh moisture) / (1 − final moisture): with 76.5% fresh moisture, 5% final gives about 24.7% and 10% gives about 26.1%; with 70% fresh, 5% gives about 31.6% and 10% about 33.3% (my calculation). A lot above about 35% is almost certainly under-dried; below about 22% suggests very wet fresh larvae or fat and mass lost by over-roasting.

Texture is set by the heating rate: rapid dehydration builds steam pressure until it releases, giving "puffed and crispy larvae" of higher market value in pet food, while slow dehydration shrinks the shell into a "hard and stiff" product ([Eawag drying factsheet](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/schwerpunkte/swm/Practical_knowhow_on_BSF/bsf_factsheet_drying.pdf)).

| Parameter | Microwave | Sand roasting, pan | Sand roasting, drum | Oven |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Product | crispy, puffed, yellowish | crispy, puffed, yellowish/brown | crispy, puffed, yellowish/brown | hard, stiff, dark brown |
| Bulk density | 110 to 130 g/L | 110 to 130 g/L | 110 to 130 g/L | 220 to 230 g/L |
| Max product temperature | 180 °C | 180 °C | 150 °C | 65 °C |
| Batch | 0.25 kg | 0.7 kg | 4 kg | 30 kg |
| Time | 15 min | 15 min | 30 min | 24 h |
| Throughput | 0.9 kg/h | 2.5 kg/h | 6.2 kg/h | 1.2 kg/h |
| Energy per kg dried | "3,700 Watt" (as printed, probably Wh) | 0.9 kg LPG | 0.7 kg LPG | 0.7 kg LPG |
| Labour | high | high | medium | low |

The oven method: kill first ("e.g. by rapid boiling"), dry at 65 °C with a fan, load 3 kg of freshly boiled larvae per 65×45×3.5 cm mesh tray (4 mm mesh) on 10 trays, dry in one 16 hour night phase plus two 4 hour day phases with fixed tray-position shuffling, and end at "a hard and dry texture, shrunken shape". Microwave: 250 g fresh larvae, 3 cycles of 5 min at 1000 W with 20 s venting between; excess steam gives "damp and non-puffed larvae". Sand roasting: 700 g sand (0.8 to 1.5 mm) preheated to about 200 °C, 700 g larvae stirred for about 15 min, then sieved ([Eawag drying factsheet](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/schwerpunkte/swm/Practical_knowhow_on_BSF/bsf_factsheet_drying.pdf)). A reversible-airflow flatbed dryer at 70 °C took 12.8 hours to bring larvae from 73.0% to 8.6% moisture (snippet); thin-layer drying at 40 to 70 °C followed the Page model (activation energy 48.66 kJ/mol; snippet; [Food Biophysics 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11483-024-09867-1)). In-vitro digestibility of full-fat larvae was "fairly comparable" across hot-air, microwave, freeze-dried and infrared drying, but microwave-dried material had higher free fatty acids and acid value (snippet; [Agriculture 2024](https://doi.org/10.3390/agriculture14101792)). Dielectric drying cut aw effectively and retained microbiological quality (snippet; [JIFF 2025](https://brill.com/view/journals/jiff/11/17/article-p21_4.xml)). A microwave-dryer vendor claims conventional dryers use 5 to 10 kWh per kg of dried larvae (vendor; [Kerone](https://kerone.com/microwave-drying-revolutionizes-black-soldier-fly-bsf-processing/)). Brazil's MAPA requires documented dryer operation, monitored minimum parameters and "studies showing that the dehydration temperature and time are applied uniformly throughout the equipment" ([MAPA Ofício-Circular 33/2023](https://wikisda.agricultura.gov.br/manual_fiscalizacao_aa/manual_autocontrole_aa/circ_2023-33_aa_processamento_de_insetos.pdf)).

*Implication for Tryento.* Eawag's loading of 3 kg per 0.2925 m² tray is about 10 kg of fresh larvae per m² in a single layer on mesh; Tryento uses solid metal trays, so it must shuffle positions or stir. A golden, puffed, crunchy product needs fast, high-intensity heating (microwave with steam venting, or roasting at 150 to 180 °C product temperature); low-temperature oven drying (65 °C, 24 h) yields dark, dense, hard larvae. A hybrid that is untested in the literature is blanch, partly oven-dry, then a short high-temperature finish; scorching risk rises above about 180 °C and with thick unstirred layers on hot metal. The link between crunch grade and moisture is unstudied. Hypothesis to check: "Blando" is under-dried (moisture above 10%, aw above 0.6), "Muy Crujiente" is a few percent moisture at aw about 0.3 to 0.5 (Eawag's crispy product was 2.6% and aw 0.4), and "Tostado" is a colour and odour defect, not a moisture state. Oven energy of 0.7 kg LPG per kg dried is about 9 kWh of fuel per kg (assuming about 46 MJ/kg LPG).

### Quality control and microbial criteria

The clearest numerical microbial standard is Reg. (EU) 142/2011 Annex X for insect processed animal protein: *Salmonella* absent in 25 g; Enterobacteriaceae n = 5, c = 2, m = 10, M = 300 CFU/g ([Reg. 142/2011 Annex X](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2011/142/annex/X)). EU frass rules (Reg. 2021/1925) require at least 70 °C for at least 60 minutes, *E. coli* or Enterococcaceae below 3.0 log CFU/g and *Salmonella* absent in storage (snippet; [PMC12120769](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12120769/)). Brazil's MAPA circular lists SOP contents: receiving checks for dead or decomposing larvae and atypical odour or colour; the separation technique and monitoring frequency; the cooking step (temperature, time, monitoring); the drying step (minimum parameters and proof of uniform heat); finished-product microbiological specifications based on literature or international legislation; temperature-monitored storage; supplier qualification; and substrate specification ([MAPA Ofício-Circular 33/2023](https://wikisda.agricultura.gov.br/manual_fiscalizacao_aa/manual_autocontrole_aa/circ_2023-33_aa_processamento_de_insetos.pdf)). Oxidation is measured as peroxide value (meq O₂/kg fat) and p-anisidine value ([SUSINCHAIN](https://susinchain.eu/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Best-practice-Storage-and-transport-of-BSF-larvae.pdf)). A Colombian study checked larva meal against ICA criteria and found counts below limits (snippet; [Rev. Fac. Nal. Agr. Medellín 2004](http://www.scielo.org.co/scielo.php?script=sci_arttext&pid=S0304-28472004000200009)).

Suggested QC tiers for Tryento (inference): **every lot** yield within 25 to 35% with a back-calculated final moisture, colour against a printed reference card, a standard crunch test, smell (no rancid, sour or ammonia note), and a visual check for sand, frass, mould and grey dead larvae; **every lot if affordable** moisture by halogen analyser at about 105 °C (target at most 10%, probably at most 5% for "Muy Crujiente") and aw (below 0.60); **monthly or quarterly at an external lab** *Salmonella* in 25 g, Enterobacteriaceae (EU limits as the benchmark), total plate count, moulds and yeasts, and peroxide value on extracted fat at 0, 90 and 180 days; **on each new substrate** Cd and Pb in larvae. Tie the approved/rejected decision to objective cut-offs (yield, aw, colour card) as well as the crunch grade. No regulatory peroxide-value limit for dried insects, no insect-specific mould/yeast/TPC limits in EU law, and no Venezuelan COVENIN feed microbiology standard were found.

### Shelf life, packaging and the 180-day best-before

Once aw is below 0.6, dried larvae are microbiologically stable, and shelf life is limited by oxidation of their 30 to 36% fat. In dried ground BSFL stored six months at 23.6 ±2.7 °C and 57.6 ±7.0% RH, chemical deterioration was **1.5 times faster in woven polypropylene sacks and 1.2 times faster in polyethylene bags** than in screw-lid plastic containers ([Kamau et al. 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29579943/)). Blanched, microwave-dried BSFL packed under 100% N₂ and held at 40 °C for three days kept oxidation far below international threshold values, and N₂ flushing "may thus serve as a more cost-efficient way to preserve dried insects compared to freezing" ([SUSINCHAIN](https://susinchain.eu/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Best-practice-Storage-and-transport-of-BSF-larvae.pdf)). Frozen, freeze-dried, mechanically pressed larval fat showed essentially no change in peroxide value over 24 weeks, and oven-dried material had higher Rancimat stability than freeze-dried (15.9 against 4.6 h) ([Foods 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10142283/)). Retailers claim "up to 6 months" in a dark, cool cabinet with a tight lid, or a year frozen or refrigerated, and one claims two years (vendor; [Rainbow Mealworms](https://www.rainbowmealworms.net/dehdrated-black-soldier-fly-larvae/); [Mealworms by the Pound](https://www.mealwormsbythepound.com/products/dried-mealworms-11-lb-wpz8j-6pe3f)).

*Implication for Tryento.* **180 days is plausible if aw is below about 0.5 at packing, the bag is a high-barrier laminate (for example PET/Al/PE or metallised film) rather than plain PE or woven PP, headspace oxygen is removed with an N₂ flush or an oxygen absorber, and stock is kept cool and dark.** At Venezuelan ambient temperatures of 28 to 35 °C and thin PE bags, oxidation will run faster than in Kamau's roughly 24 °C trial, and 90 to 120 days would be more cautious. No shelf-life study of whole (not ground) full-fat dried BSFL at tropical temperatures, none testing oxygen absorbers, and no scientific basis for a 180-day date specifically, were found; whole larvae may oxidise more slowly than meal but this is unverified. Validate in-house: keep retained samples from each lot and measure peroxide value, p-anisidine value and smell at 0, 90 and 180 days plus an accelerated test at about 40 °C.

### Composition of the product

Dried BSFL is roughly 36 to 44% crude protein and 22 to 37% fat on a DM basis, exceptionally high in calcium (5 to 8% DM on some substrates), lysine-rich (6 to 8% of protein) and dominated by lauric acid. Feedipedia gives crude protein 40 to 44% DM, ether extract 15 to 25% on poultry manure, 28% on swine manure, 35% on cattle manure and 42 to 49% on oil-rich food waste, ash 11 to 28%, Ca 5 to 8%, P 0.6 to 1.5% ([Feedipedia](https://www.feedipedia.org/node/16388)). A 2026 aquaculture review lists crude protein 26 to 47%, lipid 18 to 48%, crude fibre 3.6 to 16.2%, ash 2.7 to 15.5%, and warns that chitin can hinder nutrient uptake at high inclusion ([Frontiers in Aquaculture 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/aquaculture/articles/10.3389/faquc.2026.1862744/full)). Commercial whole dried larvae (4th to 5th instar) in a cat trial were 42% protein, 36.2% fat, Ca 1.49%, P 0.81% (DM) ([PMC12023938](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12023938/)); whole dehydrated BSFL in a laying-hen trial were 34.9% crude protein and 30.3% ether extract as-fed ([PMC11394493](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11394493/)); a US label reads protein at least 30%, fat at least 33%, fibre at most 8%, moisture at most 10% (as-fed). IVIC found 56.06% protein (basis not stated) in Venezuelan pupae reared on cafeteria waste ([IVIC via Telecom](https://telecom.gob.ve/resultados-de-estudio-con-mosca-soldado-negra-indican-que-es-un-potencial-para-alimento-balanceado-de-animales/)). Fat and ash depend strongly on substrate (see Section 4). For a full-fat whole dried larva at about 5% moisture, realistic label guarantees would be crude protein at least 35% and crude fat at least 25 to 30%, to be confirmed by proximate analysis per substrate mix, since fat can swing by 15 or more percentage points. Fibrous spent grain, bran and bagasse will probably give lower fat than oil-rich food waste, dairy may raise it, and the high calcium suits laying-hen and reptile supplements. No composition data for larvae reared on BSG, bagasse or dairy waste, a larva-versus-prepupa comparison, or chitin % DM were captured.

### Products, markets and prices

Whole dried larvae sell mainly as treats for backyard poultry, wild birds, reptiles and fish. A 22 lb (10 kg) bag of imported dried BSFL was listed at US$109.99, about **US$11/kg**, in the US ([Mealworms by the Pound](https://www.mealwormsbythepound.com/products/dried-mealworms-11-lb-wpz8j-6pe3f)); Spanish retail is about €6.2/kg (5 kg for €31; snippet; [Criainsecta](https://criainsecta.com/tienda/larva-bsf/larva-bsf-deshidratada/larva-mosca-soldado-negra-bsf-deshidratado-5kg/)); aggregator figures for B2B dried larvae are US$1 to 4/kg, with Vietnamese bulk at US$0.50 to 0.60/kg for 10 t or more and small-quantity retail from about US$15/kg (low reliability; [Accio](https://www.accio.com/plp/bsf-larvae-price-per-kg)). Tractor Supply stocks several 5 to 10 lb poultry-treat products, confirming the backyard channel. In Colombia, MercadoLibre sells 200 g packs of live young larvae (about 8,000 to 10,000). The benchmark competitor in commodity feed is fishmeal at **US$1,840/t (February 2026) and US$1,837/t (March 2026)** ([IndexMundi/World Bank](https://www.indexmundi.com/commodities/?commodity=fish-meal)). A 2023 EU techno-economic analysis put BSF meal production cost at €3.80 to 5.20/kg against €1.10/kg for soybean meal and €1.60/kg for fishmeal (snippet; [ScienceDirect 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2949824424001587)). Rabobank projected insect protein demand rising from about 10,000 t to 500,000 t by 2030 ([Feedinfo](https://www.feedinfo.com/our-content/insight-demand-for-insect-protein-could-reach-500k-tonnes-by-2030-rabobank/225041)); a market-research figure claims US$341.8 M (2024) to US$5.01 B (2034) ([market.us](https://market.us/report/black-soldier-fly-larvae-market/)); such forecasts are typically optimistic. AAFCO now allows dried BSFL in adult dog and adult cat food, and a work group is extending coverage to wild birds and exotic species ([AAFCO minutes Jan 2025](https://www.aafco.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/IDC_Minutes_MY_2025.pdf)). In Venezuela, ACAV and IVIC run a joint project to feed fish, poultry, pigs and small livestock near the Barinas and Portuguesa reservoirs ([ACAV](https://www.acav.gob.ve/2023/11/06/fortalecimiento-del-proyecto-de-mosca-soldado-negro-hermetia-illucens-entre-acav-e-ivic/)), and UCV/MIZA report feed demand exceeding supply ([FENAVI](https://fenavi.com.ve/la-mosca-soldado-negra-revoluciono-la-produccion-agroindustrial/)).

A small Venezuelan artisanal product will struggle on price in commodity meal but can compete in local premium niches (backyard layers, fighting-cock and ornamental-bird keepers, reptile and aquarium hobbyists, pet shops) where golden, crunchy, low-dust, clean-smelling product justifies a premium; competitors are dried mealworms in treat and reptile channels, fishmeal and soybean meal in compound feed, and probably local dried shrimp and fish-based treats (unverified). No Venezuelan price data (dried larvae, live larvae, meal, frass), no Mexico or Brazil retail prices, and no BSF oil, chitin or frass prices were found.

### Feeding-trial evidence and customer guidance

Moderate inclusion is safe and often neutral to positive: about 5 to 10% for laying hens, broilers and weaned piglets, 25 to 50% fishmeal replacement for trout and tilapia, 10 to 25% for shrimp; higher levels (18% full-fat BSFL in layers, 15% or more in some broiler trials) tend to depress intake, egg output or FCR. Whole dehydrated BSFL at 5% of DM intake for 15 weeks in hens aged 23 to 38 weeks gave no difference in egg production, weight, mass or FCR, higher Haugh units early, and lower plasma cholesterol and triglycerides at 32 weeks ([PMC11394493](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11394493/)). Free-range hens on 0, 10 or 18% full-fat dried BSFL for 17 weeks: controls laid more and bigger eggs on less feed than hens on 18% (snippet; [Agriculture 2024](https://doi.org/10.3390/agriculture14010031)). Broiler meta-analyses find no significant overall effect on intake, gain or FCR at 5.7 ±2.3% and 15.5 ±6.0% (snippets; [PMC13382029](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13382029/); [Ital. J. Anim. Sci. 2023](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1828051X.2023.2194909)). Feedipedia summaries: layers 3, 6 or 9% defatted meal maintained production; broilers 5, 10, 15% (15% impaired FCR in one study); weaned pigs 5 or 10%; salmonids 25 or 50% fishmeal replacement; tilapia 10 to 100%; catfish best at 25% ([Feedipedia](https://www.feedipedia.org/node/16388)). Tilapia up to 100% replacement without adverse effects (Tippayadara 2021) but best growth at 25 to 50% (Muin 2017); Pacific white shrimp 10 to 20% replacement kept growth, declining at 30% ([Frontiers in Aquaculture 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/aquaculture/articles/10.3389/faquc.2026.1862744/full)). Cats: 20% dried whole BSFL in queen and kitten diets gave similar intake and litter size, with kitten protein digestibility of 84.5% against 75.4% ([PMC12023938](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12023938/)).

*Customer guidance (inference).* For backyard layers, treat-level feeding (about 5% of intake, roughly 5 to 6 g per hen per day for a hen eating about 110 g/day, an assumed intake) matches the neutral-to-positive evidence; advise against replacing more than about 10% of the ration with full-fat larvae; partial fishmeal replacement at 25 to 50% is well supported for fish. Most trials use defatted meal, so evidence for whole full-fat larvae is thinner but consistent at 5 to 10%. No Latin American trials with local substrates and no tropical laying-hen trials at 10% or less were captured.

### Regulation and contaminants

The EU and Brazil have the most detailed rules; both require heat-processed insects only, a ruminant feed ban, controlled substrates and registered processing sites. In the EU, Reg. 2017/893 lifted the TSE ban for insect processed animal protein in aquaculture feed, and Reg. 2021/1372 (in force 7 September 2021) added poultry and pig feed, provided PAP is made in approved plants "dedicated exclusively to the production of products derived from farmed insects" ([IPIFF FAQ](https://ipiff.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/Apr-13-2021-Frequently-asked-questions-insect-PAPs.pdf); [EUR-Lex 2021/1372](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2021/1372/oj/eng)). IPIFF states that killed whole insects not processed under Reg. 1069/2009 "may not be used as feed for food-producing animals"; my reading is that whole dried larvae for hens or pigs in the EU must be heat-processed in an approved plant, and the status of live insects fed to poultry was not verified. EU substrates: vegetable-origin materials plus milk, eggs, honey, rendered fat and non-ruminant blood products are allowed; slaughterhouse and rendering products, **manure**, **catering waste** and unsold products containing meat or fish are prohibited ([IPIFF](https://ipiff.org/insects-eu-legislation-general/)). EFSA's 2015 risk profile found prion hazard in non-processed insects equal to or lower than in currently authorised proteins "as long as the insects are fed on substrates that do not harbour material of ruminant or human (manure) origin", with contaminant-transfer data "very limited" ([EFSA 2015](https://efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2903/j.efsa.2015.4257)).

In the US, AAFCO 60.117 "Dried Black Soldier Fly Larvae" became Official in January 2025 for "finfish, poultry, and swine feed, and in adult dog food, and adult cat food", raised on feed-grade feedstock, dried to at most 10% moisture and labelled with minimum crude protein and fat as-fed ([AAFCO minutes Jan 2025](https://www.aafco.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/IDC_Minutes_MY_2025.pdf)). Brazil (MAPA) authorised BSF larva meal in November 2020 (IN 110/2020; snippet) and Ofício-Circular 33/2023 requires processors to register as "PROCESSADOR DE INSETOS", bans raw unheated insects and ruminant use (labelled "Uso Proibido na Alimentação de Ruminantes"), allows plant by-products, milk products, eggs, honey, rendered fats and pre-consumer non-ruminant food residues, prohibits table scraps, leachate, faeces, raw animal residues and **reuse of substrate**, and does not require rearing-only farms to register in the feed area ([MAPA circular](https://wikisda.agricultura.gov.br/manual_fiscalizacao_aa/manual_autocontrole_aa/circ_2023-33_aa_processamento_de_insetos.pdf)). Argentina's SENASA Resolution 1039/2024 brought insect-derivative plants into inspection regulation, with rearing farms registering in RENSPA (snippets; [Argentina.gob.ar](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/noticias/se-incorporan-en-el-reglamento-de-inspeccion-plantas-que-elaboran-derivados-de-insectos)). Colombia's ICA registers feed products and manufacturers, but no insect-specific resolution was found ([ICA](https://www.ica.gov.co/areas/pecuaria/servicios/alimentos-para-animales.aspx)); Mexico and Peru were not resolved.

**No Venezuelan regulation specific to insects as feed was found.** INSAI Providencia Administrativa 023/2016 (Gaceta Oficial N° 40.981, 5 September 2016) sets the registration procedure for products including "Alimentos balanceados para consumo animal (ABA), premezclas, materias primas" (requirements per secondary transcription: technical sheet, quality-control certificate, INSAI-format label, and possibly composition certificate and environmental plan), but is aimed at re-registering existing SASA products and shows no explicit route for new products such as insects ([Pandectas Digital](https://pandectasdigital.blogspot.com/2016/09/procedimiento-para-el-registro-de.html); [Legis Venezuela](http://www.legis.com.ve/BancoConocimiento/N/noticia_080916-1/noticia_080916-1.asp?Miga=1&CodSeccion=25)). INSAI's SIGMAV issues movement guides (non-official source). The likely route is registering dried larvae as a "materia prima" or feed product with INSAI; the status is uncertain and should be confirmed directly. Voluntarily aligning with EU and Brazilian rules (no manure, catering or table-scrap substrates; no substrate reuse; heat treatment before drying; a "Prohibido su uso en la alimentación de rumiantes" label; *Salmonella* and Enterobacteriaceae testing) is the most defensible approach. All of Tryento's substrates (BSG, bagasse, wheat bran, yogurt/dairy) are allowed under both EU and Brazilian rules. Suggested label contents: species, "whole dried larvae", guaranteed minimum crude protein and fat, maximum fibre and moisture (as-fed), target species, ruminant warning, lot, best-before date and storage instructions.

Cadmium is the key chemical hazard: in a controlled study Cd accumulated in larvae in every treatment with a **bioaccumulation factor above 6**, larval Cd exceeded EU limits even at lower exposures, lead had BAF about 1.2 to 1.4, arsenic 0.5 to 0.6, and the authors say EU limits "should be revised per insect species" ([Van der Fels-Klerx et al. 2016](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0166186)). A 2023 review found Cd mainly retained in the larval body (up to about 93%), while Pb, Hg and As are mostly excreted (snippet; [Environ Monit Assess 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10661-023-11186-w)). Directive 2002/32/EC limits are expressed at 12% moisture (Hg 0.1 mg/kg in animal-origin materials); exact EU Cd and Pb limits were not verified. Mycotoxins (aflatoxin from spent grain or bran), pesticides and veterinary residues were not researched. Test Cd and Pb in larvae at least once per substrate source and after any supplier change.

## 6. The industry shakeout favours lean tropical farms

### Failures, survivors and market size

The Western mega-factory model has largely failed or retreated. Ÿnsect (France, mealworm) raised over $500M, laid off about two-thirds of staff in June 2025 and was put into judicial liquidation in early December 2025 ([AgFunderNews, 3 Dec 2025](https://agfundernews.com/judicial-liquidation-for-ynsect-as-insect-farming-sector-struggles-to-become-competitive)). ENORM (Denmark, BSF; €50M raised 2022) entered reconstruction in April 2025 and was declared bankrupt in November 2025 (The Fish Site dates ENORM and Inseco closures to October 2025, a conflict); Inseco (South Africa) stopped in 2025 citing loadshedding, poor hiring and scaling "too quickly"; Aspire (Canada, crickets) entered receivership in September 2025 partly on equipment design and copying a Texas design into Ontario's climate; Beta Hatch closed in 2023; Agronutris sought protection in January 2025 ([AgFunderNews, Mar 2026](https://agfundernews.com/insect-ag-recalibrates-after-a-brutal-shakeout-where-are-the-key-players-now); [The Fish Site, Dec 2025](https://thefishsite.com/articles/insect-meal-can-the-sector-still-deliver-on-its-2030-promise)). AgriProtein (South Africa/UK) raised $105M in 2018, planned 100 factories by 2024, lost $38.9M in 2019 and went into administration in February 2021, with assets sold for $10M; stated causes were over-expansion, labour-intensive plants, feedstock disruption and feedstocks not properly characterised ("not all wastes are the same") ([Lux Research, 2023](https://luxresearchinc.com/blog/what-happened-to-agriprotein/)). A market-research blog says commodity buyers want BSF meal under about $2/kg while many Western producers need about $4/kg to break even (market-research firm; [Meticulous Research](https://www.meticulousresearch.com/blog/378/why-insect-farming-startups-failed)). Sector venture funding fell from $151.9M (2024) to $88.45M (2025).

Survivors: Protix produces 17,000 to 18,000 t/yr of live larvae with a 4× expansion planned, but paused its Tyson Nebraska plant in 2026 (designed for 70,000 t/yr of live-larvae equivalent from 250,000 t/yr of waste); its CEO said Southeast Asia is "much more cost-effective" on energy, land, construction and labour ([GlobalPETS, 1 Apr 2026](https://globalpetindustry.com/news/protix-and-tyson-foods-halt-investment-in-us-insect-facility/)). InnovaFeed's Nesle plant produces about 1,000 t of insects a week (company figure), it raised €51M in June 2026, cut 60 of 300 staff and targets profitability at Nesle by end-2027 (company claim; [AgFunderNews, 5 Jun 2026](https://agfundernews.com/innovafeed-raises-59m-scales-production-cuts-rd-headcount-as-insect-ag-hits-industrialization-phase)); it paused its Decatur, Illinois plant in August 2025. Lean survivors (company-sourced): Entobel (Vietnam) 300 to 400 t/month of meal; Protenga (Malaysia/Singapore), profitable and "capital-light"; Full Circle Biotechnology (Thailand) 7,000 t/yr, "semi-automated over full automation"; nextProtein (France/Tunisia) with a founder saying a "European-only model... faces nearly insurmountable structural challenges"; Volare (Finland) 5,000 t/yr; FreezeM (Israel) selling breeding-as-a-service to 40 clients ([AgFunderNews, Mar 2026](https://agfundernews.com/insect-ag-recalibrates-after-a-brutal-shakeout-where-are-the-key-players-now)). Nutrition Technologies (Malaysia) raised $28M and ships to Europe, Asia and South America; EnviroFlight (US, Darling) opened Maysville, Kentucky with 900 t/yr Phase 1 capacity. Lessons distilled by AgFunder: full automation is not essential, climate control is highly energy-intensive, pioneers underestimated scaling complexity, and viable models use residual streams with little value.

Physical supply is small: a joint InnovaFeed-Protix white paper (company sources) says global insect meal supply is "more than 15,000 metric tons" ([Food Ingredients First, 28 Sep 2026](https://www.foodingredientsfirst.com/news/insect-protein-scale-carbon-cost.html)). Market-size forecasts differ by roughly ten times: US$1.01bn (2025) to US$2.22bn (2030) at 17% ([Business Research Company](https://www.thebusinessresearchcompany.com/report/black-soldier-fly-global-market-report)); US$3.4bn by 2030 at 34.7% ([Meticulous, 2021](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2021/09/13/2295798/0/en/Black-Soldier-Fly-BSF-Market-is-Expected-to-reach-3-4-billion-by-2030-at-a-CAGR-of-34-7-During-the-Forecast-Period-of-2021-to-2030-Exclusive-Report-by-Meticulous-Research.html)); US$317.2M (2025) to US$546.8M (2033) at 8.1% (snippet; [DataM](https://www.datamintelligence.com/research-report/black-soldier-fly-market)). At roughly US$2 to 3.5/kg, 15 kt of meal is a market of tens of millions of dollars, so the billion-dollar figures must include live larvae, pet treats, frass, oil, equipment and services, or be inflated; they should not be treated as physical demand.

Chile's ByBug raised $1.4M for recombinant proteins in engineered BSF ([AgFunderNews](https://agfundernews.com/bybug-black-soldier-flies-can-deliver-cheaper-more-scalable-recombinant-protein-production)). ### Farm models, facility design and climate

There is a continuum from unheated backyard bins, through Eawag-style semi-open facilities of 1 to 5 t of waste per day run on ambient tropical climate with passive measures and 1 to 3 rearing staff, to modular "capital-light" plants of a few thousand t/yr in Southeast Asia, to fully climate-controlled vertical factories. The Eawag model is based on a one-tonne-per-day facility in Indonesia in operation for over two years ([Dortmans et al. 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)); the second edition covers 5 t/day. Climate management is mainly passive: a closed ventilated rearing room, sunlight for the love cages, a sheltered area for treatment containers, ventilation frames between stacked crates, and fans in the last days. Eawag capacity: 21 t waste/week and about 14 million 5-DOL/week with 3 workers and about 40 m² (every day, full capacity); 10.5 t/week, 2 workers, about 35 m² (half capacity); 9 t/week, 1 worker (Mon/Wed/Fri). ICIPE scales BSF with push-pull vegetable cropping in Kenya, Rwanda and Uganda through two central egg-production hubs supplying smallholders (hub-and-spoke) ([IKEA Foundation](https://ikeafoundation.org/grants/scaling-regenerative-black-soldier-fly-farming-along-with-vegetable-push-pull-cropping-systems-in-rural-kenya-rwanda-and-uganda/)). A UK micro-unit costs about £100,000; the Frontier Economics model uses large (250,000 t waste/yr, £50M, 40 FTE, about 12,500 t protein), medium (70,000 t, £16M, 25 FTE, about 3,500 t) and small (20,000 t, £7.5M, 10 FTE, about 1,000 t) sites from operator interviews ([Frontier Economics 2023](https://www.frontier-economics.com/media/o12jxgjt/frontier-economics_delivering-insect-feed-at-scale_final-report_01-12-23.pdf)).

Eawag's layout separates waste receiving and pre-processing, a treatment unit, a rearing unit, harvesting, a lab, storage and a buffer zone; rules of thumb are **50 m² for the nursery plus 100 m² per tonne of incoming waste per day**, and a 2 t/day sample layout is about 36 m² receiving, 180 m² treatment, 50 m² rearing, 36 m² nursery, 36 m² harvesting, 18 m² lab, 18 m² storage and about 25 m² buffer. Siting: cheap fresh waste, year-round roads, away from dense neighbourhoods and downwind of residences. Treatment stacks hold 36 larveros on pallets with 6 ventilation frames. Love cages of sturdy mosquito netting hold 6,000 to 10,000 flies, are used 6 days and washed at 30 °C; dark cages have 16 pupation containers. By that rule a 1 t/day site needs roughly 150 to 200 m² built area (my inference). IPIFF's Guide on Good Hygiene Practices (9th version, November 2022) covers feeding through transport; HACCP is expected of food/feed operators but voluntary for insect primary production ([IPIFF GHP](https://ipiff.org/good-hygiene-practices/)). No m²-per-tonne for industrial vertical farms, no costed containerised units, no tropical biosecurity data (ants, predators, mites) and no smallholder yield data from Kenya's warm lowlands were found.

For grow-out, the main thermal problem is heat produced inside the substrate, not cold: substrate reached 45 to 50 °C in large trials and 21 to 47 °C in industrial Portugal with ambient air at 21.8 to 29.2 °C ([Klammsteiner et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12205609/); [Oliveira et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13116064/)). Optimal larval growth is about 27 to 35 °C, the cycle completes within about 15 to 37 °C, and 38 to 40 °C causes mortality (snippets). Eawag notes "the intensity of active ventilation depends on the air humidity and the moisture content of the starting material". AgFunder calls climate control "highly energy-intensive"; Inseco was hurt by grid outages ([AgFunderNews](https://agfundernews.com/insect-ag-recalibrates-after-a-brutal-shakeout-where-are-the-key-players-now)). For a Venezuelan lowland site the risk is substrate-core overheating and condensation in stacks, not cold, so the cage heater is the least-needed actuator in grow-out. Evaporative cooling (misting) is cheap but works against the humidity cap; in mating cages set to 72% ±3 RH the atomiser adds both cooling and humidity, so the 9-case rule will sometimes face a cooling-versus-RH conflict that should be an explicit logged decision (see [LAB_SPEC §4](../LAB_SPEC.md)). Grid reliability matters: a UPS for the Pi and router, power-loss logging and an "outage in progress" alert are proportionate. CO₂ is the best cheap proxy for larval metabolic activity and ventilation adequacy. No published CO₂ or NH₃ setpoints or air-change rates, no kWh per kg for ventilation or cooling and no heat output per kg of larvae were found.

### Sensing, IoT and automation

Industrial leaders automate neonate dosing and counting by machine vision (Entocycle claims 3,000 larvae per second at 96 to 100% accuracy and a doser of up to 4,000 neonates/s; a trial at EnviroFlight gave 4.52% mean absolute percentage error, company claims; [The Manufacturer, 2025](https://www.themanufacturer.com/articles/siemens-partners-with-entocycle-to-digitalise-insect-farming/)), feeding and chamber climate; research systems add substrate-temperature probes, thermal cameras and gas logging. Klammsteiner et al. logged substrate temperature every minute with DS18B20 probes and call it essential for reproducibility ([iScience 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12205609/)); a UC Riverside "smart bin" used thermal imaging and automatic aeration to produce larvae "on par with manual techniques" ([arXiv 2207.04335](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.04335)); ESP32/DHT22/Firebase builds resemble Tryento's stack but are simple loops without alerting.

The DHT22 is the weak link. An independent bench test found DHT22/AM2302 with "typical errors around 5%" RH, strong temperature-dependent drift, and of six units an "average life expectancy… around a year. Four are now dead", while the BME280 met "±3% at the reference temperature of 25 °C" with almost no temperature drift ([kandrsmith.org](https://www.kandrsmith.org/RJS/Misc/Hygrometers/overview_conclusions.html)); vendor blogs claim SHT31 at ±2% RH with a heater that drives off condensation ([Zbotic](https://zbotic.in/sht31-vs-dht22-vs-bme280-best-humidity-sensor-for-your-project/)). On/off control without hysteresis chatters near the setpoint; practical controllers use a deadband and minimum run and cooldown timers ([Omron](https://www.ia.omron.com/support/guide/53/explanation_of_terms.html); [ERSA](https://www.ersaelectronics.com/blog/temperature-regulator-relay-guide)). Tryento's controller is at or above published small-farm IoT builds; the industry gap is not the controller but (a) alerting, (b) substrate-core temperature, (c) CO₂ and (d) automated counting and weighing. Ranked additions (inference): waterproof DS18B20 probes in grow-out substrate with over-temperature alerts at about 40 °C; push alerts for sensor disagreement, stale data, out-of-band readings for N cycles and relay-on-without-effect; one NDIR CO₂ sensor per room; replacing DHT22s with SHT3x/SHT4x or BME280; and a load-cell workflow tied to the log. Averaging two DHT22s hides failures, so treat each independently, reject frozen or out-of-range values, fall back to the healthy sensor, alert, and default safely if both fail. Machine-vision dosing pays only at millions of neonates per day; at Tryento's scale weighing (eggie mass, love-cage fly mass, crate mass) gives most of the value. No published BSF alarm thresholds, SHT4x long-term high-RH data or complete open-source BSF monitoring stack were found.

### KPIs, records and traceability

The core KPI set is waste reduction, bioconversion rate (dry larval mass ÷ dry substrate), FCR, larval yield, survival, eggs or neonates per day, flies per love cage, emergence rate, residue moisture and drying yield (25 to 35%), all from disciplined weighing at every transfer and oven-dry sampling (105 °C, 24 h). Eawag weighs each love cage before and after connecting each dark cage (emergence rate; a 20-fly reference sample converts grams to count), weighs eggies before and after (egg mass), weighs each larvero and the larval harvest (residue = content − larvae), and dries 50-larva and residue samples; date codes and colour-coded strings label every container ([Dortmans et al. 2017](https://www.eawag.ch/fileadmin/Domain1/Abteilungen/sandec/publikationen/SWM/BSF/BSF_Biowaste_Processing_LR.pdf)). Other benchmarks: FCR 1.78 (aquaculture sludge) to 4.11 (fish salad) (snippet); industrial Portuguese restaurant-waste BCR 18.34% against 11.02%, FCR 5.48 against 9.09, survival 69.3% against 51.3% ([Oliveira et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13116064/)); 1 t of food waste yields about 30 kg of dried larvae plus 335 kg of compost (snippet; [Salomone et al. 2017](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0959652616308411)); Frontier assumes bioconversion of 25%, larvae-to-meal conversion of 45%, 59% protein meal and a 25% "refresh rate" (share retained for breeding). A rough grow-out productivity benchmark of about 0.5 kg fresh larvae/m² of crate area/day is my inference from Eawag and Salomone, not a published KPI. Tryento's log already maps onto the Eawag record set; the missing pieces are mandatory weights at each transfer plus dry-matter samples, and a lot-ID chain (colony → egg lot → tray → harvest lot → oven run → pack → dispatch) with environmental data joined by room/cage and time turns Pi logs into HACCP evidence (for example drying time and temperature as a critical control point). Batch-to-batch variation is listed among the sector's key barriers ([The Fish Site](https://thefishsite.com/articles/insect-meal-can-the-sector-still-deliver-on-its-2030-promise)). No public industrial values for labour hours per tonne, kWh/kg, eggs per day or breeding-unit mortality were found.

### Economics, staffing, safety and sustainability

BSF meal sells at roughly $2 to 3.5/kg in the EU/US ($1,652 to 2,100/t elsewhere) against soy at about $0.4 to 0.45/kg and fishmeal at about $1.7 to 1.8/kg (snippet; [ScienceDirect 2025 review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666833525000553)). In the UK model (commissioned by WWF and Tesco) BSF meal is about £1,800/t against soy modelled at £351/t and fishmeal at about £1,400/t; parity with soy on protein is about £430/t; a tolerable c.£500/t needs roughly a **£20 per tonne gate fee** at a large site; about half the cost base is fixed (capex, utilities, labour); processing 20% more waste at a medium site cuts price per tonne only 7.5% ([Frontier Economics 2023](https://www.frontier-economics.com/media/o12jxgjt/frontier-economics_delivering-insect-feed-at-scale_final-report_01-12-23.pdf)). Spent brewers' grains cost about £40/t wet in the UK because farmers compete for them. Small-scale BSF meal in Benin had a lowest estimated cost of $1.84/kg (snippet; [2021 study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666154321000557)); a claim of $0.60 to 0.70/kg DM across 20 farms in six countries appears only in a press release and is unverified. Capex per annual tonne of meal capacity is roughly £4,400 at the small UK site against about £2,400 at the large site (my derivation), so Tryento's lever is low capex per tonne, not scale economies. Ranked unit-economics levers (inference): free or gate-fee feedstock (market, agro-industrial or brewery residues); premium local niches (poultry and fish farmers, pet and bird food, broodstock) instead of commodity meal; frass as a second revenue line; cheap drying (microwave for small premium batches, gas or solar for bulk) with kWh or LPG per oven run logged. A soy-competitive price is unreachable at small scale; fishmeal-parity pricing is plausible only with gate fees or near-zero feedstock, and about $1.84/kg is a realistic benchmark for an efficient tropical smallholder. No Latin American cost or price data and no itemised opex split were found.

Staffing at small scale is labour-intensive but low-skill: 1 to 3 rearing workers at Eawag scale; Frontier models 10, 25 and 40 FTE. The underrated hazard is **occupational allergy**: a small cohort study of insect-farm employees found 60% self-reported upper-respiratory symptoms, 30.7% positive skin prick tests, 58.3% positive basophil activation and about two-thirds with bronchial hyperreactivity (snippet; species mix not confirmed as BSF) ([PubMed 37543086](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37543086/)); BSF carries the pan-allergens tropomyosin and arginine kinase, which cross-react with crustaceans, mites and cockroaches ([Sci Rep 2020](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-020-57863-5)). SOP items: FFP2/N95 masks for dusty tasks (sieving frass, handling dry larvae, emptying love cages of dead flies), gloves, long sleeves, local ventilation at sieves, baseline and annual symptom questionnaires, and PPE and training sign-offs logged per SOP step. No BSF-specific occupational exposure data or Latin American operator training curricula were found.

Life-cycle assessments find BSF beats soy or fishmeal only when larvae are reared on true low-opportunity-cost residues with low-carbon energy and credits for avoided waste treatment; hotspots are feed provision and energy for production and processing, and "soybean meal and fishmeal are less harmful than insect-derived protein in most cases" (snippet; [Resources, Conservation & Recycling 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S092134492300054X)). InnovaFeed reports 1.2 kg CO₂e/kg protein (2025, company claim; [Food Ingredients First](https://www.foodingredientsfirst.com/news/insect-protein-scale-carbon-cost.html)). For Tryento the LCA story is structurally good if feedstock is genuinely residual and energy use is dominated by fans and drying rather than heating; the largest controllable hotspots are likely drying energy and any commercial-feed nursery diet. To make credible claims later, log feed type and source per batch, kWh or LPG per oven run, and relay on-time multiplied by rated wattage. No LCA specific to tropical BSF farms and none comparing passive tropical rearing with climate-controlled rearing were found.

The table below sets each working practice recorded in [the lab specification](../LAB_SPEC.md) against the evidence, with a verdict and the change or test the evidence suggests.

| Practice or assumption | What the evidence says | Verdict and suggested action |
|---|---|---|
| Cage setpoint 30 °C ±1 | Fecundity, hatch and population growth peak at 28.6 to 30 °C; 35 to 37 °C is the decline; reproduction robust over about 24 to 35 °C | **Supported.** 28 °C is near-optimal and cheaper; avoid excursions above 34 to 35 °C at cage tops; ±1 °C is tighter than biology needs |
| Cage RH 72% ±3 | 60 to 85% supported; about 70% gave about 87% hatch and 91% emergence; below 50% costly | **Supported.** ±3% is at DHT22 error; keep traps dry and away from mist |

| Fast of 24 h (v1) or 48 h (v2) | 24 h clears most residues; 48 h adds little, costs weight and fat, may push larvae past 83 mg critical weight (inference) | Keep 24 h unless Tryento's own data justify 48 h; log pre and post weights |

## Conclusion

The evidence reframes BSF farming as a cohort-timing and heat-management discipline rather than a climate-precision one. The strongest effects on egg output in the literature are not light spectrum or a degree of temperature but cohort age (2 to 6.6 times), and the strongest risks to larval output are core temperatures the room sensor never sees. That makes Tryento's most valuable next steps inexpensive: weigh and count at every transfer, probe tray cores, load single-age cohorts, and calibrate the constants (individuals per kg, eggs per gram, dry-matter fractions) that every downstream KPI depends on. It also means the cage-1 photoperiod experiment, as designed, will likely be unreadable, because it changes several things in one cage against effects that the literature predicts are small.

The product side turns on two cheap process changes and one unresolved validation. A 40 second blanch, better packaging and a validated best-before date address the largest documented weaknesses in dried larvae (microbial load, lipid oxidation, welfare), at little cost, while the actual crunch-to-moisture relationship and the safety of the oven step alone remain untested anywhere in the literature and are therefore Tryento's own to establish. Strategically, an industry that has spent 2025 and 2026 discovering that scale and automation do not rescue commodity economics points toward what Tryento already is: a small, tropical, low-capex, controlled-breeding-unit farm selling premium whole larvae into local niches, with the caveat that nothing in this evidence base was measured in Venezuela.

## 9. Open research gaps in priority order

Priority reflects the effect of the unknown on a Tryento decision or KPI, the cost and speed of closing it, and the strength of the current evidence. P1 items either invalidate a current KPI or affect product safety and can be closed with in-house measurements in weeks.

| Priority | Gap | Why it matters | How to close |
|---|---|---|---|
| P1 | Real mass and count per kg of pupae and adults (50,000/kg) | Every per-fly and per-female KPI is 2.5 to 10 times off | Weigh 3×100 at each colony start |
| P1 | Eggs per gram and hatch for Tryento's strain | Tray seeding and g larvae per g eggs depend on it; literature 34,000 to 40,000 vs 77,000 | Count 3 × 10 mg aliquots; hatch 3 × 0.1 g aliquots |
| P1 | Tray-core temperature under Tryento's tropical room conditions | Overheating is the main grow-out risk; no tropical farm-scale data exist | Continuous DS18B20 probes, daily log, alarm at 38 to 40 °C |
| P1 | Microbial safety and oxidation of oven-only versus blanched-then-dried larvae | Product safety and welfare; oven step is unvalidated as a kill step | Side-by-side lots with TVC, Enterobacteriaceae, *Salmonella*, peroxide value |
| P1 | Feed per larva, tray density and final larval weight under the v2 plan | The plan implies about 31 mg wet feed per larva per day; results unknown | Count larvae per tray; measure DM% of ensilaje and starter; compare final weights |
| P1 | Dry-matter fractions of each feed and of larvae | Wet FCR is not comparable to published dry FCR | Oven-dry 105 °C for 24 h per feed batch and per lot of larvae |
| P1 | Shelf life of whole dried larvae at Venezuelan storage temperatures | The 180-day best-before has no scientific basis; no whole-larva tropical study exists | Peroxide value and smell at 0, 90, 180 days, accelerated 40 °C, packaging comparison |
| P1 | Venezuelan regulatory route for dried larvae as feed (INSAI) | Legality of sale for animal feed is unclear | Ask INSAI directly; document the answer and the registration category |
| P2 | Cage-1 light experiment redesign and baseline variance | Current design is confounded; effect sizes likely small | Uniformity trial, then blue-only manipulation with crossover and per-channel µmol |
| P2 | Fast duration: 24 versus 48 hours (weight loss, gut clearance, microbes) | v1 and v2 differ; no published weight-loss figure | Paired fasts with weights and plate counts |
| P2 | Nursery versus direct seeding, and starter type (afrecho, yogurt, BSG) | Affects survival and KPI interpretation; no head-to-head evidence | Split-batch trays with counts at day 5 and final weight |
| P2 | Yogurt and dairy as BSF feed | No yogurt study exists | Graded inclusion into afrecho or BSG; observe survival, pH, moisture |
| P2 | Crunch grade versus moisture and aw; golden-crunchy process | No published link; product differentiation depends on it | Moisture and aw on graded samples; trial blanch plus high-temperature finish |
| P2 | Cd, Pb and other contaminants in Tryento's larvae | BSG is the highest-risk substrate; regulatory limits | External test per substrate source |
| P2 | Colony origin, genetics and inbreeding (Ne, F-number) | Narrow stocks collapse; Venezuelan strains unstudied | Record origin and generation; pooled breeder sampling; consider local wild augmentation |
| P2 | Cohort discipline effect in Tryento's cages | Literature effect of 2 to 6.6 times (preprint) may exceed any light effect | Compare same-age single-batch loads against topped-up cages |
| P2 | Temperature and RH bands and sensor quality in cages | ±1 °C / ±3% RH is below sensor accuracy | Reference hygrometer check; log pair difference; consider hysteresis and SHT/BME sensors |
| P2 | Adult mating and egg output under Tryento's light at cage level | Intensity requirements conflict in the literature | Measure µmol and spectrum; correlate lagged mating with trap eggs |
| P3 | Parasitoid, mite and pathogen incidence in Venezuela | Dirhinus killed 52 to 76% of pupae elsewhere; no Latin American records | Screen pupation and emergence rooms; sample pupae |
| P3 | Dead-adult removal, cage shape and mesh effects | No controlled studies | Small factorial tests once baseline is stable |
| P3 | Prepupal cold storage to smooth egg supply | Only one 2026 study (14 °C, 60 days, 75.3% eclosion) | Hold surplus prepupae at 14 to 15 °C and track emergence |
| P3 | Gas thresholds (CO₂, NH₃) and ventilation rates | No peer-reviewed thresholds | CO₂ sensor per room; correlate with core temperature and die-offs |
| P3 | Larva versus prepupa composition, chitin and calcium for product spec | Not captured | External proximate and mineral analysis at two harvest stages |
| P3 | Energy per kg for oven versus microwave/roasting; drying cost | Largest controllable cost and LCA hotspot | Meter kWh or weigh LPG per run |
| P3 | Frass: compost maturity, market and Venezuelan rules | Second revenue line; no Venezuelan rules found | Composting trials; check INSAI/fertiliser rules |
| P3 | Venezuelan and Latin American prices, costs and demand | No local price data | Price log per channel; talk to UCV/MIZA, FENAVI |
| P3 | Occupational allergy prevalence and controls | Staff exposure; no BSF-specific data | Baseline symptom questionnaire; PPE log |
| P3 | Welfare-oriented killing: instant methods at small scale | Precautionary principle; blanching also a safety gain | Test blanch step; consider grinder-based alternatives only after settings are verified |
| P3 | Neonate mass, dead-adult and dry adult mass, larva-to-prepupa gut mass loss | No peer-reviewed values | Weigh at Tryento; publish internally |

## 10. How to maintain this document

This is a living document; edit it whenever the lab, its configuration or the research base changes. Keep it consistent with [the lab specification](../LAB_SPEC.md), which records what Tryento actually does, while this document records what is known and what has been measured.

1. When Tryento measures a value in Section 8, fill the "Tryento value" cell with the value, unit, date, and the colony, tray or lot, and add a one-line interpretation in the relevant narrative section ("Implication for Tryento") if it confirms or contradicts the literature. Do not delete the literature value. If a measurement invalidates a lab assumption, add a flag in the lab specification too.
2. When a new paper, manual or dataset is found, add its finding to the matching section with conditions, units and a link; mark preprints, abstract-level reads and vendor claims explicitly; if it conflicts with an existing figure, state both and say which is preferred and why. Remove a gap from Section 9 only when it is closed by evidence or measurement.
3. When the lab changes (setpoints, lighting, feeding protocol, kill or drying step, packaging), update Section 7 and the relevant narrative section, and note the reason. Research changes and configuration changes should each add a changelog line.
4. Re-rank Section 9 after each change; a gap becomes P1 when it blocks a decision or invalidates a KPI.
5. Bump the version: increase the minor number for new content, the patch number for corrections; update the date at the top and add a row to Section 11. Do not rewrite history in the changelog.
6. Keep paragraphs narrative with inline links; keep tables for benchmarks and comparisons. If the file grows unwieldy, split by section but keep this document as the index.
